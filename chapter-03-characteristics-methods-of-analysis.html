Characteristics of turbulent flow and methods of analysis: telling turbulence from other irregular motion, and what can be done once a flow qualifies

Three irregular motions are put in front of a designer: the air in a round jet, the surface of the sea under a long swell, and the white trail that an aircraft leaves at cruise altitude. All three fluctuate irregularly, and a record of any of them looks random. Averaging, closure models and scaling laws are worth building only for the first. The lecture's list of attributes is meant to separate them, but an attribute is useful only if it can be measured. This chapter turns each attribute into a number, evaluates the numbers for a jet of air and for the motions the lecture excludes, and then examines the methods the lecture offers for a flow that qualifies: a statistical description with its closure problem, an eddy-viscosity model, dimensional analysis, and two invariance principles. Two results frame the chapter. The kinetic energy of the energy-containing eddies of the jet equals the energy that they dissipate in 1.5 to 3.2 of their own turnover times, whereas a swell of 100 m wavelength dissipates its energy on an e-folding time of about \(8\times10^{6}\) wave periods. And a spectrum plotted in the right dimensionless variables collapses for flows whose Taylor-microscale Reynolds numbers run from 23 to 3180, a factor of 140, but a 20 % error in the dissipation rate moves a point by only 1/700 of the height of the plot.

Grounded in two recordings of the NPTEL course Essentials of Turbulence (Prof. Abhilash J. Chandy, IIT Bombay): Lecture 6 and Lecture 7. Lecture material: the occurrence of turbulence in nature and engineering and the statement that laminar flow is the exception; the attributes irregularity, diffusivity, large Reynolds numbers, three-dimensional vorticity fluctuations and dissipation; the further attributes of continuum and of turbulence as a property of flows rather than of fluids; the methods of analysis (no general approach, the closure problem, ad hoc assumptions, the eddy-viscosity hypothesis, dimensional analysis, asymptotic invariance, local invariance) and the compiled spectra that illustrate local invariance. Research extensions: Kärcher (2018) on the persistence and spreading of contrails; Chapman and Tobak (1985) on the eddy-viscosity and mixing-length ideas; Saddoughi (1993) for the compilation of Kolmogorov-scaled spectra and the extent of the inertial range; the direct numerical simulations of Lee and Moser (2015) for a Reynolds-number comparison of mean velocity profiles; the U.S. Standard Atmosphere (1976) for the properties of air; McComb and co-authors (2015) for the dissipation constant. The objects of the chapter and the running case

The chapter works with one formal model and one flow. The model is the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations for the full velocity \(\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)\) and the pressure \(p\) of a fluid of constant density \(\rho\) and constant kinematic viscosity \(\nu\) (units \(\mathrm{m^2/s}\)):

\[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf U=0,\qquad \frac{\partial\mathbf U}{\partial t}+(\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U=-\frac1\rho\nabla p+\nu\nabla^{2}\mathbf U. \tag{3.1} \]

The lecture writes the same momentum equation with \(\mathbf U_t\) for the time derivative and \(P\) for the pressure, with the factor \(1/\rho\) written in the equation; this book writes \(\partial\mathbf U/\partial t\) and \(p/\rho\), as chapters 1 and 6 do. Chapter 6 derives (3.1) and states its approximations. Upper-case \(\mathbf U\) is the full velocity, the rate-of-strain tensor is \(S_{ij}=\tfrac12(\partial U_i/\partial x_j+\partial U_j/\partial x_i)\) and the vorticity is \(\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf U\), the local rate of rotation of the fluid in units of \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) (twice the local angular velocity). Repeated indices are summed. The angle brackets \(\langle\cdot\rangle\) are the expectation over repeated realizations (chapter 7), and \(\langle\cdot\rangle_V\) is the average over a periodic box. The symbol \(\sim\) means "of the same order of magnitude"; it fixes a power law and not its coefficient.

The running case is the round air jet of chapters 6–8 and 10. At the nozzle the exit velocity is \(\mathcal U=56\ \mathrm{m/s}\) and the diameter is \(\mathcal L=0.025\ \mathrm m\), as in chapter 6, so the nozzle Reynolds number is \(\mathrm{Re}=\mathcal U\mathcal L/\nu=9.6\times10^{4}\). At a station far downstream the root-mean-square velocity fluctuation is \(u_0=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\) and the energy-containing eddies are \(\ell_0=0.1\ \mathrm m\) across, which give \(\mathrm{Re}_0=u_0\ell_0/\nu=1.37\times10^{4}\) and a turnover time \(\ell_0/u_0=0.05\ \mathrm s\). These values were chosen in chapter 6; they are not measurements of a particular jet. The air properties are the sea-level values of the U.S. Standard Atmosphere, 1976 (Table 10, p. 20): kinematic viscosity \(\nu=1.4607\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), speed of sound \(c=340.294\ \mathrm{m/s}\), mean free path \(\lambda_{\mathrm m}=6.6328\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm m\) and mean speed of an air molecule \(\bar V=458.94\ \mathrm{m/s}\). Chapter 5 gives the Kolmogorov scales from the inviscid estimate of the dissipation rate, \(\varepsilon=C_\varepsilon u_0^3/\ell_0\) with \(C_\varepsilon=1\) for the order-of-magnitude arguments, which for the jet gives \(\varepsilon=80\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\) and \(\eta=(\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}=79\ \mu\mathrm m\). Chapter 1 introduces the jet, and chapter 10 states the hypotheses on which the later sections lean.

Three further words are used in a fixed sense. An attribute is a property that the lecture lists as characteristic of turbulence. A test is a measurable quantity with a stated value that separates a flow that has the attribute from one that has not. A motion that the lecture excludes is an irregular motion that the lecture says is not turbulent: a contrail, a cyclone, a random wave field, acoustic noise. The lecture presents its list as a description, not as a definition; the chapter titled "Descriptions, definitions, and a short history of turbulence" treats the lecture's definition and its six-item list of attributes, and this chapter does not repeat that discussion. Symbols that differ from the slides: the slides write the Knudsen number as \(\mathrm{Kn}=\lambda/\ell\); this book writes \(\mathrm{Kn}=\lambda_{\mathrm m}/\ell_g\) with \(\ell_g\) the smallest length of the flow (chapter 6). The slides write the eddy viscosity as \(\mu_t\), a dynamic viscosity; this book writes the kinematic quantity \(\nu_T\) (chapter 9).

Laminar flow is the exception, and large Reynolds numbers are the rule in the lecture's examples L6 2:36

The lecture opens with a list of places where flows are turbulent. In nature: the boundary layer of the atmosphere, the jet streams of the upper troposphere, cumulus clouds, ocean currents below the surface, the Gulf Stream, the photosphere of the Sun and of similar stars, interstellar gas clouds, and the solar wind and its wake behind the Earth. In engineering: boundary layers on aircraft wings, combustion (fuel and oxidizer must mix quickly), natural-gas and oil pipelines (the wall stress is large and the pipe must be designed for it), the mixing of fluids and the acceleration of chemical reactions, rivers and canals, and the wakes of ships, cars, submarines and aircraft. Its bold sentence is that laminar flow is the exception and not the rule, because one must have small dimensions or high viscosity to meet laminar flow; the example is lubricating oil in a bearing, whose clearance is of the order of micrometres to a millimetre.

The statement can be put as one number. The Reynolds number of a flow of speed \(U\), length \(L\) and kinematic viscosity \(\nu\) is \(\mathrm{Re}=UL/\nu\); the chapter titled "Descriptions, definitions, and a short history of turbulence" derives it as a ratio of inertial to viscous terms. The jet has \(\mathrm{Re}=9.6\times10^{4}\). A bearing film of thickness \(50\ \mu\mathrm m\) in which oil of kinematic viscosity \(1.0\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) moves at \(5\ \mathrm{m/s}\) has \(\mathrm{Re}=2.5\); a river of depth \(1\ \mathrm m\) moving at \(1\ \mathrm{m/s}\) in water of kinematic viscosity \(1.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) has \(\mathrm{Re}=10^{6}\). The inputs of the last two are assumed for this illustration; neither is a measurement. They show the lecture's rule in its own terms: the Reynolds number is small only when \(L\) is small or \(\nu\) is large, and it is large by many orders of magnitude for a river of ordinary size. A large Reynolds number does not by itself make a flow turbulent. The value at which a given flow becomes turbulent depends on the flow and on the disturbances present, which chapter 4 (section "Turbulence begins as an instability of a laminar flow") treats, and a Reynolds number below that value does not exclude turbulence that is supplied from upstream. The lecture's sentence that laminar flow is "the exception" also states how often the situations of interest occur, and that frequency is a judgment of the lecturer that no calculation here tests; the calculation checks only that each listed example has a large Reynolds number.

Irregularity is shared by the motions the lecture excludes L6 9:40

The first attribute is irregularity. The slide's caption, over a photograph of smoke, reads: one characteristic of turbulent flows is their irregularity or randomness; a full deterministic approach is very difficult; turbulent flows are usually described statistically; turbulent flows are always chaotic, but not all chaotic flows are turbulent. The lecture adds an argument. The Navier–Stokes equations are deterministic. If the flow were random, a deterministic description would contradict it, and one would need stochastic differential equations. The lecture therefore corrects the statement that it attributes to Tennekes and Lumley, that turbulent flows are random, to "seemingly random": the prevailing view when their book was written (1972) was that the flow is random, and the modern view is that it is chaotic and deterministic.

Two points need to be separated. The first is the logical step. A statistical description of a deterministic system does not require stochastic equations. What (3.1) determines is the evolution of one particular initial and boundary state. In a real flow those data are never known exactly, so the same experiment repeated gives different records with reproducible statistics, and the statistics of the ensemble of records obey equations that are themselves deterministic: chapter 9 derives them (the Reynolds equations) and counts what they leave undetermined. Chapter 7 (section "Deterministic equations, incomplete conditions and sensitivity") develops this with the Lorenz equations as a three-variable example. In that sense "random" in the old descriptions means "known only statistically because the data are incompletely known", and the lecture's "seemingly random" is a fair statement of it. The second point is what irregularity can show. Irregularity is shared by every motion on the list of excluded motions: random surface waves, acoustic noise and the smoke trail are irregular, and the slide itself says that not all chaotic flows are turbulent. Irregularity is therefore necessary and not sufficient, and a test that separates turbulence from these motions must rest on the other attributes. A measurable form of irregularity, an autocorrelation that falls to zero in a finite time and a continuous spectrum, is defined in chapter 8; it separates an irregular record from a periodic or quasi-periodic one and does not separate turbulence from noise.

Diffusivity: how fast a patch of fluid spreads L6 13:36

The slide states that if a flow pattern looks random but does not exhibit spreading of velocity fluctuations through the surrounding fluid, it is surely not turbulent, and that the diffusivity of turbulence is the single most important feature as far as applications are concerned: it prevents boundary-layer separation on an airfoil at large angles of attack, increases heat transfer rates in machinery, is the source of the resistance of flow in pipelines, and increases momentum transfer between winds and ocean currents. The photograph on the slide is a spillway with the caption that the diffusivity of turbulence causes rapid mixing and increased rates of momentum, heat and mass transfer, and that a flow that is chaotic but not diffusive is not turbulent.

A test needs a number. Let a patch of a passive scalar, such as dye or smoke, be released at a point, and let \(\sigma^2(t)\) be the variance of one coordinate of the position of the scalar about the centre of the patch (in an isotropic motion each coordinate has the same variance). The effective diffusivity is half the rate of growth of that variance,

\[ K\equiv\tfrac12\,\frac{d\sigma^{2}}{dt}. \tag{3.2} \]

For molecular diffusion alone \(K\) is the molecular diffusivity \(\Gamma\) of the scalar, and \(\sigma^2=2\Gamma t\). The test is the ratio \(K/\Gamma\): a motion passes if the ratio is large and if \(K\) belongs to the flow, so that it changes from flow to flow and from place to place in a flow. Chapter 4 derives the turbulent estimate \(K\sim u_0\ell_0\), the Péclet number \(\mathrm{Pe}=u_0\ell_0/\Gamma\) and the conditions under which \(K\) can be used as a diffusivity (section "When an eddy diffusivity is a diffusivity"). For the jet, with \(\Gamma=\nu/0.73=2.0\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) (air, with the Prandtl number 0.73 used in chapter 5), a dye patch the size of an energy-containing eddy needs \(\ell_0^2/\Gamma=500\ \mathrm s\) to spread across itself by molecular diffusion and \(\ell_0/u_0=0.05\ \mathrm s\) by the motion of the eddy. The ratio is \(\mathrm{Pe}=1.0\times10^{4}\), and \(K/\Gamma\) is of that order.

The lecture compares two trails. Smoke from an industrial chimney, it says, spreads into the atmosphere within a few metres of the stack when the wind is turbulent; if the flow were laminar the black smoke would extend for hundreds of metres. The statement can be checked in order of magnitude for a wind of \(5\ \mathrm{m/s}\) (an assumed value; the lecture gives none). A parcel reaches 500 m downwind after \(100\ \mathrm s\). In a laminar flow the plume widens only by molecular diffusion, by a distance of order \((2\Gamma t)^{1/2}=(2\times2.0\times10^{-5}\times100)^{1/2}=0.063\ \mathrm m\), about 6 cm, on each side of the stack diameter. A laminar plume would therefore keep the width of the stack, give or take a few centimetres, for kilometres, which is the lecture's picture. The other trail is the condensation trail of an aircraft. The lecture says that near the exhaust the flow is probably turbulent, but that a trail that extends for miles cannot be turbulent there, because turbulent diffusion would have mixed it with the atmosphere.

The inference uses the wrong variable, and the source that was opened does not support it. Kärcher (2018) distinguishes short-lived contrails, which form in air that is subsaturated with respect to ice and last 0.1 to 10 min with widths of 10 to 100 m, from persistent contrails, which occur in ice-supersaturated air and last 10 min to 10 h with widths of 0.1 to 10 km (Table 1, p. 2). The review states that ice supersaturation "is required for contrails to be long-lived" (p. 2), that long-lived contrails "spread out due to wind shear that is common at cruise altitudes" (p. 2), and that ice crystals in the wake keep growing by taking up entrained, ice-supersaturated ambient vapour (p. 4). It also states that turbulent mixing cools the exhaust plume at formation (p. 3) and that flow instability triggered by turbulence eventually collapses the organized wake and mixes it with ambient air (p. 4).

Three consequences follow. First, the length of a trail is the distance the aircraft flew while the ice persisted, and persistence is set by the humidity of the air, not by whether the air is turbulent. Second, the growth of the width is the quantity that bears on diffusion, not the width itself. A contrail does not start as a point: Table 1 gives 10 to 100 m as the width, and 100 m as the depth, of a short-lived contrail 0.1 to 10 min old, and the text describes the wake as a vortex pair that descends about 100 m below the flight level (pp. 3–4). The table does not say how much of that width is the size of the wake and how much has been added by spreading during the first 10 min. A width of the order of 100 m is therefore the starting condition of the long-lived stage, to the precision of the table, and only the growth beyond it can be assigned to diffusion. The molecular spread after 10 h, the longest duration in the table, is, with the sea-level \(\Gamma\), \((2\times2.0\times10^{-5}\times3.6\times10^{4})^{1/2}=1.2\ \mathrm m\), or 3.8 m for a \(\Gamma\) ten times larger, an assumed bound for the lower density at cruise altitude. The lower end of the persistent-contrail width range, 100 m, is therefore no evidence of spreading beyond what short-lived contrails already show. The upper end, 10 km, exceeds the initial width by about 10 km, which is \(8\times10^{3}\) times 1.2 m (\(2.6\times10^{3}\) times 3.8 m). Comparing with the spread at the longest duration is conservative, because the molecular spread is largest then.

Third, the source attributes the spreading of long-lived contrails to wind shear, a mean-flow effect, so that the table does not separate shear dispersion from turbulent diffusion, and the contrail is not by itself evidence for or against the attribute. The lecture's conclusion that the trail is "not turbulent" is therefore not supported by the source opened, and the trail's length is not a test of diffusion. Exercise 6 asks for a measurement that would separate the two mechanisms.

The lecture's list of consequences of diffusivity (the delay of separation, the heat transfer, the pipe resistance, the momentum exchange between wind and ocean) is stated without argument and is not examined here; chapter 4 estimates the heat transfer in a room, and the claim about separation at large angles of attack is the lecture's.

Large Reynolds numbers: a wide range of sizes between inertia and viscosity L6 18:55

The slide states that turbulence often originates as an instability of laminar flows if the Reynolds number becomes too large, that the interaction between viscous and inertial terms is very complex, that randomness and nonlinearity combine to make the equations of turbulence nearly intractable, and that nonlinear concepts and mathematical tools have to be developed along the way because one cannot rely on the equations alone. The lecture writes (3.1), marks the terms of the acceleration as inertia and the viscous term as viscous, and writes \(\mathrm{Re}=\)inertia/viscous. The derivation of the Reynolds number as the ratio of the two terms is in the chapter titled "Descriptions, definitions, and a short history of turbulence", and the instability that starts turbulence is in chapter 4; they are not repeated here.

What a test of "large" needs is the Reynolds number of a particular set of motions, because one flow carries several. The jet has three. The nozzle Reynolds number \(9.6\times10^{4}\) is a property of the flow as a whole. The Reynolds number of the energy-containing eddies, \(\mathrm{Re}_0=1.37\times10^{4}\), is the one that decides how far the scales extend. The Reynolds number of the smallest eddies is exactly one: chapter 5 shows that with the Kolmogorov scales \(\mathrm{Re}_\eta=\eta u_\eta/\nu=1\) is an identity. Between the largest and the smallest motions the ratio of sizes is, with \(C_\varepsilon=1\),

\[ \frac{\ell_0}{\eta}=\mathrm{Re}_0^{3/4}=1.27\times10^{3}\qquad(\text{jet}), \tag{3.3} \]

which is the result of chapter 5 (section "The scale ratios widen as the Reynolds number increases") and agrees with \(0.1\ \mathrm m/79\ \mu\mathrm m\). "Large Reynolds number" therefore means a wide band of sizes, \(\tfrac34\log_{10}\mathrm{Re}_0=3.1\) decades for the jet, over which inertia dominates the dynamics at the large end and viscosity at the small end. The viscous term cannot be dropped at large Reynolds number; it multiplies the highest derivative of (3.1), and chapter 5 (section "Inertia and viscosity act at different lengths") shows that setting it to zero changes the character of the problem.

The lecture's two judgments about the equations can be graded. That the equations are "nearly intractable" because of the nonlinear term is a statement about exact solutions. The nonlinear term \((\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U\) is quadratic in the unknown, so a sum of two solutions is not a solution, and general solutions cannot be assembled from particular ones. The statement is not a statement about computation, which the chapter titled "Direct numerical simulation: pseudo-spectral methods and cost" treats. What the nonlinear term does can be stated exactly and is shown by the program of the executable section: in a periodic box it moves kinetic energy between wavenumbers and adds none, because the convective and pressure terms contribute nothing to the rate of change of the total energy (the section on dissipation derives this). The "complexity of the interaction" is thus a statement about the redistribution among scales, and the one process that removes energy is viscous. That randomness "combines" with nonlinearity is the lecture's phrase for the two difficulties of this section and the previous one, and the chapter treats them separately.

Three-dimensional vorticity fluctuations: what stretching does to size L6 23:41

The slide states that turbulent flows are rotational (their vorticity is non-zero) and that mechanisms such as the stretching of three-dimensional vortices play a key role. The lecture writes the vorticity transport equation, which in the book's notation is chapter 6's (6.18),

\[ \frac{D\boldsymbol\omega}{Dt}=(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U+\nu\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol\omega, \tag{3.4} \]

and circles the first term on the right as vortex stretching. Chapter 6 (section "The vorticity equation and vortex stretching") derives (3.4) from (3.1) and states what it needs: constant density, so that the pressure term has no curl; constant viscosity; an inertial frame. It also shows that the stretching term vanishes identically in a two-dimensional flow, because with \(\mathbf U=(U_1(x_1,x_2,t),U_2(x_1,x_2,t),0)\) the vorticity has the single component \(\omega_3\) and \((\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U=\omega_3\,\partial\mathbf U/\partial x_3=0\), and that the mean enstrophy of an unforced two-dimensional flow then cannot increase. The lecture's three statements in this part, that stretching is absent in two dimensions, that cyclones in the atmosphere are not turbulence because they are substantially two-dimensional, and that random waves on the sea are not turbulent because they are irrotational, are respectively a result, a stipulation and a result with a qualification. The stipulation follows the lecture's definition of turbulence as three-dimensional; the literature reviewed in chapter 6 (Boffetta and Ecke, 2012) treats forced two-dimensional turbulence as a regime in its own right, with an inverse cascade of energy, so the sentence says what the lecture means by the word and not that two-dimensional flows are regular. The statement on waves is examined in the next section.

The lecture also says that vortex stretching is "primarily responsible for the breakup of vortices". Equation (3.4) does not say that. For \(\nu=0\), chapter 6 shows that the vorticity of a particle changes like the length of a material line that lies along it: stretching increases the magnitude of the vorticity, and, because the fluid is incompressible, a stretched vortex tube becomes thinner. What reaches small scales is the cross-section of the vortex. A solution of (3.1) shows the mechanism and its limit. Let the velocity be the sum of a uniform axisymmetric strain \(U_r=-ar/2\), \(U_z=az\) (with \(r\) the distance from the axis and \(a\gt0\) a constant strain rate, in \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\)) and a swirl that carries vorticity \(\omega(r)\) along \(z\). The strain stretches the axial vorticity and carries it towards the axis, while viscosity spreads it. The steady balance is

\[ -\tfrac12 a r\,\omega'=a\,\omega+\nu\Bigl(\omega''+\frac{\omega'}{r}\Bigr),\qquad \omega(r)=\omega_c\,e^{-r^{2}/r_c^{2}},\quad r_c=\Bigl(\frac{4\nu}{a}\Bigr)^{1/2}. \tag{3.5} \]

Direct substitution confirms it. With \(\omega'=-(ar/2\nu)\omega\) and \(\omega''=[-a/(2\nu)+(ar/2\nu)^2]\omega\), the left side is \((a^2r^2/4\nu)\,\omega\), and the right side is \(a\omega+\nu[-a/(2\nu)+a^2r^2/(4\nu^2)-a/(2\nu)]\omega=(a^2r^2/4\nu)\,\omega\). The program of the executable section verifies the residual numerically. Stretching thins the vortex until viscous diffusion balances it at \(r_c\), and the thinner the stronger the strain. If the strain rate is the largest one a flow supplies, of order \(1/\tau_\eta=(\varepsilon/\nu)^{1/2}\), then \(r_c=2\nu^{3/4}\varepsilon^{-1/4}=2\eta\): stretched vortices of the jet have a core radius of \(158\ \mu\mathrm m\), twice the Kolmogorov length. This ties the lecture's two attributes, three-dimensional vorticity and viscous dissipation, to the length scale of chapter 5. The solution is steady, axisymmetric and driven by an imposed strain; in a turbulent flow the strain is produced by the other eddies and fluctuates, so the result gives the size at which a balance of stretching and diffusion is possible and not the size of every vortex. A vortex of this form is called a Burgers vortex in the literature; the attribution was not checked against Burgers's paper, which was not obtained.

Dissipation: the one process that removes energy L7 0:43

The fifth attribute is dissipation. The first slide states that random motions with insignificant viscous losses, such as gravity waves in planetary atmospheres and random sound waves (acoustic noise), are not turbulent, and that waves are essentially non-dissipative, though often dispersive, while turbulence is essentially dissipative. The second, over a photograph of a river, states that turbulent flows are dissipative, that kinetic energy is converted into heat by viscous shear stresses, and that turbulent flows die out quickly when no energy is supplied. The lecture adds that viscous stresses do deformation work that raises the internal energy of the fluid at the expense of kinetic energy.

The statement has an exact form. Multiply the momentum equation of (3.1) by \(U_i\) and average over a periodic box. The convective term gives \(\langle U_iU_j\partial U_i/\partial x_j\rangle_V=\langle U_j\,\partial(\tfrac12U_iU_i)/\partial x_j\rangle_V=-\tfrac12\langle U_iU_i\,\partial U_j/\partial x_j\rangle_V=0\), by integration by parts over a period and incompressibility. The pressure term gives \(-\rho^{-1}\langle U_i\,\partial p/\partial x_i\rangle_V=\rho^{-1}\langle p\,\partial U_i/\partial x_i\rangle_V=0\) in the same way. The viscous term gives \(\nu\langle U_i\nabla^2U_i\rangle_V=-\nu\langle(\partial U_i/\partial x_j)(\partial U_i/\partial x_j)\rangle_V\). With \(\mathcal K\equiv\tfrac12\langle U_iU_i\rangle_V\), the kinetic energy per unit mass of the box,

\[ \frac{d\mathcal K}{dt}=-\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon=\nu\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial U_i}{\partial x_j}\frac{\partial U_i}{\partial x_j}\Bigr\rangle_V=2\nu\langle S_{ij}S_{ij}\rangle_V=\nu\langle\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\boldsymbol\omega\rangle_V. \tag{3.6} \]

The second equality uses \(\langle(\partial_jU_i)(\partial_iU_j)\rangle_V=\langle(\partial_iU_i)^2\rangle_V=0\), and the third uses that \(\langle|\boldsymbol\omega|^2\rangle_V\) equals \(\langle(\partial_jU_i)(\partial_jU_i)\rangle_V-\langle(\partial_jU_i)(\partial_iU_j)\rangle_V\). Chapter 5 defines the dissipation rate \(\varepsilon=2\nu\langle s_{ij}s_{ij}\rangle\) of the fluctuation, (5.13); (3.6) is the same expression for the full velocity of a periodic box. Without forcing, the box loses energy at the rate \(\varepsilon\geq0\), and every other term of the momentum equation only redistributes it. This is the exact content of "turbulence needs a continuous supply of energy": a supply must be added to the right side of (3.6) to keep \(\mathcal K\) constant. The program of the executable section evaluates each term for a random solenoidal field with \(\nu=0.01\): the convective and pressure contributions to \(d\mathcal K/dt\) are zero to round-off, the viscous contribution is \(-9.26\times10^{-2}\), and the three forms of \(\varepsilon\) agree. Convection moves the energy, in that field out of the shell \(|\boldsymbol\kappa|\approx4\) into the shells \(|\boldsymbol\kappa|\approx1\) to 3, with a total of zero.

Viscous dissipation is positive in every flow with a non-zero rate of strain, laminar or turbulent, so \(\varepsilon\gt0\) cannot be the test; Exercise 4 gives a laminar example. The discriminating quantity is how fast the energy is lost compared with the motion's own time scale, and how that depends on \(\nu\). For turbulence the inviscid estimate of chapter 5, (5.16), \(\varepsilon=C_\varepsilon u_0^3/\ell_0\), contains no viscosity. With the kinetic energy per unit mass \(k=\tfrac32u_0^2\) (an isotropic fluctuation of component \(u_0\)), the time \(k/\varepsilon\) is \(\tfrac32\,\ell_0/u_0\) for \(C_\varepsilon=1\), 1.5 turnover times, and 0.160 s or 3.2 turnover times with \(C_\varepsilon=0.468\), the asymptotic value that McComb and co-authors (2015, manuscript p. 1) measured in simulations of forced isotropic turbulence (the value belongs to their definitions of \(u\) and \(\ell\) and is not a constant of the jet). The energy of the energy-containing eddies of the running jet is therefore gone in a few turnover times, whatever the viscosity.

For a linear wave the loss time depends on the viscosity. Take a deep-water surface wave of wavenumber \(\kappa\) and angular frequency \(\omega_w=(g\kappa)^{1/2}\), whose velocity is the gradient of the potential \(\phi=Ae^{\kappa z}\cos(\kappa x-\omega_wt)\) (\(\phi\) is local to this section; elsewhere the book uses it for a passive scalar) (\(z\le0\) below the surface). Its rate of strain follows by differentiation, \(S_{xx}=-S_{zz}=-\kappa^2Ae^{\kappa z}\cos\theta\) and \(S_{xz}=-\kappa^2Ae^{\kappa z}\sin\theta\) with \(\theta=\kappa x-\omega_wt\), so that \(S_{ij}S_{ij}=2\kappa^4A^2e^{2\kappa z}\) at every \(x\). The dissipation integrated over depth per unit area is \(\rho\int_{-\infty}^{0}2\nu S_{ij}S_{ij}\,dz=2\rho\nu\kappa^3A^2\), and the mechanical energy per unit area (kinetic plus potential, equal on average) is \(E=\tfrac12\rho\kappa A^2\) (\(E\) is local to this section and is not the spectrum \(E(\kappa)\) of chapter 8). The energy therefore decays as

\[ \frac{dE}{dt}=-4\nu\kappa^{2}E,\qquad \tau_E=\frac1{4\nu\kappa^{2}}. \tag{3.7} \]

For the interior of an irrotational flow this is the viscous loss rate; the boundary layer needed to satisfy the stress condition at the free surface is not included, so (3.7) is an estimate of the loss rate, not a complete solution. A classical treatment of the viscous damping of surface waves exists (Lamb); it was not obtained, and (3.7) is not checked against it. For a swell of wavelength 100 m (\(\kappa=0.0628\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\)) in water of kinematic viscosity \(1.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) (assumed), the period is \(2\pi/\omega_w=8.0\ \mathrm s\) and \(\tau_E=6.3\times10^{7}\ \mathrm s\), about two years, or \(7.9\times10^{6}\) periods. Figure 3.1 puts the two loss times on one axis. The ratio of the swell to the jet is between \(2.5\times10^{6}\) and \(5.3\times10^{6}\). Real swell loses energy by other mechanisms (breaking and interaction with the wind) that (3.7) does not describe; the number says that the viscous loss is not the one that matters for the wave, which is the lecture's statement of "insignificant viscous losses". The slide's own examples, atmospheric gravity waves and sound, are not computed here; the lecture's statement is taken as stated for them. The viscous term in the momentum equation of an irrotational flow is itself zero, because \(\nabla^2\mathbf U=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U)-\nabla\times\boldsymbol\omega=0\) when \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U=0\) and \(\boldsymbol\omega=0\), so the potential wave solves (3.1) in the interior; the dissipation (3.7) is nevertheless not zero, and is the work of the viscous stresses at the boundary.

\[ \mathrm{Kn}_\eta=\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm m}}{\eta}\ll1. \tag{3.8} \]

\[ \mathrm{Kn}_\eta=\frac{\nu}{f\bar V\ell_0}\,\mathrm{Re}_0^{3/4}=\frac{u_0}{f\bar V}\,\mathrm{Re}_0^{-1/4}=\frac{c}{f\bar V}\,\mathrm{Ma}\,\mathrm{Re}_0^{-1/4}=1.545\,\mathrm{Ma}\,\mathrm{Re}_0^{-1/4}, \tag{3.9} \]

A model need not be complete: the eddy viscosity as the lecture's case in point L7 12:26

The slide states that one must be willing to use simple physical concepts based on experience to bridge the gap between the equations and actual flows; that one needs spirited inventors just as badly as dedicated analysts (according to Tennekes and Lumley, as quoted in the lecture); and that the model need not be "complete" but can still be useful. The case in point is the eddy-viscosity hypothesis, which the lecture writes beside Newton's law of viscosity: \(\tau=\mu\,du/dy\) for the molecular stress and \(\tau_t=\mu_t\,\overline{du/dy}\) for the turbulent stress, with an overbar on the mean strain. In this book's notation, for a simple shear \(\langle U_1\rangle(y)\),

\[ -\langle u_1u_2\rangle=\nu_T\,\frac{d\langle U_1\rangle}{dy}, \tag{3.10} \]

the form that chapter 9 develops and tests as (9.29). The history of the hypothesis (Boussinesq 1877, Schmitt's reading of it, and the missing isotropic term) is in the chapter titled "Descriptions, definitions, and a short history of turbulence"; the lecture's remark about the mixing length, a fluid particle that keeps its momentum over a distance before dispersing it as a molecule does over its mean free path, is there too. What this chapter adds is a measurement of how good the analogy can be.

The molecular law rests on a separation of scales. The viscosity \(\nu=f\bar V\lambda_{\mathrm m}\) is a momentum flux carried by molecules that travel a distance \(\lambda_{\mathrm m}\) between collisions, and the law \(\tau=\mu\,du/dy\) holds when the mean velocity changes little over that distance, that is when \(\mathrm{Kn}=\lambda_{\mathrm m}/\ell_g\) is small. For the 25 mm nozzle of the running case \(\lambda_{\mathrm m}/\mathcal L=2.7\times10^{-6}\). The turbulent counterpart takes the eddies as the carriers, with \(\nu_T\sim u_0\ell_0\) as in chapter 5, and so needs \(\ell_0\) to be small compared with the length over which the mean velocity changes. Chapter 5 states the opposite for the largest eddies: they are about as large as the flow is wide, so the ratio is of order one, not \(10^{-6}\). Chapman and Tobak (1985) say that the conditions justifying the mixing-length idea "rest on the assumptions of kinetic theory (small units traveling relatively long distances between interactions), and these conditions are not completely fulfilled by the properties of turbulent eddies" (p. 5). The lecture calls the resemblance superficial for the same reason. The analogy is a closure assumption, and the chapter on turbulent viscosity and the chapters on models (the chapter titled "Turbulent viscosity models") test it.

The lecture makes a second point: the eddy viscosity, an analogue of a molecular property, seems to contradict the attribute that turbulent flows are flows and not fluids. Chapman and Tobak state the concern in the same direction: Boussinesq's introduction of the eddy-viscosity concept "entailed an inextricable confounding of flow properties and material properties" (pp. 4–5, the sentence runs across the page break). The point can be sharpened. A fluid property is a number that the fluid fixes and that is the same in every flow of that fluid; \(\nu\) is one. The eddy viscosity of (3.10) is a function of position in the flow and differs between flows (chapter 9 evaluates it for a channel), and no value is fixed by the fluid. In the sense of the slide's attribute, \(\nu_T\) is therefore a flow property and the hypothesis does not violate the attribute. What the analogy risks is the habit of treating \(\nu_T\) as a constant of the fluid, as \(\nu\) is, and the cases in which this fails are those that chapter 9 lists: the hypothesis predicts no normal-stress anisotropy in a simple shear, and chapter 9 reports, for the Lee–Moser channel, a normalized anisotropy \(b_{11}=\langle u_1^2\rangle/\langle u_\ell u_\ell\rangle-\tfrac13\) (chapter 9's (9.17)), which the hypothesis predicts to be zero, between 0.13 and 0.51 away from the wall. The lecture adds that large-eddy simulation has proved to be a powerful tool and that the eddy-viscosity hypothesis is at its centre. The statement concerns models of the unresolved scales; it is outside this chapter and is treated in the chapters on large-eddy simulation (the chapters titled "Large-eddy simulation: conceptual steps and filtering" and "Filtered equations, the Smagorinsky model, and LES in wavenumber space"), where it is examined, and it is not checked here.

Dimensional analysis: the form follows, the constant does not L7 17:54

The slide calls dimensional analysis one of the most powerful tools: some aspects of the structure of turbulence depend only on a few independent variables, and in those cases dimensional methods often dictate the relation between the dependent and independent variables; the example is the inertial subrange of the turbulent kinetic energy spectrum. The lecture recalls Buckingham's \(\Pi\) theorem from fluid mechanics.

The statement is this. Let \(q\) variables carry dimensions built from \(r\) independent base dimensions (here length and time, so \(r=2\)), and let \(D\) be the matrix whose entry in row \(i\) and column \(j\) is the exponent of base dimension \(i\) in variable \(j\). A product \(\prod_jx_j^{a_j}\) is dimensionless if and only if \(Da=0\), so the exponent vectors of the dimensionless groups form the null space of \(D\), which has dimension \(q-\operatorname{rank}D\) (Exercise 7 uses it). Buckingham's theorem adds that every relation among the variables that does not depend on the choice of units can be written as a relation among \(q-\operatorname{rank}D\) independent groups. The program computes the null space numerically; the last statement is used without proof, as in the lecture.

First example: the far field of a round jet, an original construction for this chapter. Far from the nozzle, take the mean centreline velocity \(U_c\) to depend on the distance \(x\) from the nozzle, on the kinematic momentum flux \(M=\int\langle U_1\rangle^2\,dA\) (units \(\mathrm{m^4/s^2}\); for a uniform exit profile \(M=\tfrac\pi4\mathcal U^2\mathcal L^2\)), and on \(\nu\). The momentum flux of a jet into still air is conserved along the jet to leading order in the slender-jet approximation, because the ambient pressure is uniform and the entrained fluid carries no streamwise momentum; this is an assumption of the construction. With \(q=4\) variables and \(r=2\), there are two groups,

\[ \Pi_1=\frac{U_cx}{M^{1/2}},\qquad \Pi_2=\frac{\nu}{M^{1/2}}=\frac1{\mathrm{Re}_M},\qquad U_c=\frac{M^{1/2}}{x}\,\Phi\!\left(\mathrm{Re}_M\right). \tag{3.11} \]

If, in addition, \(\nu\) is irrelevant far from the nozzle at large \(\mathrm{Re}_M\), so that \(\Phi\) tends to a constant \(B\), then \(U_c=BM^{1/2}/x\) and the jet width grows in proportion to \(x\), the only length left. The two results are consistent with the conservation of \(M\), since \(M\sim U_c^2\delta^2\) is then independent of \(x\). The prediction has a use that needs no constant: doubling the distance halves the centreline velocity. It rests on two assumptions that dimensional analysis cannot supply: that the nozzle matters only through \(M\) far downstream, and that \(\nu\) drops out. The constant \(B\) is a measured number whose value is not quoted here, because no source for it was available. For the running jet \(M^{1/2}=0.886\times56\times0.025=1.24\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) and \(\mathrm{Re}_M=8.5\times10^{4}\).

Second example: the plotted spectrum of the lecture. Let the one-dimensional longitudinal spectrum \(E_{11}(\kappa_1)\), the density of the energy of the velocity component \(u_1\) in the wavenumber \(\kappa_1\) along \(x_1\) (units \(\mathrm{m^3/s^2}\); the chapters titled "The energy spectrum" and "Spectral energy: Fourier modes, the velocity spectrum tensor, and one-dimensional spectra" define it), depend only on \(\kappa_1\), the dissipation rate \(\varepsilon\) and the viscosity \(\nu\). The dimension matrix of \((E_{11},\kappa_1,\varepsilon,\nu)\) has rank 2, so there are two groups, and the program finds them as the null space of the matrix:

\[ \Pi_a=\frac{E_{11}(\kappa_1)}{(\varepsilon\nu^{5})^{1/4}},\qquad \Pi_b=\kappa_1\Bigl(\frac{\nu^{3}}{\varepsilon}\Bigr)^{1/4}=\kappa_1\eta,\qquad \Pi_a=\Phi(\Pi_b). \tag{3.12} \]

The groups are the axes of the lecture's figure. def tiny(x):
    """Print round-off as '<1e-15' so that the output does not depend on the machine."""
    return "<1e-15" if abs(x) < 1e-15 else f"{x:.0e}"


def box_budget(n=32, nu=0.01):
    """Energy budget of a random solenoidal field in a periodic box (rho = 1)."""
    k1 = np.fft.fftfreq(n, 1/n)
    K = np.array(np.meshgrid(k1, k1, k1, indexing="ij"))
    K2 = (K**2).sum(0)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
    Uh = rng.standard_normal((3, n, n, n)) + 1j*rng.standard_normal((3, n, n, n))
    Uh[:, (K2 == 0) | (K2 > 16)] = 0.0              # modes 0 < |k| <= 4: no aliasing
    Uh -= K*(K*Uh).sum(0)/np.where(K2 == 0, 1, K2)  # remove the part parallel to k
    U = np.fft.ifftn(Uh, axes=(1, 2, 3)).real
    U /= np.sqrt((U*U).sum(0).mean())               # <U.U> = 1

    def ddx(f, j):
        return np.fft.ifftn(1j*K[j]*np.fft.fftn(f)).real

    A = np.array([[ddx(U[i], j) for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)])  # dU_i/dx_j
    conv = np.einsum("j...,ij...->i...", U, A)
    ph = np.fft.fftn(np.einsum("ij...,ji...->...", A, A))/np.where(K2 == 0, 1, K2)
    ph[0, 0, 0] = 0.0
    gradp = np.array([ddx(np.fft.ifftn(ph).real, j) for j in range(3)])
    visc = nu*np.array([sum(ddx(ddx(U[i], m), m) for m in range(3)) for i in range(3)])
    S = 0.5*(A + A.transpose(1, 0, 2, 3, 4))
    w = np.array([A[2, 1] - A[1, 2], A[0, 2] - A[2, 0], A[1, 0] - A[0, 1]])
    print(f"KE={0.5*(U*U).sum(0).mean():.4f}  2 nu <S:S>={2*nu*(S*S).sum((0, 1)).mean():.4e}  "
          f"nu <w.w>={nu*(w*w).sum(0).mean():.4e}")
    print(f"<U.conv>={tiny((U*conv).sum(0).mean())}  <U.grad p>={tiny((U*gradp).sum(0).mean())}"
          f"  <U.visc>={(U*visc).sum(0).mean():.4e}")
    Uh = np.fft.fftn(U, axes=(1, 2, 3))/n**3
    Ch = np.fft.fftn(conv, axes=(1, 2, 3))/n**3
    T = -(np.conj(Uh)*Ch).sum(0).real        # energy gain of each mode from convection
    shell = np.rint(np.sqrt(K2)).astype(int)
    print("convective transfer by shell round(|k|):",
          " ".join(f"{s}:{T[shell == s].sum():+.1e}" for s in range(1, 5)),
          f" sum={tiny(T.sum())}")


def wave_rate(nu, wavelength=1.0, n=256, m=800):
    """Energy decay rate of a deep-water wave from its interior dissipation."""
    k = 2*np.pi/wavelength
    x = np.linspace(0, wavelength, n, endpoint=False)
    z = np.linspace(-8/k, 0, m)
    X, Z = np.meshgrid(x, z, indexing="ij")
    u, w = -k*np.exp(k*Z)*np.sin(k*X), k*np.exp(k*Z)*np.cos(k*X)   # potential flow, A = 1
    dx, dz = x[1] - x[0], z[1] - z[0]
    uz, ux = np.gradient(u, dz, axis=1), np.gradient(u, dx, axis=0)
    wz, wx = np.gradient(w, dz, axis=1), np.gradient(w, dx, axis=0)
    ss = ux**2 + wz**2 + 0.5*(uz + wx)**2
    diss = np.trapezoid(2*nu*ss.mean(0), z)                     # per unit area, rho = 1
    energy = 2*np.trapezoid(0.5*(u**2 + w**2).mean(0), z)       # kinetic + potential
    return diss/energy


def pi_groups(D, first, last):
    """Dimensionless groups of the columns of D with exponents (1, 0) and (0, 1) on two columns."""
    _, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(D)
    N = Vt[len(s):].T                    # null-space basis, one column per group
    return N @ np.linalg.inv(N[[first, last], :])


def frac(v):
    return "(" + ", ".join(str(Fraction(float(e)).limit_denominator(12)) for e in v) + ")"


def burgers_residual(nu, a, n=4000):
    """Residual of the steady stretched-vortex equation for omega = exp(-a r^2/(4 nu))."""
    rc = np.sqrt(4*nu/a)
    r = np.linspace(rc/100, 5*rc, n)
    om = np.exp(-a*r**2/(4*nu))
    d1, d2 = np.gradient(om, r), np.gradient(np.gradient(om, r), r)
    res = -0.5*a*r*d1 - a*om - nu*(d2 + d1/r)
    return rc, np.abs(res[5:-5]).max()/a

What the chapter establishes and what it does not

Seven statements are established, each with its boundary. The kinetic energy of a periodic box is changed only by viscosity, \(d\mathcal K/dt=-\varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon=\nu\langle\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\boldsymbol\omega\rangle_V\), (3.6); the convective and pressure terms redistribute energy, and the program verifies this on one random field. The viscous loss time of the jet's energy-containing eddies is 1.5 to 3.2 turnover times by the estimate \(\varepsilon=C_\varepsilon u_0^3/\ell_0\), and that of a 100 m deep-water swell is \(7.9\times10^{6}\) periods by (3.7), whose boundary layers and other loss mechanisms are not included. The Knudsen number of the smallest eddies is \(1.545\,\mathrm{Ma}\,\mathrm{Re}_0^{-1/4}\) for air at sea level, (3.9), \(8.4\times10^{-4}\) for the jet. A steady stretched vortex exists with core radius \((4\nu/a)^{1/2}\), which is \(2\eta\) at the Kolmogorov strain rate. Dimensional analysis gives the far field of a jet, \(U_c\propto x^{-1}\), under the conservation of \(M\) and the irrelevance of \(\nu\), and the axes and the \(-5/3\) exponent of the lecture's spectrum under Kolmogorov's hypotheses, without their constants. Among three channel simulations spanning a factor of 9.5 in \(\mathrm{Re}_\tau\), the outer defect and the wall-scaled velocity vary by 5 to 10 % and by 0.7 %, and the outer-scaled velocity by 24 to 30 %. The compiled spectra collapse at large wavenumber and separate at small wavenumber in the order of the Reynolds number, and the log–log plot cannot show an error of 20 % in \(\varepsilon\).

Six things are not established. The lecture's statement that the trail of an aircraft is not turbulent is not supported by the source opened, and the diffusion of a contrail by turbulence is not separated from its spreading by shear. The wave estimate does not compute the surface boundary layer, and the lecture's own examples (atmospheric gravity waves, sound) are not computed. The stretched vortex is a model solution with an imposed steady strain. Tennekes and Lumley (1972), Pope (2000), Saddoughi and Veeravalli (1994), Chapman (1979) and Burgers's paper were not opened; their statements are given as the lecture and the sources opened give them. The Reynolds-number comparison uses three simulations of one flow and tests the form of the invariance statements and not the limit \(\mathrm{Re}\to\infty\); no jet data were available. The statement that large-eddy simulation rests on the eddy-viscosity hypothesis is the lecture's and is not examined. Speaker judgments that this book does not adopt as findings: that laminar flow is the exception in the sense of frequency, that diffusivity is the single most important feature, that a contrail is not turbulent, that the Reynolds number alone decides whether a flow is turbulent, that success of an ad hoc assumption depends on inspiration, and that the inertial subrange overlaps entirely in every flow.

Exercise 1 (derive). Show that \(\mathbf U=(U_a\,e^{-\nu\kappa^2t}\cos\kappa y,\,0,\,0)\) is an exact solution of (3.1) with no pressure gradient. Compute the dissipation rate of its energy and the decay rate of \(\langle\tfrac12U_iU_i\rangle\), and compare with the wave rate (3.7) for the same \(\kappa\). Explain the difference.

Solution

Here \(U_1=U_1(y,t)\) only, so \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U=\partial U_1/\partial x=0\) and \((\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U=U_1\,\partial U_1/\partial x=0\) exactly. The momentum equation reduces to \(\partial U_1/\partial t=\nu\,\partial^2U_1/\partial y^2\), which \(U_ae^{-\nu\kappa^2t}\cos\kappa y\) satisfies, since both sides equal \(-\nu\kappa^2U_1\). The only strain is \(S_{12}=\tfrac12\partial U_1/\partial y=-\tfrac12U_a\kappa e^{-\nu\kappa^2t}\sin\kappa y\), so \(S_{ij}S_{ij}=2S_{12}^2=\tfrac12U_a^2\kappa^2e^{-2\nu\kappa^2t}\sin^2\kappa y\), with the average \(\tfrac14U_a^2\kappa^2e^{-2\nu\kappa^2t}\). The dissipation is \(\varepsilon=2\nu\cdot\tfrac14U_a^2\kappa^2e^{-2\nu\kappa^2t}=\tfrac12\nu U_a^2\kappa^2e^{-2\nu\kappa^2t}\). The energy is \(\tfrac12\langle U_1^2\rangle=\tfrac14U_a^2e^{-2\nu\kappa^2t}\), so \(\varepsilon/\text{energy}=2\nu\kappa^2\), and the energy decays as \(e^{-2\nu\kappa^2t}\). The wave loses its total energy twice as fast, at \(4\nu\kappa^2\). In the shear wave all the energy is kinetic. In the potential wave the local ratio of dissipation to kinetic energy is \(2\nu S_{ij}S_{ij}/(\tfrac12U_iU_i)=4\nu\kappa^4A^2/(\tfrac12\kappa^2A^2)=8\nu\kappa^2\), four times the shear-wave value, because \(S_{ij}S_{ij}/(U_iU_i)=2\kappa^2\) for the potential flow against \(\tfrac12\kappa^2\) on average for the shear wave; half of the wave's total energy is potential and is not dissipated directly, which brings the rate for the total energy to \(4\nu\kappa^2\). Both are decays of linear solutions at rates proportional to \(\nu\kappa^2\).

Exercise 4 (counterexample). A student writes: "Turbulence is the motion that dissipates energy, so a flow with \(\varepsilon\gt0\) is turbulent." Use plane Poiseuille flow between plates \(y=\pm h\) with mean speed \(U\) to give a counterexample, and state what property of \(\varepsilon\) discriminates. Take \(h=0.01\ \mathrm m\), \(U=10\ \mathrm{m/s}\), air.

Solution

The laminar profile \(u=(G/2\mu)(h^2-y^2)\) with \(U=Gh^2/(3\mu)\) is an exact solution of (3.1) at every Reynolds number. Its dissipation per unit mass is \(\nu\langle(du/dy)^2\rangle=\nu(G^2/\mu^2)(h^2/3)=3\nu U^2/h^2\), the same as the power of the pressure gradient per unit mass, \(GU/\rho\). For air this is \(3\times1.4607\times10^{-5}\times100/10^{-4}=44\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\): positive, in a flow that is laminar by construction. Dissipation being positive is therefore not a test. What discriminates is the dependence on viscosity at fixed \(U\) and \(h\). The laminar value is proportional to \(\nu\) and falls to \(0.44\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\) when \(\nu\) is 100 times smaller. The turbulent estimate (5.16), with \(u_0=0.05U=0.5\ \mathrm{m/s}\) (assumed) and \(\ell_0=h\), is \(u_0^3/\ell_0=12.5\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\) and does not contain \(\nu\). The attribute is that \(\varepsilon\) stays finite as \(\nu\) decreases at fixed large-scale conditions. The comparison is mathematical: laminar flow at \(Uh/\nu=6.8\times10^{3}\) is not expected to be stable (chapter 4), and the turbulent estimate has an undetermined constant.

Exercise 5 (judge a claim). The lecture says that on the plot of Kolmogorov-scaled spectra the inertial subrange overlaps entirely, no matter what the flow is. Judge the sentence against the compilation of Saddoughi (1993) and give the passages that decide it.

Solution

The sentence is not supported as stated. What the figure supports is that all the spectra lie on one curve at large \(\kappa_1\eta\), where the dissipation range lies. The inertial subrange is a straight \(-5/3\) segment between the energy-containing range and the dissipation range; the figure shows the spectra with \(R_\lambda=23\) to 72 leaving the common curve at \(\kappa_1\eta\) of order \(10^{-2}\) (read from the figure) without a visible straight segment, and the report states that the spectra "peel off from the \(-5/3\) law at the low-wavenumber end in order of increasing Reynolds number" (p. 246). The extent of the \(-5/3\) range depends on the Reynolds number: the report finds "approximately two decades" on the log–log plot at \(R_\lambda\approx1500\) (p. 246) and, from the compensated spectra, "more than one decade ... but less than the log–log plot (Figure 3) suggested" (p. 249). The sentence is true for the dissipation range and for the part of the inertial range that exists at high Reynolds number, and false for flows at low Reynolds number, which have no inertial subrange.

Exercise 6 (design). You want to decide, from lidar images of a persistent contrail, whether its spreading is molecular, shear-driven or turbulent. State what you would measure, what you would compute, and what result would favour each. Use a mean shear \(S=dU/dz\) and assume that the vertical displacement of a trail element is a random walk of diffusivity \(K_z\).

Solution

Measure the vertical width \(\sigma_z(t)\) and the horizontal width \(\sigma_x(t)\) in the direction of the shear, at several times, and the wind profile \(S\). Compute \(K_z=\tfrac12d\sigma_z^2/dt\) from (3.2) and compare it with \(\Gamma\) (about \(2\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\)); molecular spreading gives \(K_z/\Gamma=1\) and a width of 1.2 m after 10 h. A value many orders larger means that the spreading is not molecular. To separate shear from turbulence, use the horizontal width. If the horizontal displacement of an element is \(x=S\int z\,dt\) and \(\langle z^2\rangle=2K_zt\), then, with \(\langle z(t_1)z(t_2)\rangle=2K_z\min(t_1,t_2)\) for a random walk that starts at \(z=0\) (the displacements after the earlier time are independent of the earlier position), \(\langle x^2\rangle=S^2\int_0^t\!\int_0^t2K_z\min(t_1,t_2)\,dt_1dt_2=S^2\cdot2K_z\cdot\tfrac{t^3}{3}=\tfrac23S^2K_zt^3\). A horizontal width that grows as \(t^{3/2}\) with the coefficient predicted by the measured \(S\) and \(K_z\) says that shear acting on a vertical diffusivity is the cause of the horizontal spreading; a vertical diffusivity much greater than \(\Gamma\) says that something other than molecular motion produces the vertical spreading, and a velocity record in the trail, with a variance that does not vanish, would show whether that something is turbulence. If \(\sigma_z\) were also molecular, \(K_z/\Gamma\) would be of order one and the trail would not pass the test of section "Diffusivity". The source opened (Kärcher 2018) states the shear mechanism and does not give \(K_z\).

Exercise 7 (derive and judge). Add the buoyancy flux \(B\) (units \(\mathrm{m^4/s^3}\); \(B\) is local to this exercise and is not the constant \(B\) of the far-field law) to the variables of (3.11) for a heated jet. How many dimensionless groups are there, what is the new group, and what does it say about the claim that \(U_c\propto x^{-1}\)? Use the dimension matrix.

Solution

There are now \(q=5\) variables \((U_c,x,M,\nu,B)\) and \(r=2\), so three groups. The new one must contain \(B\): with exponents \(a\) on \(x\) and \(b\) on \(M\), \(x^{a}B^{1/2}M^{b}\) has length exponent \(a+2+4b\) and time exponent \(-\tfrac32-2b\); setting both to zero gives \(b=-\tfrac34\) and \(a=1\), so \(\Pi_3=xB^{1/2}/M^{3/4}=x/\ell_B\) with \(\ell_B=M^{3/4}/B^{1/2}\), a length. The law \(U_c\propto x^{-1}\) holds only for \(x\ll\ell_B\), where \(\Phi\) does not depend on \(\Pi_3\), if the other assumptions hold; beyond \(\ell_B\) the buoyancy changes the exponent. The original result (3.11) did not contain \(B\) and could not warn of this. This is the limit of dimensional analysis stated in the chapter: a variable that is not in the list is invisible.
