Reynolds equations, closure and turbulent viscosity: what the mean flow needs to know about the fluctuations

A liquid flows at a mean speed of 1.0 m/s through a channel 4 cm high. If the flow were laminar, the pressure gradient that drives it would produce a parabolic profile with a mean speed of about 17 m/s. The real, turbulent flow reaches about one seventeenth of that speed for the same pressure gradient, because between 10 % and 90 % of the way from a wall to the centreplane the shear stress carried by the velocity fluctuations is 37 to 79 times the viscous shear stress. An engineer who wants the mean velocity, the wall friction or the pressure drop cannot afford to compute every fluctuation, and wants equations for the mean alone. This chapter derives those equations from the Navier–Stokes equations, shows that averaging leaves six unknown correlations, the Reynolds stresses, without equations of their own, and examines the simplest way of supplying them: the hypothesis that turbulence acts like an enlarged viscosity. Data from a direct numerical simulation of the same channel test each step.

The objects of the chapter and the running case

The formal model is the incompressible flow of a Newtonian fluid with constant density \(\rho\) and constant kinematic viscosity \(\nu\) (units \(\mathrm{m^2/s}\)), the setting of chapter 6. The velocity field is \(\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)\) with components \(U_i\), \(i=1,2,3\), the pressure is \(p(\mathbf x,t)\), and repeated indices are summed. A conserved passive scalar \(\phi(\mathbf x,t)\) with molecular diffusivity \(\Gamma\) enters in the last third of the chapter. Turbulence makes \(\mathbf U\), \(p\) and \(\phi\) random fields in the sense of chapter 8: at a given point and time each takes a different value in each repetition of the experiment.

The question of the chapter is what equations the mean fields satisfy. The mean is the expectation \(\langle\cdot\rangle\) of chapters 7 and 8, the average over an infinite ensemble of repetitions of the flow, so \(\langle\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)\rangle\) is a non-random function of position and time. The lecture describes the mean as an average "over a period of time". A time average at a fixed point can replace the expectation only when the flow is statistically stationary and the time average converges to the expectation (the ergodic assumption); chapter 8's section "Time averages, ergodicity and the length of a record" states both conditions and the error of a finite record. In this chapter the angle brackets always denote the expectation.

The running case is pressure-driven flow between two parallel plane walls, far from the inlet and from the side walls, where the flow is statistically stationary and the velocity statistics are homogeneous in the streamwise direction \(x_1\) and the spanwise direction \(x_3\) (the mean pressure falls along \(x_1\)) (the channel of chapter 8's section "Stationarity and homogeneity of a field, and the channel flow"). The wall-normal coordinate \(x_2\) is measured from the centreplane, so the walls are at \(x_2=\pm d\), where \(d\) is the half-width (Lee and Moser write \(\delta\)). The distance from the lower wall is \(y=x_2+d\). The wall shear stress is \(\tau_w\), and the friction velocity \(u_\tau\equiv(\tau_w/\rho)^{1/2}\) and the viscous length \(\nu/u_\tau\) define wall units: \(y^+=yu_\tau/\nu\), \(U^+=\langle U_1\rangle/u_\tau\), and a Reynolds stress divided by \(u_\tau^2\) carries a superscript \(+\). The friction Reynolds number is \(\mathrm{Re}_\tau\equiv u_\tau d/\nu\).

The data come from the direct numerical simulation of Lee and Moser (2015) at \(\mathrm{Re}_\tau=1000.512\), which solves the Navier–Stokes equations with no turbulence model in a periodic domain \(8\pi d\) long and \(3\pi d\) wide (data-file header; also arXiv preprint p. 3). The header gives the bulk (cross-section averaged) velocity as \(1/0.0500256=19.99\) times \(u_\tau\). The simulation is dimensionless; to attach units, this chapter chooses \(d=2\ \mathrm{cm}\), \(\nu=1.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) and \(\rho=1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\), round values of the order of those of water at room temperature, used here as inputs, not as measured properties. Then \(u_\tau=\mathrm{Re}_\tau\nu/d=0.0500\ \mathrm{m/s}\), the bulk velocity is \(U_b=1.000\ \mathrm{m/s}\), the bulk Reynolds number \(U_b\,2d/\nu\) is \(4.0\times10^{4}\), the wall shear stress is \(\tau_w=\rho u_\tau^2=2.50\ \mathrm{Pa}\), and the viscous length is \(20\ \mu\mathrm m\). Each section uses this channel to check or to bound its result.

The Reynolds decomposition and the mean continuity equation L23 1:28

The Reynolds decomposition writes the velocity as its mean plus a fluctuation:

\[ \mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)=\langle\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)\rangle+\mathbf u(\mathbf x,t),\qquad \mathbf u\equiv\mathbf U-\langle\mathbf U\rangle. \tag{9.1} \]

The definition is exact; the fluctuation \(\mathbf u\) is whatever the mean does not account for. Upper case denotes the full velocity and lower case the fluctuation, as in chapters 6 to 8. The same decomposition applies to the pressure, \(p=\langle p\rangle+p'\), and to the scalar, \(\phi=\langle\phi\rangle+\phi'\); the prime is used for these two because a lower-case symbol is not available.

Three properties of the expectation carry every derivation in this chapter. For random fields \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\), non-random numbers \(c_1\), \(c_2\), and a non-random function \(g(\mathbf x,t)\):

\[ \langle c_1Q_1+c_2Q_2\rangle=c_1\langle Q_1\rangle+c_2\langle Q_2\rangle,\qquad \langle gQ_1\rangle=g\langle Q_1\rangle,\qquad \Bigl\langle\frac{\partial Q_1}{\partial t}\Bigr\rangle=\frac{\partial\langle Q_1\rangle}{\partial t},\quad \Bigl\langle\frac{\partial Q_1}{\partial x_i}\Bigr\rangle=\frac{\partial\langle Q_1\rangle}{\partial x_i}. \tag{9.2} \]

The first two are linearity of the expectation; the second uses the fact that \(g\) has the same value in every realization. The third is the commutation of the average with differentiation, which the slide notes as "the differentiation commute". It holds because a derivative is a limit of difference quotients and the expectation is linear; chapter 8's section "Mean, fluctuation, Reynolds stresses and the N-point PDF" states the condition under which the limit and the expectation can be exchanged, and a turbulent velocity at finite Reynolds number, smoothed by viscosity, satisfies it.

The first consequence is that the fluctuation has zero mean. Since \(\langle\mathbf U\rangle\) is non-random, \(\langle\langle\mathbf U\rangle\rangle=\langle\mathbf U\rangle\), and averaging (9.1) gives \(\langle\mathbf u\rangle=\langle\mathbf U\rangle-\langle\mathbf U\rangle=0\). The second is that a product of the mean and a fluctuation averages to zero: \(\langle\langle U_i\rangle u_j\rangle=\langle U_i\rangle\langle u_j\rangle=0\). A product of two fluctuations does not: \(\langle u_iu_j\rangle\) is a covariance, and it is zero only if the two components are uncorrelated.

Continuity. For incompressible flow \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U=\partial U_i/\partial x_i=0\) holds in every realization. Averaging and using (9.2), then subtracting the result from the instantaneous equation,

\[ \nabla\cdot\langle\mathbf U\rangle=\frac{\partial\langle U_i\rangle}{\partial x_i}=0,\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf u=\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_i}=0. \tag{9.3} \]

The mean field and the fluctuating field are each solenoidal (divergence-free). The averaged continuity equation is the instantaneous one with \(\mathbf U\) replaced by \(\langle\mathbf U\rangle\), because the equation is linear and nothing new appears. In the channel, statistical homogeneity in \(x_1\) and \(x_3\) makes \(\langle U_i\rangle\) a function of \(x_2\) alone, so (9.3) reduces to \(d\langle U_2\rangle/dx_2=0\); with \(\langle U_2\rangle=0\) at the impermeable walls, \(\langle U_2\rangle=0\) everywhere. The mean flow is streamwise, \(\langle\mathbf U\rangle=(\langle U_1\rangle(x_2),0,0)\) (\(\langle U_3\rangle=0\) follows from the spanwise reflection symmetry of section "Reflection symmetries").

Averaging the momentum equation: the Reynolds stresses appear L23 8:41

The momentum equation contains the nonlinear advection term, and the average of a product is not the product of the averages. The slide therefore begins with the substantial derivative written in conservative form. Since \(U_i\,\partial U_j/\partial x_i=\partial(U_iU_j)/\partial x_i-U_j\,\partial U_i/\partial x_i\) and the last term vanishes by continuity, the Navier–Stokes equations (6.9) can be written

\[ \frac{DU_j}{Dt}=\frac{\partial U_j}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}(U_iU_j)=-\frac1\rho\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_j}+\nu\frac{\partial^2U_j}{\partial x_i\partial x_i}. \tag{9.4} \]

Every term except the product \(U_iU_j\) is linear and averages by (9.2) into the same term with \(U_j\) and \(p\) replaced by their means. Substituting the decomposition into the product and averaging the four terms,

\[ \langle U_iU_j\rangle=\bigl\langle(\langle U_i\rangle+u_i)(\langle U_j\rangle+u_j)\bigr\rangle =\langle U_i\rangle\langle U_j\rangle+\langle U_i\rangle\langle u_j\rangle+\langle U_j\rangle\langle u_i\rangle+\langle u_iu_j\rangle =\langle U_i\rangle\langle U_j\rangle+\langle u_iu_j\rangle. \tag{9.5} \]

The two cross terms vanish because the fluctuations have zero mean. The last term survives. The covariances \(\langle u_iu_j\rangle\) are the Reynolds stresses (chapter 8 named them in (8.22)); this section and the next explain the name.

The mean substantial derivative. The slide defines the rate of change following the mean velocity,

\[ \frac{\bar D}{\bar Dt}\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\langle\mathbf U\rangle\cdot\nabla. \tag{9.6} \]

(The overbar on \(\bar D\) is the slide's notation for this operator only; the book's overbar for a filtered field belongs to the chapters on large-eddy simulation.) The operator \(\bar D/\bar Dt\) is not the mean of \(D/Dt\). Using (9.5) and the mean continuity equation (9.3), \(\partial(\langle U_i\rangle\langle U_j\rangle)/\partial x_i=\langle U_i\rangle\,\partial\langle U_j\rangle/\partial x_i\), so \(\langle DU_j/Dt\rangle=\bar D\langle U_j\rangle/\bar Dt+\partial\langle u_iu_j\rangle/\partial x_i\). The average of (9.4) is therefore

\[ \frac{\bar D\langle U_j\rangle}{\bar Dt}=\nu\nabla^2\langle U_j\rangle-\frac{\partial\langle u_iu_j\rangle}{\partial x_i}-\frac1\rho\frac{\partial\langle p\rangle}{\partial x_j}. \tag{9.7} \]

These are the Reynolds equations, the mean momentum equations. In appearance they are the Navier–Stokes equations for \(\langle\mathbf U\rangle\) and \(\langle p\rangle\), with one additional term, the divergence of the Reynolds stresses. The slide calls this "a crucial difference". The derivation used only (9.2), continuity and constant density; it does not assume that the fluctuations are small, Gaussian or isotropic. If \(\langle u_iu_j\rangle\) were zero, \(\langle\mathbf U\rangle\) would satisfy the Navier–Stokes equations, and in a laminar flow, where all realizations coincide with the mean, the fluctuation is zero and (9.7) is (9.4). The section "The running case" shows by how much the extra term changes the mean flow in the channel.

The Poisson equation for the mean pressure L23 17:29

Chapter 6 showed that in incompressible flow the pressure is not given by an equation of state; it is the field that keeps the velocity solenoidal, and taking the divergence of the momentum equation gives the Poisson equation (6.12), \(\nabla^2p=-\rho\,(\partial U_i/\partial x_j)(\partial U_j/\partial x_i)\). The mean pressure has the same role for the mean velocity. The slide states that its equation can be obtained in two ways, by averaging (6.12) or by taking the divergence of the Reynolds equations, and gives

\[ -\frac1\rho\nabla^2\langle p\rangle=\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial U_i}{\partial x_j}\frac{\partial U_j}{\partial x_i}\Bigr\rangle =\frac{\partial\langle U_i\rangle}{\partial x_j}\frac{\partial\langle U_j\rangle}{\partial x_i}+\frac{\partial^2\langle u_iu_j\rangle}{\partial x_i\partial x_j}. \tag{9.8} \]

The first equality is the average of (6.12) by linearity. The hinge of the second is that the two routes must agree, and they do because the fluctuating field is solenoidal. Averaging the product of the decomposed gradients gives \(\langle\partial U_i/\partial x_j\,\partial U_j/\partial x_i\rangle=(\partial\langle U_i\rangle/\partial x_j)(\partial\langle U_j\rangle/\partial x_i)+\langle(\partial u_i/\partial x_j)(\partial u_j/\partial x_i)\rangle\), as in (9.5). The product rule gives

\[ \frac{\partial^2(u_iu_j)}{\partial x_i\partial x_j}=\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}+u_i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\Bigl(\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_j}\Bigr)+u_j\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\Bigl(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_i}\Bigr)+\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_i}\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_j}, \]

and the last three terms vanish by (9.3). Averaging and commuting the average with the derivatives turns the fluctuation term into \(\partial^2\langle u_iu_j\rangle/\partial x_i\partial x_j\). The divergence route gives the same result directly: the divergence of the left side of (9.7) is \((\partial\langle U_i\rangle/\partial x_j)(\partial\langle U_j\rangle/\partial x_i)\) after continuity is used, and the divergence of the Reynolds-stress term is \(-\partial^2\langle u_iu_j\rangle/\partial x_i\partial x_j\). If the fluctuations were not solenoidal, the two routes would differ by terms containing \(\partial u_i/\partial x_i\).

Equation (9.8) says that the mean pressure is set by two sources: the gradients of the mean velocity, as in a laminar flow, and the second derivatives of the Reynolds stresses. Exercise 4 evaluates both in the channel, where the first source vanishes and the second fixes how the mean pressure varies across the flow.

The Reynolds stress as an apparent stress L23 21:22

The name "stress" comes from rewriting (9.7) as the divergence of a single tensor. Multiplying by \(\rho\) and writing the viscous term through the mean rate of strain (chapter 6's Newtonian stress (6.8)), the slide gives

\[ \rho\frac{\bar D\langle U_j\rangle}{\bar Dt}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\Bigl[\mu\Bigl(\frac{\partial\langle U_i\rangle}{\partial x_j}+\frac{\partial\langle U_j\rangle}{\partial x_i}\Bigr)-\langle p\rangle\delta_{ij}-\rho\langle u_iu_j\rangle\Bigr], \tag{9.9} \]

with \(\mu=\rho\nu\) the dynamic viscosity and \(\delta_{ij}\) the Kronecker delta. The divergence of the first term in the brackets is \(\mu\nabla^2\langle U_j\rangle\) because \(\partial\langle U_i\rangle/\partial x_i=0\). (The equation on the slide omits the factor \(\rho\) on \(\langle u_iu_j\rangle\) inside the brackets; the slide's own text below it names the term \(-\rho\langle u_iu_j\rangle\), which is the dimensionally consistent form used in (9.9).) The bracket is the sum of three stresses: the viscous stress, the isotropic stress \(-\langle p\rangle\delta_{ij}\) of the mean pressure, and the apparent stress \(-\rho\langle u_iu_j\rangle\) that arises from the fluctuating velocity.

Two sign conventions coexist, and this book keeps them apart. The covariance \(\langle u_iu_j\rangle\) has units of \(\mathrm{m^2/s^2}\) and is the quantity that the code, the data files and most statements of this chapter use. The stress is \(-\rho\langle u_iu_j\rangle\), in pascals. A positive diagonal covariance \(\langle u_1^2\rangle\) is a compressive normal stress, like a pressure; a negative covariance \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle\) is a positive shear stress. "Reynolds stress" in reader text means the tensor \(\langle u_iu_j\rangle\) unless the factor \(-\rho\) is written.

The slide also states the physical difference between the two non-pressure stresses. The viscous stress represents the transfer of momentum by molecular motion; the Reynolds stress represents the transfer of momentum by the fluctuating velocity field. The analogy is exact in one sense and fails in another. It is exact in that both are mean momentum fluxes, as the next section shows. It fails in that the molecular flux is related to the mean velocity gradient by a constitutive law with a material constant \(\mu\), whereas the Reynolds stress is a property of the flow, with no law yet connecting it to anything. That gap is the subject of the rest of the chapter.

The Reynolds stress as a mean momentum flux L23 24:19

Consider a fixed control volume \(\mathcal V\) with bounding surface \(\mathcal A\) and outward unit normal \(\mathbf n\) (the slide's symbols). The momentum per unit volume is \(\rho\mathbf U\), and the volume flow rate per unit area into \(\mathcal V\) through \(\mathcal A\) is \(-\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf n\). The rate at which flow through the surface brings momentum into the volume is therefore \(\dot{\mathbf M}=\iint_{\mathcal A}\rho\mathbf U(-\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf n)\,dA\). Taking the mean of its \(j\) component, using (9.5), and converting the surface integral to a volume integral with the divergence theorem,

\[ \langle\dot M_j\rangle=-\iint_{\mathcal A}\rho\bigl(\langle U_i\rangle\langle U_j\rangle+\langle u_iu_j\rangle\bigr)n_i\,dA =-\iiint_{\mathcal V}\rho\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\bigl(\langle U_i\rangle\langle U_j\rangle+\langle u_iu_j\rangle\bigr)\,dV. \tag{9.10} \]

(The slide carries the factor \(n_i\) into the volume integral as well; after the divergence theorem the normal no longer appears.) The first term is the flux carried by the mean velocity. The second is the mean flux of momentum carried across the surface by the fluctuations, and the volume integrand is the term \(-\partial\langle u_iu_j\rangle/\partial x_i\) of (9.7). The slide's conclusion is that the Reynolds stress in the Reynolds equations arises from the mean momentum flux due to the fluctuating velocity on the boundary.

The flux reading explains the sign of the channel's shear stress. Near the lower wall the mean velocity increases with \(y\). A fluid particle that moves away from the wall (\(u_2\gt0\)) comes from a region of lower mean velocity and tends to arrive with \(u_1\lt0\); a particle that moves toward the wall (\(u_2\lt0\)) tends to arrive with \(u_1\gt0\). Both kinds of event make \(u_1u_2\) negative, so \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle\lt0\): the fluctuations carry streamwise momentum toward the wall, down the mean gradient. This argument is a heuristic, not a derivation: it assumes that particles keep their streamwise velocity for a while as they move, which is the analogy with molecules that the turbulent-viscosity hypothesis will make explicit. The channel data confirm the sign: \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle^+\) is negative at every point of the lower half of the channel off the wall, with a minimum of \(-0.903\) at \(y^+=54\) (data file).

The running case: the Reynolds equations in a fully developed channel

In the channel the mean fields are stationary and depend on \(x_2\) only, except the mean pressure, which must also fall along the channel to drive the flow. With \(\langle\mathbf U\rangle=(\langle U_1\rangle(x_2),0,0)\), the left side of (9.7) vanishes: \(\partial/\partial t=0\), and \(\langle\mathbf U\rangle\cdot\nabla\) is \(\langle U_1\rangle\,\partial/\partial x_1\), which acts on functions of \(x_2\). Of the Reynolds-stress divergence only the \(x_2\) derivatives survive. The \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) components of (9.7) become

\[ 0=-\frac1\rho\frac{\partial\langle p\rangle}{\partial x_1}+\frac{d}{dx_2}\Bigl(\nu\frac{d\langle U_1\rangle}{dx_2}-\langle u_1u_2\rangle\Bigr), \tag{9.11} \] \[ 0=-\frac1\rho\frac{\partial\langle p\rangle}{\partial x_2}-\frac{d\langle u_2^2\rangle}{dx_2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle p\rangle+\rho\langle u_2^2\rangle=p_w(x_1). \tag{9.12} \]

The second integrates at once: \(\langle u_2^2\rangle\) is zero at the wall (the wall is impermeable, so \(u_2=0\) there in every realization), so \(p_w(x_1)\) is the mean pressure on the wall. The mean pressure across the channel is lower than at the wall by \(\rho\langle u_2^2\rangle\). This is the hinge of the whole reduction. Since \(\langle u_2^2\rangle\) does not depend on \(x_1\), \(\partial\langle p\rangle/\partial x_1=dp_w/dx_1\) is the same at every \(x_2\). In (9.11) the first term then depends on \(x_1\) only and the second on \(x_2\) only, so both are constants. Integrating from the wall, where the viscous stress is \(\tau_w\) and \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle=0\), to the centreplane, where both vanish by the reflection symmetry about \(x_2=0\) (section "Reflection symmetries": \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle\) is odd in \(x_2\) and \(\langle U_1\rangle\) is even, so its derivative is odd), gives \(-dp_w/dx_1=\tau_w/d\), and

\[ \nu\frac{d\langle U_1\rangle}{dy}-\langle u_1u_2\rangle=u_\tau^2\Bigl(1-\frac yd\Bigr),\qquad\text{in wall units}\quad \frac{dU^+}{dy^+}-\langle u_1u_2\rangle^+=1-\frac yd. \tag{9.13} \]

The total shear stress, viscous plus Reynolds, falls linearly from \(\tau_w\) at the wall to zero at the centreplane, whatever the turbulence does. Equation (9.13) is exact for the statistically stationary, fully developed channel; it contains no model. It also states the closure problem of the next section in its smallest form: one equation for the two unknown functions \(\langle U_1\rangle\) and \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle\).

Checks against the data. The Lee–Moser files give \(dU^+/dy^+\), \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle^+\) and the mean pressure at 256 points of the lower half (the centreplane point is not computed, the header states). The largest departure of the left side of (9.13) from \(1-y/d\) over the 256 points is \(3.9\times10^{-4}\) in wall units; this is a test, because \(dU^+/dy^+\) and \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle^+\) are averaged independently, and the residual is below 0.002, the bound that Lee and Moser report for the discrepancy in all their simulations (preprint p. 5). The pressure column is not a second test: \(|\langle p\rangle^++\langle u_2^2\rangle^+|\) is at most \(2\times10^{-15}\), eleven orders of magnitude below the first residual, which shows that the file's mean pressure was obtained from \(\langle u_2^2\rangle\) through (9.12) rather than averaged on its own (the simulation method eliminates the pressure, preprint p. 3). The file therefore uses (9.12) as its convention for \(\langle p\rangle\); it does not confirm it. Lee and Moser use the linearity of the total stress as their test of stationarity (preprint p. 5; chapter 8 cites the same page). For the running case, \(-dp_w/dx_1=\tau_w/d=2.50/0.02=125\ \mathrm{Pa/m}\).

Figure 9.1 shows how the fixed total stress is shared. At \(y^+=5\), 0.1 mm from the wall in the running case, viscosity carries 88 % of it. The two parts are equal at \(y^+\approx11\), and at \(y/d=0.1\) (\(y^+=99\), 2 mm from the wall) the Reynolds stress carries 97.3 %. Over most of the channel the momentum transfer that sets the mean velocity is done by the fluctuations, as the slide states.

What the Reynolds stress does to the mean flow. Set \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle=0\) in (9.13) at the same wall stress: \(dU^+/dy^+=1-y/d\), so \(U^+=\mathrm{Re}_\tau\,(y/d-\tfrac12y^2/d^2)\), the laminar parabola. Its average over the half-width is \(U_b^+=\mathrm{Re}_\tau/3=333.5\), against 19.99 in the turbulent flow, a factor of 16.7; its centreline value is \(\mathrm{Re}_\tau/2=500\), against 22.6. For the running case, the pressure gradient of \(125\ \mathrm{Pa/m}\) that drives water at 1.0 m/s through the turbulent channel would drive the laminar flow at 16.7 m/s. The laminar solution is a solution of the equations at this pressure gradient, not a flow that the channel would realize; it measures the effect of the one term in which (9.7) differs from the Navier–Stokes equations.

The closure problem: four equations and ten unknowns L24 8:03

The slide states the closure problem by counting. For a general statistically three-dimensional flow there are four independent equations for the mean fields: the three components of the Reynolds equations (9.7), together with either the mean continuity equation (9.3) or the mean-pressure equation (9.8). Not both: (9.8) was obtained from the divergence of (9.7) with (9.3) imposed, so it adds no information that the other four equations lack. The unknowns are the three components of \(\langle\mathbf U\rangle\), the mean pressure \(\langle p\rangle\), and the Reynolds stresses. The Reynolds-stress tensor is symmetric, \(\langle u_iu_j\rangle=\langle u_ju_i\rangle\), so of its nine components six are independent. Four equations for ten unknown fields cannot determine them: the system is not closed.

The lecture links this counting to the description of turbulence as one of the main unsolved problems of classical physics. The counting itself is precise; the description is the lecture's judgement. In the channel the count is one equation, (9.13), for two unknown functions.

The obvious repair, writing equations for the Reynolds stresses, does not close the system either. The averages of products of three velocities contain a triple correlation, \(\langle u_iu_ju_k\rangle\), that the decomposition does not reduce (Exercise 1 derives the decomposition), and an equation for the second moments, derived from the Navier–Stokes equations by multiplying the fluctuation equation by \(u_i\) and averaging, inherits such third moments from the advection term. Each level of moments brings in the next. The closure problem is therefore not a defect of the averaging; it is the consequence of averaging a quadratic nonlinearity. Two ways out exist: compute the fluctuations, as a direct numerical simulation does, or supply a model that expresses the unknown moments through the known ones. The rest of the chapter follows the second way in its simplest form; chapter 16, "Modeling approaches and criteria for appraising models", and chapter 18, "Turbulent viscosity models", take it further.

Properties of the Reynolds-stress tensor L24 12:40

The diagonal components \(\langle u_1^2\rangle\), \(\langle u_2^2\rangle\), \(\langle u_3^2\rangle\) are the normal stresses and the off-diagonal components, such as \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle\), are the shear stresses, by analogy with a stress tensor in which the diagonal entries act normal to a surface and the off-diagonal entries act along it. The turbulent kinetic energy is defined as half the trace:

\[ k(\mathbf x,t)\equiv\tfrac12\langle\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u\rangle=\tfrac12\langle u_iu_i\rangle=\tfrac12\bigl(\langle u_1^2\rangle+\langle u_2^2\rangle+\langle u_3^2\rangle\bigr). \tag{9.14} \]

It is the mean kinetic energy per unit mass of the fluctuating velocity field (units \(\mathrm{m^2/s^2}\)). It is distinct from the kinetic energy \(\tfrac12\langle U_i\rangle\langle U_i\rangle\) of the mean flow, and the mean of the total kinetic energy per unit mass is their sum, by (9.5) with \(i=j\) summed.

Positive semi-definiteness. The slide states that in the principal axes of the Reynolds-stress tensor the shear stresses are zero and the normal stresses are the eigenvalues, which are non-negative, so the tensor is symmetric positive semi-definite. (The slide writes "\(\langle u_1^2\rangle\gt0\)" beside the word non-negative; zero is allowed, and at a wall the normal stresses are zero.) A direct proof needs no principal axes. For any fixed unit vector \(\mathbf e\), the component of the fluctuation along \(\mathbf e\) is the random variable \(\mathbf e\cdot\mathbf u=e_iu_i\), and

\[ e_i\langle u_iu_j\rangle e_j=\bigl\langle(e_iu_i)^2\bigr\rangle\ge0. \tag{9.15} \]

The left side is the variance of the velocity component in direction \(\mathbf e\). A matrix whose quadratic form is non-negative for every vector is positive semi-definite, and a real symmetric matrix of this kind has non-negative real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors, the principal axes of the slide. Two consequences are used repeatedly below. With \(\mathbf e\) a coordinate vector, each normal stress is non-negative. Applied to the \(2\times2\) block of components \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) (no summation over Greek indices), non-negativity of the determinant gives the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality of chapter 7's section "Covariance and the correlation coefficient"; the correlation coefficient \(\rho_{\alpha\beta}\) of that section carries two subscripts and is not the density \(\rho\):

\[ \langle u_\alpha^2\rangle\ge0,\qquad \langle u_\alpha u_\beta\rangle^2\le\langle u_\alpha^2\rangle\langle u_\beta^2\rangle,\qquad \rho_{\alpha\beta}\equiv\frac{\langle u_\alpha u_\beta\rangle}{(\langle u_\alpha^2\rangle\langle u_\beta^2\rangle)^{1/2}}\in[-1,1]. \tag{9.16} \]

These are realizability conditions: a matrix that violates them cannot be the covariance matrix of any random vector, whatever the flow. For the running case at \(y/d=0.1\) the data give \(\langle u_1^2\rangle^+=3.92\), \(\langle u_2^2\rangle^+=1.16\) and \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle^+=-0.877\), so \(\rho_{12}=-0.41\); between \(y/d=0.05\) and \(0.5\) the value stays between \(-0.396\) and \(-0.417\) (data file). The streamwise and wall-normal fluctuations are correlated, but far from perfectly. Exercise 8, adapted from a practice problem, applies (9.16) to two matrices that the problem states are incorrect.

Isotropic and anisotropic parts L24 14:49

An isotropic Reynolds-stress tensor has the form \(\tfrac23k\delta_{ij}\): equal normal stresses, each one third of \(2k\), and zero shear stresses. Chapter 8's section "Isotropy" derived this form from invariance under rotations and reflections. Any Reynolds-stress tensor can be split into this isotropic part and a remainder. The slide defines the deviatoric anisotropic part \(a_{ij}\) and the normalized anisotropy tensor \(b_{ij}\):

\[ a_{ij}\equiv\langle u_iu_j\rangle-\tfrac23k\delta_{ij},\qquad b_{ij}\equiv\frac{a_{ij}}{2k}=\frac{\langle u_iu_j\rangle}{\langle u_\ell u_\ell\rangle}-\tfrac13\delta_{ij}, \tag{9.17} \] \[ \langle u_iu_j\rangle=\tfrac23k\delta_{ij}+a_{ij}=2k\bigl(\tfrac13\delta_{ij}+b_{ij}\bigr). \tag{9.18} \]

Both \(a_{ij}\) and \(b_{ij}\) are symmetric and have zero trace (\(a_{ii}=2k-\tfrac23k\cdot3=0\)); this is the meaning of "deviatoric". The tensor \(b_{ij}\) is dimensionless, and it is zero exactly when the stresses are isotropic.

The lecture describes the isotropic part as the diagonal part and the anisotropic part as the off-diagonal part. That reading is not what (9.17) says. The diagonal entries of \(a_{ij}\) are \(\langle u_\alpha^2\rangle-\tfrac23k\), which vanish only when the three normal stresses are equal. In the running case they are not equal anywhere: at \(y^+=1\), \(b_{ij}\) has diagonal \((+0.409,-0.333,-0.076)\); at \(y/d=0.1\), \((+0.229,-0.167,-0.062)\); and at the point nearest the centreplane, \((+0.129,-0.064,-0.065)\), while its off-diagonal entry \(b_{12}\) is never larger in magnitude than 0.135 (data files). In the channel, most of the anisotropy is in the diagonal.

Bounds on the anisotropy. Realizability (9.16) limits \(b_{ij}\). Since \(0\le\langle u_\alpha^2\rangle\le\langle u_\ell u_\ell\rangle=2k\), each diagonal entry satisfies \(-\tfrac13\le b_{\alpha\alpha}\le\tfrac23\). For an off-diagonal entry, (9.16) and the inequality between geometric and arithmetic means give \(|\langle u_\alpha u_\beta\rangle|\le\tfrac12(\langle u_\alpha^2\rangle+\langle u_\beta^2\rangle)\le k\), so

\[ -\tfrac13\le b_{\alpha\alpha}\le\tfrac23,\qquad |b_{\alpha\beta}|\le\tfrac12\quad(\alpha\ne\beta), \tag{9.19} \]

and, more completely, every eigenvalue of \(b_{ij}\) lies in \([-\tfrac13,\tfrac23]\), because by (9.18) the eigenvalues of \(b_{ij}\) are those of \(\langle u_iu_j\rangle/(2k)\) minus \(\tfrac13\), and the latter are non-negative and sum to 1. The lower bound is reached when one velocity component vanishes. The running case reaches it at the wall: at \(y^+=1\), \(b_{22}=-0.333\), because the wall-normal fluctuation must vanish at an impermeable wall faster than the other two (\(\langle u_2^2\rangle^+=1.3\times10^{-4}\) there, against \(0.181\) and \(0.063\)). Near the wall the turbulence is close to two-component. The largest diagonal value in the data is \(b_{11}=0.512\) at \(y^+=7.8\), below the upper bound of \(\tfrac23\).

Only the anisotropic part transports momentum: the modified mean pressure L24 20:51

Substitute (9.18) into the pressure and Reynolds-stress terms of the Reynolds equations. Since \(\partial(\tfrac23\rho k\delta_{ij})/\partial x_i=\partial(\tfrac23\rho k)/\partial x_j\) (the Kronecker delta turns the derivative index \(i\) into \(j\)),

\[ \rho\frac{\partial\langle u_iu_j\rangle}{\partial x_i}+\frac{\partial\langle p\rangle}{\partial x_j} =\rho\frac{\partial a_{ij}}{\partial x_i}+\frac{\partial p_{\mathrm m}}{\partial x_j},\qquad p_{\mathrm m}\equiv\langle p\rangle+\tfrac23\rho k. \tag{9.20} \]

The slide's conclusion is that only the anisotropic component \(a_{ij}\) is effective in transporting momentum, and that the isotropic component can be absorbed in a modified mean pressure \(p_{\mathrm m}\) (written \(\langle P_m\rangle\) on the board).

The mechanism deserves a statement, because a non-zero gradient of \(\tfrac23\rho k\) is still a force on the fluid. In incompressible flow the mean pressure has no equation of its own; it is whatever field makes the mean velocity satisfy \(\nabla\cdot\langle\mathbf U\rangle=0\), the role that (9.8) expresses. If a force per unit mass that is the gradient of a scalar, \(-\rho^{-1}\nabla\Phi\), is added to the right side of (9.7), the pair \((\langle\mathbf U\rangle,\langle p\rangle-\Phi)\) satisfies the new equations whenever \((\langle\mathbf U\rangle,\langle p\rangle)\) satisfied the old ones, with the same continuity equation and the same velocity boundary conditions. A gradient force changes the pressure and leaves the velocity unchanged. The isotropic part of the Reynolds stresses is such a force with \(\Phi=\tfrac23\rho k\). This is the precise sense in which it does not transport momentum.

The statement has two boundaries. First, the pressure that a solver of (9.7) with (9.20) returns is \(p_{\mathrm m}\), not \(\langle p\rangle\); recovering \(\langle p\rangle\) needs \(k\), and a boundary condition that prescribes \(\langle p\rangle\) itself, at an outflow or a free surface, must be converted. Second, the argument concerns the isotropic part only: \(a_{ij}\) is not a gradient in general and does change the velocity. The running case shows the first boundary: by (9.12), \(\langle p\rangle=p_w-\rho\langle u_2^2\rangle\), so \(p_{\mathrm m}=p_w-\rho(\langle u_2^2\rangle-\tfrac23k)=p_w-\rho a_{22}=p_w-2\rho kb_{22}\), a difference from the wall pressure that is set by the anisotropy \(b_{22}\), not by \(k\) alone. At \(y/d=0.1\), \(\tfrac23k^+-\langle u_2^2\rangle^+=2.327-1.164=1.163\), that is \(1.163\,\rho u_\tau^2=2.9\ \mathrm{Pa}\) in the running case.

Irrotational fluctuations: the Corrsin–Kistler result L25 1:28

The slide begins from the statement that an essential feature of turbulent flows is that they are rotational, and asks what the Reynolds stresses would do if the fluctuations were irrotational. Then the fluctuating vorticity is zero, so \(\partial u_i/\partial x_j-\partial u_j/\partial x_i=0\) for all \(i\), \(j\) (this antisymmetric combination is twice the rate-of-rotation tensor (6.23) of the fluctuation, and (6.24) relates it to the vorticity). Multiplying by \(u_i\), averaging, and using the product rule and \(\partial u_i/\partial x_i=0\):

\[ \Bigl\langle u_i\Bigl(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}-\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\Bigr)\Bigr\rangle =\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\bigl(\tfrac12\langle u_iu_i\rangle\bigr)-\frac{\partial\langle u_iu_j\rangle}{\partial x_i}=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\partial\langle u_iu_j\rangle}{\partial x_i}=\frac{\partial k}{\partial x_j}. \tag{9.21} \]

The first term uses \(u_i\,\partial u_i/\partial x_j=\partial(\tfrac12u_iu_i)/\partial x_j\); the second uses \(u_i\,\partial u_j/\partial x_i=\partial(u_iu_j)/\partial x_i-u_j\,\partial u_i/\partial x_i\). The slide calls the result the Corrsin–Kistler equation. Corrsin and Kistler (1955) derive it in their study of the boundary between turbulent and non-turbulent fluid, where the fluctuations outside the turbulence front are irrotational, and write it as \(\partial\langle u_iu_j\rangle/\partial x_j=\tfrac12\,\partial\langle q^2\rangle/\partial x_i\) with \(q^2=u_ju_j\) (NACA Report 1244, p. 15, the display following eq. (24), which states the irrotationality condition; they write overbars for averages). With (9.21) the Reynolds force becomes \(-\rho\nabla k\), a gradient, and their Reynolds equation (25) carries \(\langle p\rangle+\tfrac12\rho\langle q^2\rangle=\langle p\rangle+\rho k\) as the pressure.

By the argument of the previous section, irrotational fluctuations therefore change the mean pressure and do not change the mean velocity. Corrsin and Kistler state this as "irrotational fluctuations give no net apparent shear forces on a fluid element" (p. 15). The lecture goes further and says that in irrotational flow the Reynolds stress has only an isotropic effect, and it reads the contrast with real shear flows as further evidence that turbulence is rotational. The first part needs a qualification that Corrsin and Kistler give in the next sentence: "This does not necessarily mean, however, that the Reynolds shear force on a plane is zero or that the Reynolds stress tensor \(-\rho\overline{u_iu_k}\) has only leading diagonal terms" (p. 15). Equation (9.21) constrains the divergence of the tensor, not the tensor: \(a_{ij}\) can be non-zero, provided \(\partial a_{ij}/\partial x_i=\partial(\tfrac13k)/\partial x_j\). The conclusion that survives is the one that matters for the mean flow: momentum transport by the Reynolds stresses in the sense of section "Only the anisotropic part transports momentum" requires vorticity in the fluctuations.

Reflection symmetries of the Reynolds stresses L25 7:55

For some flows the geometry fixes some Reynolds stresses without any dynamics. The slide considers a statistically two-dimensional flow whose statistics are independent of \(x_3\) and invariant under reflection of the \(x_3\) axis. In terms of the one-point velocity PDF \(f(\mathbf V;\mathbf x,t)\) of chapter 8, with sample-space variable \(\mathbf V=(V_1,V_2,V_3)\):

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_3}=0,\qquad f(V_1,V_2,V_3;x_1,x_2,x_3,t)=f(V_1,V_2,-V_3;x_1,x_2,-x_3,t). \tag{9.22} \]

A reflection of the \(x_3\) axis changes the sign of the third coordinate and of the third velocity component. With the first condition, the second becomes \(f(V_1,V_2,V_3;\mathbf x,t)=f(V_1,V_2,-V_3;\mathbf x,t)\) at every point: the PDF is even in \(V_3\). Any mean that is odd in \(V_3\) therefore vanishes: \(\langle U_3\rangle=\int V_3f\,d\mathbf V=0\), and likewise \(\langle u_1u_3\rangle=\langle u_2u_3\rangle=0\). The Reynolds-stress tensor of such a flow has four non-zero components:

\[ \langle u_iu_j\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}\langle u_1^2\rangle&\langle u_1u_2\rangle&0\\ \langle u_1u_2\rangle&\langle u_2^2\rangle&0\\ 0&0&\langle u_3^2\rangle\end{pmatrix},\qquad\langle U_3\rangle=0. \tag{9.23} \]

(The slide prints \(\langle u_3^2\rangle\) in the middle of the diagonal, where \(\langle u_2^2\rangle\) belongs.) Of the six unknown stresses, four remain. The channel has one more symmetry: its statistics are invariant under reflection about the centreplane, \(f(V_1,V_2,V_3;x_1,x_2,x_3,t)=f(V_1,-V_2,V_3;x_1,-x_2,x_3,t)\). This symmetry relates the statistics at \(x_2\) to those at \(-x_2\); it does not make any of them vanish except on the centreplane. A quantity odd in \(V_2\), such as \(\langle U_2\rangle\) or \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle\), is an odd function of \(x_2\); a quantity even in \(V_2\), such as \(\langle U_1\rangle\) and the normal stresses, is an even function of \(x_2\).

The slide's wording "\(\langle U_2\rangle=0\) and \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle=0\) are odd functions of \(x_2\)" and the lecture's conclusion that only the three normal stresses remain in the channel do not follow. The shear stress \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle\) is odd in \(x_2\) and therefore zero on the centreplane, but it is the term that carries 97 % of the momentum flux at \(y/d=0.1\) (section "The running case"). Its sign changes across the centreplane: near the upper wall, momentum is carried upward, toward that wall. \(\langle U_2\rangle\) is zero everywhere, but by continuity and the impermeable walls (section "The Reynolds decomposition"), not by the reflection symmetry alone.

The simulation provides a check of (9.23). Lee and Moser report \(\langle u_1u_3\rangle\), \(\langle u_2u_3\rangle\) and the mean spanwise velocity, which should be zero by symmetry, "as an indicator of the quality of the statistics" (data-file header). The mean scalar equation and the scalar flux L25 10:35

The conserved passive scalar of chapter 6, a temperature difference small enough not to change \(\rho\) or \(\nu\), or a dilute dye, obeys (6.15), which in conservative form is \(\partial\phi/\partial t+\nabla\cdot(\mathbf U\phi)=\Gamma\nabla^2\phi\). Decomposing \(\mathbf U\) and \(\phi\) and averaging, the nonlinear term gives \(\langle\mathbf U\phi\rangle=\langle\mathbf U\rangle\langle\phi\rangle+\langle\mathbf u\phi'\rangle\) by the argument of (9.5). The vector \(\langle\mathbf u\phi'\rangle\) is the turbulent scalar flux: the mean rate at which the fluctuations carry the scalar across a unit area, in units of the scalar times \(\mathrm{m/s}\). The gradient-diffusion hypothesis L25 14:22

The slide calls the gradient-diffusion hypothesis the basis for a lot of the earlier simplistic models; the lecture adds that almost every turbulence model (as distinct from a direct simulation) uses it. It states that the scalar flux is down the mean gradient:

\[ \langle\mathbf u\phi'\rangle=-\Gamma_T\nabla\langle\phi\rangle, \tag{9.25} \]

where the turbulent diffusivity \(\Gamma_T(\mathbf x,t)\) is a scalar field (units \(\mathrm{m^2/s}\)) that the slide requires to be positive. Substituting into (9.24),

\[ \frac{\bar D\langle\phi\rangle}{\bar Dt}=\nabla\cdot\bigl(\Gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}\nabla\langle\phi\rangle\bigr),\qquad \Gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}\equiv\Gamma+\Gamma_T. \tag{9.26} \]

The mean scalar then obeys the instantaneous scalar equation with \(\mathbf U\), \(\phi\) and \(\Gamma\) replaced by \(\langle\mathbf U\rangle\), \(\langle\phi\rangle\) and \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}\). This is chapter 4's eddy diffusivity \(K\) written as a field and placed inside the mean equation, and it inherits the objection chapter 4 recorded: turbulence is a property of the flow, not of the fluid. The lecture says the same thing here, and names the step a hypothesis for that reason.

Equation (9.25) makes two separate claims. The first is alignment: the flux vector is parallel to the mean gradient vector. The second is direction and sign: the flux points down the gradient, which requires \(\Gamma_T\ge0\). The positivity on the slide is not a convention; a negative \(\Gamma_T\) is not a small modelling error. If \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}\lt0\), (9.26) is a backward diffusion equation, in which a sinusoidal perturbation of wavenumber \(\kappa\) grows at the rate \(|\Gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}|\kappa^2\); the growth rate has no upper bound as \(\kappa\) increases, so the initial-value problem is ill-posed and a numerical solution depends on the grid. Exercise 6 quantifies this. A model of the form (9.25) must therefore keep \(\Gamma_T\ge0\) (more precisely \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}\ge0\)).

Measured fluxes do not always point down the gradient. Milani, Ling and Eaton (2020) define counter-gradient transport as a flux component with the same sign as the corresponding gradient component, which "directly contradicts" (9.25) with a positive diffusivity, and list earlier numerical and experimental reports of it in jets in crossflow (arXiv preprint p. 3). In their highly resolved large-eddy simulations of an inclined jet in crossflow they identify two types (p. 1): one throughout the windward shear layer, caused by transport across the gradient; and one near the wall just after injection, where both the wall-normal flux \(\langle u_2\phi'\rangle\) and the gradient \(\partial\langle\phi\rangle/\partial x_2\) are positive and the budget of the flux points to non-local effects, chiefly through the fluctuating pressure (pp. 8–9). A positive \(\Gamma_T\) cannot represent either region; their remedy is a tensor diffusivity with an analytical guarantee that the resulting equation is stable (p. 1), which keeps the condition derived above in matrix form. These results concern one geometry at two velocity ratios; they show that the hypothesis can fail, not how often it fails.

The turbulent-viscosity hypothesis L25 18:50

The turbulent-viscosity hypothesis is the momentum counterpart of (9.25). The slide attributes it to Boussinesq in 1877 and presents both hypotheses as analogies: gradient diffusion with Fourier's law of heat conduction and Fick's law of diffusion, turbulent viscosity with the stress–rate-of-strain relation of a Newtonian fluid, \(\tau=\mu\,du/dy\). Schmitt (2007) confirms the date of the publication and adds that it reports a meeting of the French Academy of Sciences held in 1872, more than twenty years before Reynolds wrote the averaged equations in 1895; he reads Boussinesq's expressions for the mean stresses as a tensorial closure of the form used today (HAL preprint pp. 3–4). In terms of the mean rate of strain

\[ \langle S_{ij}\rangle=\frac12\Bigl(\frac{\partial\langle U_i\rangle}{\partial x_j}+\frac{\partial\langle U_j\rangle}{\partial x_i}\Bigr), \]

which by (9.2) is both the mean of the strain-rate tensor \(S_{ij}\) of chapter 6 and the strain rate of the mean velocity (the slide writes \(\bar S_{ij}\); this book keeps the overbar for filtered fields), the hypothesis states that the deviatoric Reynolds stress is proportional to the mean strain rate:

\[ -\rho\langle u_iu_j\rangle+\tfrac23\rho k\delta_{ij}=2\rho\nu_T\langle S_{ij}\rangle,\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad a_{ij}=-2\nu_T\langle S_{ij}\rangle, \tag{9.27} \]

with the turbulent (or eddy) viscosity \(\nu_T(\mathbf x,t)\) a scalar field in \(\mathrm{m^2/s}\), positive according to the slide. (The slide that restates (9.27) for \(a_{ij}\) writes \(\langle u_iu_i\rangle\) in its definition; (9.17) has \(\langle u_iu_j\rangle\).) The left side must be the deviatoric part, not \(-\rho\langle u_iu_j\rangle\) itself: \(\langle S_{ii}\rangle=0\) by continuity, whereas \(\langle u_iu_i\rangle=2k\gt0\), so the full Reynolds stress cannot be proportional to \(\langle S_{ij}\rangle\). Substituting (9.27) and (9.20) into (9.7),

\[ \frac{\bar D\langle U_j\rangle}{\bar Dt}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\Bigl[\nu_{\mathrm{eff}}\Bigl(\frac{\partial\langle U_i\rangle}{\partial x_j}+\frac{\partial\langle U_j\rangle}{\partial x_i}\Bigr)\Bigr]-\frac1\rho\frac{\partial p_{\mathrm m}}{\partial x_j},\qquad \nu_{\mathrm{eff}}\equiv\nu+\nu_T. \tag{9.28} \]

The mean velocity obeys the Navier–Stokes equations with \(\mathbf U\), \(\nu\) and \(p\) replaced by \(\langle\mathbf U\rangle\), \(\nu_{\mathrm{eff}}\) and the modified mean pressure. The isotropic part, which (9.27) cannot represent, has gone into \(p_{\mathrm m}\), as (9.20) allows.

The hypothesis is a model, not a consequence of the equations of motion, and it makes a strong claim. Both sides of (9.27) are symmetric tensors with zero trace, so each has five independent components. The hypothesis relates the five components of \(a_{ij}\) to the five of \(\langle S_{ij}\rangle\) through one scalar: the principal axes of the anisotropy coincide with those of the mean strain, and the ratios of the eigenvalues are equal. For the scalar the corresponding claim is that the three components of the flux are aligned with the gradient through one scalar \(\Gamma_T\). The closure problem shrinks from six unknown stresses and three unknown flux components to two unknown scalar fields, \(\nu_T\) and \(\Gamma_T\); \(k\) disappears into \(p_{\mathrm m}\). The lecture states that every turbulence model of this family differs in how it specifies \(\nu_T\) and \(\Gamma_T\); chapter 18, "Turbulent viscosity models", develops those specifications.

Normal stresses in simple shear. In the channel the only non-zero mean strain-rate components are \(\langle S_{12}\rangle=\langle S_{21}\rangle=\tfrac12\,d\langle U_1\rangle/dy\). Schmitt (2007) measures how far a database is from (9.27) by the cosine of the angle between the two tensors, with the inner product \(A:B=A_{ij}B_{ij}\) and the norm \(\|A\|=(A:A)^{1/2}\) (HAL preprint p. 6). In this book's notation, and writing \(\gamma_{aS}\) for the indicator that Schmitt writes \(\rho_{RS}\) (\(\rho\) is the density here),

\[ \gamma_{aS}\equiv\frac{\lvert a_{ij}\langle S_{ij}\rangle\rvert}{\|a\|\,\|\langle S\rangle\|},\qquad 0\le\gamma_{aS}\le1. \tag{9.30} \]

The value is 1 if and only if \(a_{ij}\) is a scalar multiple of \(\langle S_{ij}\rangle\), which is what (9.27) asserts; the absolute value in (9.30) makes the indicator blind to the sign of that multiple, so it tests alignment, not the sign of \(\nu_T\). Schmitt treats values above 0.86, an angle below \(\pi/6\), as approximate alignment (p. 6). For the channel, with the Reynolds stress alone, \(\gamma_{aS}=\sqrt2\,|\langle u_1u_2\rangle|/\|a\|\), independent of the size of the gradient; with the viscous stress included, \(a_{12}\) is replaced by \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle-\nu\,d\langle U_1\rangle/dy\), which in wall units is \(-(1-y/d)\) by (9.13). Schmitt applies (9.30) to simulations of plane Couette flow (friction Reynolds number 82), channel flow (180 and 590), a boundary layer and an annular pipe, with the viscous stress either included or excluded (pp. 6–7). With the viscous stress included, alignment is good very close to the wall, where viscosity dominates, and poor for \(3\lt y^+\lt70\), with a minimum at \(y^+=10.5\pm0.5\) in all the databases (pp. 7–8). In the annular pipe he finds a position where the mean strain rate vanishes while \(a_{ij}\) does not, which no relation of the form (9.27) can represent (p. 8).

The same computation on the running case gives consistent results with one difference. With the Reynolds stress alone, \(\gamma_{aS}\) rises from 0.013 at \(y^+=1\) to 0.52 at \(y/d=0.1\) and reaches at most 0.59 (near \(y/d=0.5\)). With the viscous stress included it is 0.996 at \(y^+=1\), falls below 0.86 at \(y^+=2.9\) and does not exceed 0.73 beyond that point, with a near-wall minimum of 0.23 at \(y^+=14\), not at 10.5. The same computation on the Lee–Moser \(\mathrm{Re}_\tau=543\) file, close to Schmitt's channel at 590, gives the minimum at \(y^+=13.8\) (0.238), and on the \(\mathrm{Re}_\tau=5186\) file at \(y^+=14.8\) (0.209), so a difference in Reynolds number cannot explain the shift from 10.5 to 14: over a factor of 9.5 in \(\mathrm{Re}_\tau\) the minimum moves by about 1 in \(y^+\); this chapter has not traced the difference further, which may lie in the databases or in the derivative estimate. In the running case the minimum lies one grid point from the peak of the streamwise normal stress (\(\langle u_1^2\rangle^+=8.10\) at \(y^+=15.2\)); by (9.30) it is the point where the diagonal of \(a_{ij}\) is largest relative to the total shear stress. The running case also contains Schmitt's annular-pipe situation: on the centreplane \(\langle S_{ij}\rangle=0\) by symmetry, so (9.27) requires \(a_{ij}=0\), while the data give \(b_{11}=0.13\) at the point nearest it, where \(\gamma_{aS}=0.018\).

Schmitt's conclusion is that the hypothesis is "almost never verified" in the databases tested (abstract, p. 1), and that turbulent-viscosity models nevertheless predict mean fields of simple flows, because there the shear stress dominates the mean equations, while their predictions of second-order moments are not satisfactory (p. 10). The running case illustrates both halves: (9.29) with the measured \(\nu_T\) reproduces the mean velocity exactly, by construction, and (9.27) predicts every normal stress wrongly.

Executable check: the channel data against the equations and the hypothesis

The program below copies nine rows of the Lee–Moser \(\mathrm{Re}_\tau=1000\) data files into the listing, rounded to four significant digits, so that it reads no file: \(y/d\), \(y^+\), \(dU^+/dy^+\) and the four non-zero Reynolds stresses in wall units. For each row it builds the Reynolds-stress matrix (9.23), checks realizability through the eigenvalues (9.15), computes \(k\), \(a_{ij}\) and \(b_{ij}\) by (9.14) and (9.17), and asserts three statements that a sign or index error would break: the eigenvalues of \(b_{ij}\) lie in \([-\tfrac13,\tfrac23]\), the trace of \(b_{ij}\) is zero, and the total stress (9.13) equals \(1-y/d\) to within \(2\times10^{-3}\), the size of the rounding. It prints the share of the total stress carried by the Reynolds stress, \(\nu_T/\nu\) from (9.29), the diagonal of \(b_{ij}\) that (9.27) predicts to be zero, and the alignment indicator (9.30) without and with the viscous stress (columns g_t and g_tot; the viscous part of the deviatoric stress in wall units is \(-2\langle S_{ij}\rangle^+\)). It then evaluates the laminar comparison and the dimensional running case, and applies the realizability function to the two matrices of the practice problem in Exercise 8.

def realizable(r, tol=1e-12):
    """Eigenvalues of a Reynolds-stress matrix; ValueError if one < 0."""
    r = np.asarray(r, dtype=float)
    if not np.allclose(r, r.T):
        raise ValueError("Reynolds-stress matrix is not symmetric")
    lam = np.linalg.eigvalsh(r)
    if lam[0] < -tol:
        raise ValueError(f"not positive semi-definite: {lam[0]:.3f}")
    return lam


def anisotropy(r):
    """Return k, a_ij and b_ij of a Reynolds-stress matrix."""
    k = 0.5*np.trace(r)
    a = r - 2/3*k*np.eye(3)
    return k, a, a/(2*k)


def alignment(a, s):
    """Cosine of the angle between two symmetric tensors, |a:s|/|a||s|."""
    return abs(np.sum(a*s))/(np.linalg.norm(a)*np.linalg.norm(s))

What the equations establish and what they do not

The Reynolds equations (9.7), the mean continuity equation (9.3), the mean-pressure equation (9.8) and the mean scalar equation (9.24) are exact consequences of the Navier–Stokes and scalar equations under three conditions: incompressible flow, constant density and viscosity, and an average that is linear and commutes with derivatives. The expectation satisfies the last condition; a time average replaces it only for a stationary flow and under the ergodic assumption (Exercise 2 shows what fails otherwise). With variable density the decomposition (9.1) leaves additional correlations such as \(\langle\rho'u_i\rangle\) (Exercise 7). The channel results (9.11)–(9.13) need, in addition, statistical stationarity and homogeneity in \(x_1\) and \(x_3\), which the simulation imposes and a laboratory channel approaches only far from its inlet and side walls.

The exact results do not determine the mean flow, because the Reynolds stresses and the scalar flux are unknown: four equations for ten unknowns in general, one for two in the channel. The properties of the stress tensor (symmetry, positive semi-definiteness, the bounds (9.19), the reflection symmetries (9.23), and the Corrsin–Kistler identity for irrotational fluctuations) restrict the unknowns without supplying them. Positive semi-definiteness and (9.19) hold for every flow; the symmetry results hold only for flows with the stated symmetries; (9.21) holds only where the fluctuations are irrotational.

The gradient-diffusion and turbulent-viscosity hypotheses (9.25) and (9.27) are models. Each claims alignment between a turbulent flux and a mean gradient through one non-negative scalar, and each fails in documented cases: the normal stresses of the channel, where (9.27) predicts isotropy; the centreplane of the channel and the annular pipe, where the mean strain rate vanishes and the anisotropy does not; and the counter-gradient scalar flux in a jet in crossflow, which no non-negative \(\Gamma_T\) can represent. The tests in this chapter are a priori tests on one flow: they compare the hypothesis with exact simulation data. They do not measure how well a complete model, with a rule for \(\nu_T\), predicts a mean flow; that is the subject of the modelling chapters. The value \(\mathrm{Pr}_T\approx1\) and the attribution of the hypothesis to Boussinesq are reported from the lecture and from Schmitt (2007); Boussinesq's memoir was not read for this chapter.

Exercise 1 (derive). (a) Show that \(\langle U_iU_jU_k\rangle=\langle U_i\rangle\langle U_j\rangle\langle U_k\rangle+\langle U_i\rangle\langle u_ju_k\rangle+\langle U_j\rangle\langle u_iu_k\rangle+\langle U_k\rangle\langle u_iu_j\rangle+\langle u_iu_ju_k\rangle\), and state which rule of (9.2) removes each of the terms that do not appear. (b) The mean flux of kinetic energy per unit mass in direction \(j\) is \(\langle\tfrac12U_iU_iU_j\rangle\). Which of its parts is a new unknown, beyond \(\langle\mathbf U\rangle\) and the Reynolds stresses? What does this imply for an attempt to close the Reynolds equations by writing equations for second moments?

Solution

(a) Expand \((\langle U_i\rangle+u_i)(\langle U_j\rangle+u_j)(\langle U_k\rangle+u_k)\) into eight terms. The term with three means is non-random and averages to itself. The three terms with two means and one fluctuation, such as \(\langle U_i\rangle\langle U_j\rangle u_k\), average to the product of the means times \(\langle u_k\rangle=0\), by the second rule of (9.2) (a non-random factor leaves the average) and \(\langle\mathbf u\rangle=0\). The three terms with one mean and two fluctuations average to \(\langle U_i\rangle\langle u_ju_k\rangle\) and its two permutations by the same rule. The term with three fluctuations averages to \(\langle u_iu_ju_k\rangle\), which no rule reduces.

(b) With \(i=k\) summed and the factor \(\tfrac12\): \(\langle\tfrac12U_iU_iU_j\rangle=\tfrac12\langle U_i\rangle\langle U_i\rangle\langle U_j\rangle+\langle U_i\rangle\langle u_iu_j\rangle+k\langle U_j\rangle+\tfrac12\langle u_iu_iu_j\rangle\), using \(\langle u_iu_i\rangle=2k\). The first three parts contain only the mean velocity and the Reynolds stresses. The last, the triple correlation \(\tfrac12\langle u_iu_iu_j\rangle\), is new. Any equation for second moments obtained by averaging the Navier–Stokes equations multiplied by velocities contains such averages of three velocities from the quadratic advection term, and therefore third moments. Writing equations for the Reynolds stresses trades six unknowns for new unknowns of higher order; it does not close the system.

Exercise 2 (check an assumption). A channel is started from rest at \(t=0\). At one point the velocity in each realization is \(U(t)=U_\infty(1-e^{-t/\tau_s})+u(t)\), where \(u\) is a zero-mean fluctuation and \(\tau_s\) a spin-up time. An experimenter has a single record from \(t=0\) to \(T=5\tau_s\) and defines the "mean" as the time average \(\langle U\rangle_T=T^{-1}\int_0^TU\,dt\) and the fluctuation as \(U-\langle U\rangle_T\). (a) Compute the expected value of \(\langle U\rangle_T\) in units of \(U_\infty\). (b) What is the ensemble mean of the experimenter's fluctuation at \(t=0\) and at \(t=T\)? (c) Which property used in deriving (9.7) fails, and which term of (9.7) does the time average lose?

Solution

(a) The fluctuation averages to zero in expectation, so \(\langle\langle U\rangle_T\rangle=U_\infty[1-(\tau_s/T)(1-e^{-T/\tau_s})]=U_\infty[1-0.2\times(1-e^{-5})]=U_\infty(1-0.2\times0.99326)=0.801\,U_\infty\).

(b) The ensemble mean of \(U(t)-\langle U\rangle_T\) is \(U_\infty(1-e^{-t/\tau_s})-0.801\,U_\infty\): \(-0.801\,U_\infty\) at \(t=0\) and \(U_\infty(1-e^{-5})-0.801U_\infty=+0.192\,U_\infty\) at \(t=T\). The experimenter's fluctuation has a time average of zero by construction, but its ensemble mean is not zero at any given time, which violates \(\langle\mathbf u\rangle=0\), the property that removed the cross terms in (9.5).

(c) The time average is a single number for the record, so it has no time derivative, and the term \(\partial\langle U_j\rangle/\partial t\) of the mean substantial derivative (9.6) disappears; the spin-up is moved into the "fluctuation" and from there into the apparent Reynolds stresses, which acquire a contribution from the mean evolution. The time average replaces the expectation only for a statistically stationary flow, with the ergodic assumption and a record long compared with the integral time scale (chapter 8). For a non-stationary flow, an ensemble of runs, or a phase average for a periodic flow, is needed.

Exercise 3 (compute). For the running case (\(d=2\ \mathrm{cm}\), \(\nu=1.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), \(\rho=1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\), \(\mathrm{Re}_\tau=1000\)), use the rows of the program in section "Executable check". (a) Compute the distance from the wall in millimetres of the rows at \(y^+=5.0\) and \(y^+=98.8\), and the viscous and Reynolds shear stresses there in pascals. (b) At \(y^+=98.8\), what velocity gradient would a laminar flow need to carry the same total stress? Compare it with the actual gradient. (c) What is the mean pressure drop over a 1 m length of the channel?

Solution

(a) The viscous length is \(\nu/u_\tau=1.0\times10^{-6}/0.0500=2.0\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm m\), so \(y^+=5.0\) is at 0.10 mm and \(y^+=98.8\) at 1.98 mm. The stress unit is \(\rho u_\tau^2=2.50\ \mathrm{Pa}\). At \(y^+=5.0\): viscous \(0.8786\times2.50=2.20\ \mathrm{Pa}\), Reynolds \(0.1164\times2.50=0.29\ \mathrm{Pa}\); total \(2.49\ \mathrm{Pa}\), equal to \(\tau_w(1-0.005)\). At \(y^+=98.8\): viscous \(0.024\times2.50=0.060\ \mathrm{Pa}\), Reynolds \(0.8772\times2.50=2.19\ \mathrm{Pa}\); total \(2.25\ \mathrm{Pa}=\tau_w(1-0.0987)\).

(b) A laminar flow would need \(\mu\,d\langle U_1\rangle/dy=2.25\ \mathrm{Pa}\), so \(d\langle U_1\rangle/dy=2.25/10^{-3}=2.25\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\). The actual gradient is \(dU^+/dy^+\times u_\tau^2/\nu=0.024\times(0.05)^2/10^{-6}=60\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), 37.5 times smaller; the ratio is \(1+\nu_T/\nu=1+36.6\), as (9.29) requires. The turbulent flow carries the same stress with a far smaller gradient, which is why its mean profile is flat in the core and its bulk speed is 16.7 times smaller than the laminar one at the same pressure gradient.

(c) By (9.13), \(-dp_w/dx_1=\tau_w/d=2.50/0.02=125\ \mathrm{Pa/m}\), so the drop is 125 Pa over 1 m. The mean pressure inside the flow differs from \(p_w\) by \(\rho\langle u_2^2\rangle\) (9.12), which is the same at every \(x_1\) and does not change the drop.

Exercise 4 (derive). For the fully developed channel, evaluate both source terms of the mean-pressure equation (9.8). Show that (9.8) reduces to \(\nabla^2\langle p\rangle=-\rho\,d^2\langle u_2^2\rangle/dx_2^2\), and that this agrees with the solution (9.12) of the \(x_2\) momentum equation. Which part of \(\langle p\rangle\) does (9.8) not determine, and what fixes it?

Solution

The mean velocity gradient has the single component \(\partial\langle U_1\rangle/\partial x_2\). In the first source, \((\partial\langle U_i\rangle/\partial x_j)(\partial\langle U_j\rangle/\partial x_i)\), a non-zero term needs both \(\partial\langle U_i\rangle/\partial x_j\) and \(\partial\langle U_j\rangle/\partial x_i\) non-zero, that is \((i,j)=(1,2)\) and \((i,j)=(2,1)\) at once, which is impossible; the term is zero. In the second, \(\partial^2\langle u_iu_j\rangle/\partial x_i\partial x_j\), the stresses depend on \(x_2\) only, so only \(i=j=2\) survives: \(d^2\langle u_2^2\rangle/dx_2^2\). Hence \(-\rho^{-1}\nabla^2\langle p\rangle=d^2\langle u_2^2\rangle/dx_2^2\).

From (9.12), \(\langle p\rangle=p_w(x_1)-\rho\langle u_2^2\rangle(x_2)\), with \(dp_w/dx_1=-\tau_w/d\) constant, so \(p_w\) is linear in \(x_1\) and \(\partial^2p_w/\partial x_1^2=0\). Then \(\nabla^2\langle p\rangle=-\rho\,d^2\langle u_2^2\rangle/dx_2^2\), the same equation. The Poisson equation does not determine the part of \(\langle p\rangle\) whose Laplacian is zero, here the linear function \(p_w(x_1)\): its slope is fixed by the boundary conditions, in the channel by the condition that the flow is driven at the given wall stress, as in (9.13).

Exercise 5 (counterexample). A turbulent flow is subjected to the mean plane strain \(\langle U_1\rangle=sx_1\), \(\langle U_2\rangle=-sx_2\), \(\langle U_3\rangle=0\), with \(s\gt0\). (a) Use (9.27) to write the three normal stresses in terms of \(k\), \(\nu_T\) and \(s\). (b) Show that the hypothesis predicts a negative normal stress when \(\nu_Ts/k\gt\tfrac13\). (c) Evaluate the case \(k=0.1\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\), \(\nu_T=0.01\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), \(s=5\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), and state what the result implies for a model built on (9.27).

Solution

(a) \(\langle S_{11}\rangle=s\), \(\langle S_{22}\rangle=-s\), all other components zero, and the trace is zero as continuity requires. By (9.27), \(\langle u_\alpha^2\rangle=\tfrac23k-2\nu_T\langle S_{\alpha\alpha}\rangle\): \(\langle u_1^2\rangle=\tfrac23k-2\nu_Ts\), \(\langle u_2^2\rangle=\tfrac23k+2\nu_Ts\), \(\langle u_3^2\rangle=\tfrac23k\).

(b) \(\langle u_1^2\rangle\lt0\) exactly when \(2\nu_Ts\gt\tfrac23k\), that is \(\nu_Ts/k\gt\tfrac13\). Equivalently \(b_{11}=-\nu_Ts/k\lt-\tfrac13\), outside the realizability bound (9.19).

(c) \(\nu_Ts/k=0.01\times5/0.1=0.5\gt\tfrac13\). Then \(\langle u_1^2\rangle=0.0667-0.1=-0.033\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\), a negative variance, which no random field can have. The hypothesis contains nothing that prevents this: for a large enough strain rate relative to \(k/\nu_T\), (9.27) produces an unrealizable stress tensor. A model built on it must impose a separate limit, for example \(\nu_T\le k/(3s)\) in this flow, which is a property that the hypothesis itself does not supply.

Exercise 6 (judge a design). A team fits \(\Gamma_T=-\langle u_2\phi'\rangle/(\partial\langle\phi\rangle/\partial x_2)\) to simulation data of a jet in crossflow and finds \(\Gamma_T=-2\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) in a thin region near the wall, where the molecular diffusivity is \(\Gamma=2\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). They propose to use the fitted field in the mean scalar equation (9.26). (a) Find the growth rate of a perturbation \(e^{i\kappa x_2}\) of the mean scalar in that region, ignoring advection, for wavelengths of 10 mm and 1 mm. (b) What happens to a computation as the grid is refined? (c) Recommend a course of action.

Solution

(a) \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}=\Gamma+\Gamma_T=-1.8\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). With \(\partial\langle\phi\rangle/\partial t=\Gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}\,\partial^2\langle\phi\rangle/\partial x_2^2\), the amplitude of \(e^{i\kappa x_2}\) varies as \(e^{-\Gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}\kappa^2t}\), a growth rate of \(|\Gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}|\kappa^2\). For 10 mm, \(\kappa=628\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\) and the rate is \(1.8\times10^{-4}\times3.95\times10^{5}=71\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\); for 1 mm, \(\kappa=6283\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\) and the rate is \(7.1\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\).

(b) The rate grows as \(\kappa^2\) without bound, and a grid of spacing \(\Delta x\) represents wavenumbers up to about \(\pi/\Delta x\). Each refinement admits faster-growing perturbations, so the computed solution does not converge; it is dominated by amplified round-off at the grid scale. The backward diffusion equation is ill-posed.

(c) The data are probably right: Milani, Ling and Eaton (2020) report counter-gradient flux of this kind near the wall downstream of injection and attribute it to non-local effects (pp. 8–9). The fault is in the model form, which cannot represent it. Two defensible choices: clip \(\Gamma_T\) at zero (or \(\Gamma_{\mathrm{eff}}\) at a small positive value) and accept a known local error in the mean scalar, stating where it is; or use a model with more freedom, such as a tensor diffusivity constructed to keep the equation stable, which is the route Milani and co-authors take (p. 1). Using the negative field as fitted is not defensible.

Exercise 7 (derive; adapted from the course practice problems, L57 10:07). Write the instantaneous pressure, density and temperature of a gas as \(p=\langle p\rangle+p'\), \(\rho=\langle\rho\rangle+\rho'\) and \(\theta=\langle\theta\rangle+\theta'\). (The slide writes \(P\), \(\bar\rho\), \(\bar T\) for the means and calls them time averages; this book writes \(\theta\) for temperature and \(\langle\cdot\rangle\) for the average.) (a) Average the ideal-gas equation \(p=\rho R\theta\), with \(R\) the constant gas constant (not an autocovariance), and find the mean pressure. (b) If the r.m.s. density and temperature fluctuations are 1 % of their means and their correlation coefficient is \(-0.5\), how large is the correction term relative to \(\langle\rho\rangle R\langle\theta\rangle\)? (c) In the same flow, which correlation appears when the momentum density \(\rho U_i\) is averaged, and why does the rest of this chapter not contain it?

Solution

(a) Substituting, \(\langle p\rangle+p'=R(\langle\rho\rangle\langle\theta\rangle+\langle\rho\rangle\theta'+\rho'\langle\theta\rangle+\rho'\theta')\). Averaging, with \(\langle p'\rangle=\langle\rho'\rangle=\langle\theta'\rangle=0\) and the means non-random (9.2), the two terms with a single fluctuation vanish and

\[ \langle p\rangle=R\bigl(\langle\rho\rangle\langle\theta\rangle+\langle\rho'\theta'\rangle\bigr). \]

The mean pressure is not the equation of state evaluated at the mean density and mean temperature; the correlation of the density and temperature fluctuations adds to it, as \(\langle u_iu_j\rangle\) adds to \(\langle U_i\rangle\langle U_j\rangle\) in (9.5).

(b) With \(\sigma_\rho\) and \(\sigma_\theta\) the r.m.s. fluctuations, the definition of the correlation coefficient (chapter 7) gives \(\langle\rho'\theta'\rangle=-0.5\,\sigma_\rho\sigma_\theta\), so \(\langle\rho'\theta'\rangle/(\langle\rho\rangle\langle\theta\rangle)=-0.5\times0.01\times0.01=-5\times10^{-5}\). The correction is second order in the fluctuation amplitudes: here 0.005 % of the mean pressure.

(c) \(\langle\rho U_i\rangle=\langle\rho\rangle\langle U_i\rangle+\langle\rho'u_i\rangle\): a density–velocity correlation appears in the mean mass flux and, through it, in every averaged conservation law. This chapter assumes constant density, so \(\rho'=0\) and the correlation is absent; a variable-density flow needs either the extra correlations or a density-weighted average, which this book does not cover.

Exercise 8 (check an assumption; adapted from the course practice problems, L59 10:00). Each of the following is stated to be incorrect. Explain why. (a) \(\langle u_iu_j\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.1&0\\0.1&0.3&0.1\\0&0.1&-0.1\end{pmatrix}\). (b) \(b_{ij}=\dfrac{\langle u_iu_j\rangle}{\langle u_\ell u_\ell\rangle}-\dfrac13\delta_{ij}=\begin{pmatrix}-0.4&0&0.1\\0&0.2&0\\0.1&0&0.2\end{pmatrix}\). (c) Construct a symmetric matrix with positive diagonal entries that is still not a possible Reynolds-stress tensor, and show which condition it violates.

Solution

(a) The entry \(\langle u_3^2\rangle=-0.1\) is negative, which violates (9.16): a variance cannot be negative. The \(2\times2\) block of components 2 and 3 also fails, since \(0.3\times(-0.1)-0.1^2=-0.04\lt0\), and the smallest eigenvalue is \(-0.124\) (program output).

(b) The trace is \(-0.4+0.2+0.2=0\), as (9.17) requires, and the off-diagonal entries satisfy \(|b_{13}|=0.1\le\tfrac12\). The diagonal entry \(b_{11}=-0.4\) is below \(-\tfrac13\), which violates (9.19): it would require \(\langle u_1^2\rangle/\langle u_\ell u_\ell\rangle=-0.4+\tfrac13=-0.067\lt0\). Equivalently, \(b_{ij}+\tfrac13\delta_{ij}\) has the eigenvalue \(-0.083\) (program output).

(c) Take \(\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.6&0\\0.6&0.3&0\\0&0&0.2\end{pmatrix}\). Every diagonal entry is positive, but \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle^2=0.36\gt\langle u_1^2\rangle\langle u_2^2\rangle=0.15\), which violates the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in (9.16); the correlation coefficient would be \(0.6/\sqrt{0.15}=1.55\gt1\). The \(2\times2\) block has determinant \(0.15-0.36=-0.21\lt0\), so the matrix has a negative eigenvalue. A check of the diagonal alone, which is the check the problem's first part needs, is not sufficient in general; the full condition is that every eigenvalue is non-negative.
