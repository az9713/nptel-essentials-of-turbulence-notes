The energy spectrum: how energy and dissipation are distributed over wavenumber, and what the second-order correlations add

The inertial-range law \(E(\kappa)=C\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3}\) is the most quoted result about the energy spectrum, and it is natural to read it as a description of where the kinetic energy and the dissipation of a turbulent flow lie. It describes less than that. Take the air jet of chapters 6–8 at its downstream station, with the lecture's nominal range limits and the unit constant in \(\varepsilon\sim u_0^3/\ell_0\) (chapter 5, (5.16)). A \(-5/3\) law with \(C=1.5\) that holds exactly between the lecture's demarcation wavenumbers contains 7.6 % of the kinetic energy and 9.0 % of the dissipation rate. For the atmospheric boundary layer of chapter 5 the shares are 13.2 % and 11.1 %, and for any flow they cannot exceed 13.3 % and 11.1 % under these assumptions. The rest of both quantities lies outside the range that the second similarity hypothesis describes: the energy mostly at larger scales, the dissipation mostly at smaller ones. A probe or a simulation grid that resolves only the inertial range therefore misses most of the dissipation, and the power law alone cannot say how much. This chapter derives the integral definitions of the energy and the dissipation spectrum, obtains the inertial-range form from dimensional analysis and the second similarity hypothesis, examines what the lecture's convergence argument for the exponent does and does not show, and computes the shares above. It then takes the second-order statistics that the hypotheses constrain besides the spectrum, the structure function and the two-point correlation of isotropic turbulence, and from them the Taylor microscales, the Taylor-scale Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) and its exact relations to the large-scale and Kolmogorov scales: for the jet, \(\lambda_g=3.3\) mm, \(\lambda_g/\eta=42\) and \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=453\).

The objects of the chapter and the running case

The formal model of the chapter is a statistically homogeneous velocity fluctuation field \(\mathbf u(\mathbf x,t)=\mathbf U-\langle\mathbf U\rangle\) of an incompressible flow of constant kinematic viscosity \(\nu\) (units \(\mathrm{m^2/s}\)), where \(\mathbf U\) is the velocity and \(\langle\cdot\rangle\) the mean of chapter 7. Homogeneity is defined in chapter 8, (8.24), and is assumed throughout, because the spectrum and the two-point correlation are defined for homogeneous fields. Isotropy (chapter 8, section "Isotropy") is assumed only where a statement says so. The fields used are the turbulent kinetic energy per unit mass \(k=\tfrac12\langle u_iu_i\rangle\) (summation over repeated indices), the dissipation rate \(\varepsilon=2\nu\langle s_{ij}s_{ij}\rangle\) of chapter 5, (5.13), with \(s_{ij}=\tfrac12(\partial u_i/\partial x_j+\partial u_j/\partial x_i)\) the rate of strain of the fluctuation, the root-mean-square velocity \(u'\equiv(2k/3)^{1/2}\), which equals the root-mean-square of each velocity component when the three variances are equal, (8.25), and the Kolmogorov scales \(\eta=(\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}\), \(u_\eta=(\varepsilon\nu)^{1/4}\) and \(\tau_\eta=(\nu/\varepsilon)^{1/2}\) of chapter 5, (5.14). (The slides write \(\tau\) and \(v\) for the last two; chapter 5 made the same change. In the simulation chapters \(u'\) will also denote a residual velocity; those chapters state the change.) The energy-spectrum function \(E(\kappa)\) is the one defined in chapter 8 by (8.32), with \(k=\int_0^\infty E\,d\kappa\) by (8.33); its argument is always written, because \(E(\omega)\) of chapter 8 is the frequency spectrum, a different object. A wavenumber \(\kappa\) (units \(\mathrm{rad/m}\)) is associated with an eddy size \(\ell\) by the slide's relation

\[ \kappa=\frac{2\pi}{\ell}. \tag{11.1} \]

The slide uses it to place the demarcation lengths \(\ell_{EI}\) and \(\ell_{DI}\) on the wavenumber axis: \(\kappa_{EI}\equiv2\pi/\ell_{EI}\) and \(\kappa_{DI}\equiv2\pi/\ell_{DI}\). The lecture's nominal values are \(\ell_{EI}=\ell_0/6\) and \(\ell_{DI}=60\eta\), where \(\ell_0\) is the size of the energy-containing eddies, \(u_0\) their velocity and \(\mathrm{Re}_0\equiv u_0\ell_0/\nu\) their Reynolds number. The lecture states both factors as assumed values (L29, about 60 to 110 s: "we assumed it was 60 eta") and gives no justification in these two lectures; this chapter gives none either, so every number below that uses them inherits them. An eddy of size \(\ell\) is not a Fourier mode of wavelength \(\ell\): (11.1) is a convention that associates a wavenumber band with a size band, and the section "The measured spectrum" returns to what the association hides. The three kinds of range that the slide draws are the energy-containing range, \(\ell\gt\ell_{EI}\) (\(\kappa\lt\kappa_{EI}\)), the inertial subrange, \(\ell_{EI}\gt\ell\gt\ell_{DI}\) (\(\kappa_{EI}\lt\kappa\lt\kappa_{DI}\)), and the dissipation range, \(\ell\lt\ell_{DI}\) (\(\kappa\gt\kappa_{DI}\)); the universal equilibrium range is \(\kappa\gt\kappa_{EI}\), inertial and dissipation ranges together.

The running case. The running case is the round air jet of chapters 6–8, with the values chosen in chapter 6: \(u_0=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(\ell_0=0.1\ \mathrm m\), \(\nu=1.4607\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), so \(\mathrm{Re}_0=1.37\times10^4\). The contrast case is the neutral atmospheric boundary layer of chapter 5, with \(u_0=0.3\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(\ell_0=10^3\ \mathrm m\) and \(\nu=1.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), so \(\mathrm{Re}_0=2\times10^7\). For both flows the chapter takes \(\varepsilon=u_0^3/\ell_0\) (the estimate of chapter 5, (5.16), with unit constant), which gives \(\varepsilon=80\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\) and \(\eta=79\ \mu\mathrm m\) for the jet and \(2.7\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\) and 3.3 mm for the layer, and it takes \(u'=u_0\), so that \(k=\tfrac32u_0^2\). The demarcation wavenumbers are \(\kappa_{EI}=377\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\) and \(\kappa_{DI}=1.33\times10^3\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\) for the jet, and 0.0377 and 31.3 \(\mathrm{m^{-1}}\) for the layer. The jet is neither homogeneous nor known to be isotropic (chapters 7 and 8); the numbers of this chapter are what the formulas give if the station behaved as homogeneous isotropic turbulence with these scales. They illustrate the formulas and are not predictions for a particular jet. The question for both flows is where in wavenumber the energy and the dissipation lie, and which of the statements the lecture makes about the spectrum rest on a hypothesis, on a dimensional argument or on a measurement.

Energy and dissipation in a band of wavenumbers L29 10:32

The slide defines the energy and the dissipation rate contained in the modes of wavenumber \(\kappa_a\le\kappa\le\kappa_b\):

\[ k_{(\kappa_a,\kappa_b)}=\int_{\kappa_a}^{\kappa_b}E(\kappa)\,d\kappa, \tag{11.2} \] \[ \varepsilon_{(\kappa_a,\kappa_b)}=\int_{\kappa_a}^{\kappa_b}2\nu\kappa^2E(\kappa)\,d\kappa. \tag{11.3} \]

Equation (11.2) is the band form of chapter 8, (8.33). The slide gives (11.3) without derivation, and the lecture says that its derivation "is not important right now". The derivation shows which assumptions the formula needs, and the answer matters later: it needs homogeneity and incompressibility, and it does not need isotropy.

Claim. For a statistically homogeneous, incompressible, constant-viscosity fluctuation field, the dissipation rate is \(\varepsilon=2\nu\int_0^\infty\kappa^2E(\kappa)\,d\kappa\).

Proof. Expand the definition (5.13): \(\langle s_{ij}s_{ij}\rangle=\tfrac12\langle\partial_ju_i\,\partial_ju_i\rangle+\tfrac12\langle\partial_ju_i\,\partial_iu_j\rangle\), where \(\partial_j\) is \(\partial/\partial x_j\). The second average vanishes. By the product rule \(\partial_ju_i\,\partial_iu_j=\partial_j(u_i\,\partial_iu_j)-u_i\,\partial_i(\partial_ju_j)\); the last term is zero by continuity, \(\partial_ju_j=0\), and the average of the divergence \(\partial_j(u_i\,\partial_iu_j)\) is zero because the mean of a homogeneous field does not depend on position. Hence

\[ \varepsilon=\nu\langle\partial_ju_i\,\partial_ju_i\rangle. \tag{11.4} \]

Next express the right side through the two-point correlation \(R_{ii}(\mathbf r)=\langle u_i(\mathbf x)u_i(\mathbf x+\mathbf r)\rangle\) of chapter 8, (8.26). Differentiating twice with respect to \(\mathbf r\) and setting \(\mathbf r=0\) gives \(\nabla_r^2R_{ii}(0)=\langle u_i\,\partial_j\partial_ju_i\rangle=-\langle\partial_ju_i\,\partial_ju_i\rangle\), the second step by the same product rule and homogeneity. Differentiating the Fourier representation (8.30) twice gives \(\nabla_r^2R_{ii}(0)=-\iiint|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2\Phi_{ii}\,d\boldsymbol\kappa\). The definition (8.32) states that for any function \(h\) of the magnitude, \(\iiint h(|\boldsymbol\kappa|)\tfrac12\Phi_{ii}\,d\boldsymbol\kappa=\int_0^\infty h(\kappa)E(\kappa)\,d\kappa\), because the delta function in (8.32) selects the shell \(|\boldsymbol\kappa|=\kappa\); with \(h=\kappa^2\) the integral of \(|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2\Phi_{ii}\) is \(2\int_0^\infty\kappa^2E\,d\kappa\). Substituting in (11.4) gives the claim. \(\blacksquare\)

Isotropy would add a relation between \(\varepsilon\) and a single velocity derivative (the section "The Taylor microscales and the dissipation rate"); (11.3) does not need it. The units agree: \(\nu\kappa^2E\,d\kappa\) has \(\mathrm{m^2s^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm{m^{-2}}\cdot\mathrm{m^3s^{-2}}\cdot\mathrm{m^{-1}}=\mathrm{m^2s^{-3}}\). The weight \(2\nu\kappa^2\) in (11.3) is the content of the slide's remark that dissipation is concentrated at high wavenumbers: for equal \(E(\kappa)\), a mode of ten times the wavenumber dissipates 100 times as fast. The function \(D(\kappa)\equiv2\nu\kappa^2E(\kappa)\) is the dissipation spectrum of the book's notation, and \(\int_0^\infty D\,d\kappa=\varepsilon\). Total energy and total dissipation are the limits \(k_{(0,\infty)}=k\) and \(\varepsilon_{(0,\infty)}=\varepsilon\).

The transfer rate through the inertial subrange L29 2:45

The lecture restates, before it turns to the spectrum, the result of the preceding chapter, "The energy cascade and Kolmogorov's hypotheses". Let \(\mathcal T(\ell)\) be the rate at which energy per unit mass is transferred from eddies larger than \(\ell\) to eddies smaller than \(\ell\). The slide argues that if the transfer is accomplished mainly by eddies of size comparable to \(\ell\), then \(\mathcal T(\ell)\) is of the order \(u(\ell)^2/\tau(\ell)\), where \(u(\ell)\) and \(\tau(\ell)=\ell/u(\ell)\) are the velocity and time scale of an eddy of size \(\ell\); that the inertial-range forms \(u(\ell)=(\varepsilon\ell)^{1/3}\) and \(\tau(\ell)=(\ell^2/\varepsilon)^{1/3}\) make \(u(\ell)^2/\tau(\ell)=\varepsilon\); and so that \(\mathcal T(\ell)=\varepsilon\). In symbols, \(\mathcal T_{EI}\equiv\mathcal T(\ell_{EI})=\mathcal T(\ell)=\mathcal T_{DI}\equiv\mathcal T(\ell_{DI})=\varepsilon\) for \(\ell_{EI}\gt\ell\gt\ell_{DI}\): the rate at which the energy-containing range feeds the inertial subrange equals the rate at which energy passes every size in it and the rate at which it enters the dissipation range, and that rate is the dissipation rate. The step from "of the order of" to "equal to" is an estimate that the lecture does not qualify. The preceding chapter treats the estimate and its conditions. Here one consequence matters, because it fixes what the wavenumber integrals of the next sections can mean.

A flux balance. Consider statistically stationary, homogeneous turbulence in which energy is supplied only at wavenumbers below some \(\kappa\) in the inertial subrange, and let \(\mathcal T(\kappa)\) denote the rate at which energy is transferred from modes of wavenumber below \(\kappa\) to modes above it (the wavenumber counterpart of \(\mathcal T(\ell)\); the spectral transfer function that defines it is the subject of a later chapter, "Reynolds-number effects and the energy-spectrum balance"). The modes above \(\kappa\) gain energy at the rate \(\mathcal T(\kappa)\) and lose it at the rate \(\varepsilon_{(\kappa,\infty)}\). In a stationary state the two are equal, so by (11.3)

\[ \mathcal T(\kappa)=\varepsilon_{(\kappa,\infty)}=\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{(0,\kappa)}. \tag{11.5} \]

The transfer rate equals \(\varepsilon\) only to the extent that the dissipation below \(\kappa\) is negligible; the section "Where the energy and the dissipation lie" computes that dissipation for the running case and finds that it is not always negligible at the upper end of the inertial subrange. Equation (11.5) is a teaching construction. It assumes stationarity and supply below \(\kappa\); the decaying turbulence of a grid, and the jet, do not satisfy the first assumption exactly.

Dimensional analysis fixes the form of the spectrum L29 12:51

The slide applies the two similarity hypotheses to the spectrum. From the first hypothesis, in the universal equilibrium range \(\kappa\gt\kappa_{EI}\) the spectrum is a universal function of \(\varepsilon\) and \(\nu\). From the second, in the inertial subrange \(\kappa_{EI}\lt\kappa\lt\kappa_{DI}\) it is \(E(\kappa)=C\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3}\) with \(C\) a universal constant. Kolmogorov's paper states the hypotheses for velocity differences, not for spectra; the spectral form is the lecture's, derived below by dimensional analysis.

The spectrum \(E(\kappa)\) has units \(\mathrm{m^3/s^2}\) (energy per unit mass per unit wavenumber). Suppose that, for \(\kappa\gt\kappa_{EI}\), \(E\) depends on \(\kappa\) only through \(\varepsilon\), \(\nu\) and \(\kappa\) itself. These three quantities have two independent dimensions, length and time, so they form one dimensionless group, \(\kappa\eta\), and \(E\) is the product of a quantity with the dimensions of \(E\) and a function of the group. The quantity \((\varepsilon\nu^5)^{1/4}\) has the units \((\mathrm{m^2s^{-3}}\cdot\mathrm{m^{10}s^{-5}})^{1/4}=\mathrm{m^3s^{-2}}\), so

\[ E(\kappa)=(\varepsilon\nu^5)^{1/4}\,\Psi(\kappa\eta),\qquad\kappa\gt\kappa_{EI}, \tag{11.6} \]

with \(\Psi\) the same function in every flow (\(\Psi\) is the universal function; the first hypothesis does not supply its form). Using \(u_\eta^2\eta=(\varepsilon\nu)^{1/2}\nu^{3/4}\varepsilon^{-1/4}=(\varepsilon\nu^5)^{1/4}\), (11.6) states that \(E/(u_\eta^2\eta)\) is a universal function of \(\kappa\eta\). This is the scaling in which the measured spectra of the next section collapse.

For the inertial subrange the second hypothesis removes \(\nu\). Then \(E=\varepsilon^a\kappa^b\), and the exponents follow from the dimensions: time requires \(-3a=-2\), so \(a=\tfrac23\); length requires \(2a-b=3\), so \(b=-\tfrac53\). The solution is unique because \(\varepsilon\) and \(\kappa\) have independent dimensions. Therefore

\[ E(\kappa)=C\,\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3},\qquad\kappa_{EI}\lt\kappa\lt\kappa_{DI}. \tag{11.7} \]

The two forms agree: \(\Psi(x)=Cx^{-5/3}\) in (11.6) gives \((\varepsilon\nu^5)^{1/4}(\kappa\eta)^{-5/3}=\varepsilon^{1/4+5/12}\nu^{5/4-5/4}\kappa^{-5/3}=\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3}\), so viscosity drops out of (11.6) only for a \(-5/3\) power. The status of (11.7) must be stated precisely. The exponent \(-5/3\) is a consequence of the second similarity hypothesis; given the hypothesis, the argument is exact. The hypothesis is a hypothesis, supported by measurements (next section) and subject to the intermittency correction of Kolmogorov (1962), which the section "What the statements establish and what they do not" describes. The constant \(C\) is not determined by the argument; it is an empirical value, reported with its source in the next section. Dimensional analysis fixes a scaling, not its constant.

Range. Formula (11.7) holds in a range of wavenumbers, with two limits that the lecture states as \(\kappa_{EI}=2\pi/\ell_{EI}\) and \(\kappa_{DI}=2\pi/\ell_{DI}\), and that the nominal factors \(\tfrac16\) and 60 make 377 and \(1.33\times10^3\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\) for the jet. The ratio of the two limits is \(\kappa_{DI}/\kappa_{EI}=\ell_{EI}/\ell_{DI}=\ell_0/(360\eta)\), 3.5 for the jet and 831 for the layer. Whether \(\ell_{EI}\gt\ell_{DI}\) at all depends on the Reynolds number, as the preceding chapter shows for the nominal factors.

The measured spectrum: what collapses, where the power law holds, and which constant L29 7:41

The slide shows measured spectra of different flows (its Figure 6.14; the caption describes measured one-dimensional longitudinal velocity spectra as symbols and model spectra as lines for \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=30\), 70, 130, 300, 600 and 1500, with the data attributed to Saddoughi and Veeravalli (1994); the sources of this section write \(R_\lambda\)). The axes, as printed, are \(E_{11}(\kappa_1)/(\varepsilon\nu^5)^{1/4}\) against \(\kappa_1\eta\), on logarithmic scales. The lecture reads the figure as follows: spectra of grid turbulence, channel flow, jets and shear layers lie on one line when scaled with the Kolmogorov scales, which is the first similarity hypothesis; the energy-containing ranges, which are anisotropic, differ between flows.

One-dimensional and three-dimensional spectra. The plotted function is a one-dimensional spectrum. \(E_{11}(\kappa_1)\) is the spectrum of the longitudinal component \(u_1\) along the direction \(x_1\), with \(\kappa_1\) the component of the wavenumber along \(x_1\); it is defined by \(\int_0^\infty E_{11}\,d\kappa_1=\langle u_1^2\rangle\) (the book's notation, and the definition of Saddoughi, p. 237) and is what a probe in a stream measures. The function of (11.6) and (11.7), \(E(\kappa)\), is the three-dimensional spectrum of (8.32), a function of the wavenumber magnitude, with \(\int_0^\infty E\,d\kappa=k\). The two are different functions with different integrals, \(\langle u_1^2\rangle=\tfrac23k\) in isotropic turbulence, and a later chapter, "Spectral energy: Fourier modes, the velocity spectrum tensor, and one-dimensional spectra", derives the relation between them. The same hypotheses give an inertial-range law for each, with constants \(C_1\) and \(C\) respectively:

\[ E_{11}(\kappa_1)=C_1\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa_1^{-5/3},\qquad E(\kappa)=C\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3},\qquad C=\tfrac{55}{18}C_1\ \text{(isotropic)}. \tag{11.8} \]

The last relation is not derived here. Yeung and Zhou (1997) state it as the consequence of an isotropy relation between the one- and three-dimensional spectra (their equations (1)–(3), report pp. 1–2) and quote the same constants; Saddoughi (1993, p. 238) states \(C=\tfrac{55}{18}C_1\) as well. The program of this chapter checks the factor numerically from the relation \(E=\tfrac12\kappa^3\,d[\kappa^{-1}dE_{11}/d\kappa]/d\kappa\) that Yeung and Zhou give, which returns 3.0556 \(=55/18\) for a \(-5/3\) power. The relation requires isotropy, and the strongest evidence in this chapter that it is not exact for measured spectra is Saddoughi's: in his boundary layer, the transverse \(v\)-spectrum at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\approx600\) does not show a perfectly flat compensated portion at the height that the isotropic relation \(E_{22}=\tfrac43E_{11}\) predicts, which the author attributes to the Reynolds number (pp. 247, 250); at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\approx1500\) the transverse spectra show the predicted ratio (p. 249). A figure that shows \(E_{11}\) must not be read as a figure of \(E\); the lecture says "energy spectrum" for both.

What the measurements show. The collapse in Kolmogorov variables is a statement about high wavenumbers. In Saddoughi's compilation (CTR Annual Research Briefs 1992, Figure 3, p. 245), longitudinal spectra from boundary layers, a wake, grid turbulence, channels, a pipe, a round jet and a tidal channel, with \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) from 23 to 3180 in its legend, lie on one curve at large \(\kappa_1\eta\), and the data with lower \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) leave the common curve at progressively larger \(\kappa_1\eta\), as read from the figure at about \(10^{-2}\) for the lowest values. The text says that the spectra "peel off from the \(-5/3\) law at the low-wavenumber end in order of increasing Reynolds number" (p. 246). The lecture's remark that the spectra of the different flows are "identical" when scaled therefore holds at wavenumbers well above the energy-containing range, with a lower limit that decreases as the Reynolds number increases; this is the measurable form of "sufficiently high Reynolds number" in the hypotheses. The extent of the \(-5/3\) range is given twice for \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\approx1500\), and the two readings differ. On the logarithmic plot of Figure 3 the author finds "approximately two decades in wavenumber; one of the longest \(-5/3\) ranges seen in laboratory flows" (p. 246). On the linear-logarithmic plot of the compensated spectrum, which he says must support any claim of an inertial subrange (p. 250), "the compensated u-spectrum exhibits more than one decade of \(-5/3\) region, but less than the log-log plot (Figure 3) suggested" (p. 249). At \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\approx600\) the compensated longitudinal spectrum has "slightly less than one decade" (p. 247). This chapter uses the compensated-plot readings. The measured spectra have a "bump" between the inertial and the dissipation range (p. 247): the spectrum rises above the \(-5/3\) line before it falls steeply, so the transition at \(\kappa_{DI}\) is not the intersection of two power laws.

The constant. Three sources give values of \(C_1\) or \(C\), and they are not independent determinations of one number. Yeung and Zhou (report p. 2) quote Sreenivasan's survey of experiments, \(C_1\approx0.53\pm0.055\), and note that with \(C=\tfrac{55}{18}C_1\) it gives \(C=1.619\); their own simulations at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) up to 240 on grids up to \(512^3\) give \(C_1\approx0.60\) and \(C\approx1.62\) (abstract, report p. 1), which are not related by \(\tfrac{55}{18}\) (\(0.60\times\tfrac{55}{18}=1.83\)); they attribute the inconsistency to departures of the computed spectra from isotropy at low wavenumbers and to numerical and statistical limits, and they note that Zhou found \(C=1.5\) from a different calculation (report p. 2). Saddoughi takes the classical \(C=1.5\) (\(C_1\approx0.5\)) and finds that the longitudinal spectrum measured at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\approx600\) agrees with it, giving \(C=1.5\pm0.1\) with the dissipation, which scales the spectrum, uncertain by \(\pm10\ \%\) on p. 247, and by 20 % where the report discusses its dissipation estimates in general (pp. 243, 249); his dissipation comes from the isotropic relation \(\varepsilon=15\nu\int\kappa_1^2E_{11}\,d\kappa_1\), so the result is conditional on isotropy of the measured spectrum. The values quoted above range from \(C=1.5\) to \(C=1.83\) (converted from \(C_1=0.60\)), a spread of 22 %, and Sreenivasan's scatter of \(\pm10\ \%\) in \(C_1\) is of the same order. This chapter uses \(C=1.5\), which the lecture's slide leaves as "a universal constant", with that spread in mind. Yeung and Zhou add a point about method: a \(-5/3\) plateau in the compensated spectrum \(E\varepsilon^{-2/3}\kappa^{5/3}\) is necessary but not sufficient for an inertial range, because the turbulence must also be isotropic over the same wavenumbers; in their simulations a proper inertial range appears at \(\kappa\eta\) of about 0.02–0.05 once \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) exceeds about 140 (abstract, report p. 1). The upper end of that range, \(\kappa\eta\approx0.05\), is half the lecture's nominal \(\kappa_{DI}\eta=2\pi/60=0.105\); the nominal factor 60 may therefore place the end of the inertial subrange at a wavenumber where measured and simulated spectra have already left the \(-5/3\) line.

The convergence argument for the exponent L29 15:33

The lecture next asks which power laws \(E(\kappa)=A\kappa^{-p}\), with constants \(A\) and \(p\), are admissible as spectra. The slide computes the energy in the modes of wavenumber above \(\kappa\) and the dissipation in the modes below \(\kappa\):

\[ k_{(\kappa,\infty)}=\int_\kappa^\infty A\kappa'^{-p}\,d\kappa'=\frac{A}{p-1}\,\kappa^{-(p-1)}\quad(p\gt1), \tag{11.9} \] \[ \varepsilon_{(0,\kappa)}=\int_0^\kappa 2\nu\kappa'^2A\kappa'^{-p}\,d\kappa'=\frac{2\nu A}{3-p}\,\kappa^{3-p}\quad(p\lt3). \tag{11.10} \]

For \(p\le1\) the first integral diverges at the upper limit, and for \(p\ge3\) the second diverges at the lower limit. The slide concludes that \(p\) must lie in \((1,3)\) and that the Kolmogorov exponent \(p=\tfrac53\) "is around the middle of the range" (the midpoint of \((1,3)\) is 2; \(\tfrac53\) lies \(\tfrac13\) below it, one sixth of the interval's width, and no argument in the lecture makes the midpoint special), with \(k_{(\kappa,\infty)}\sim\kappa^{-2/3}\) and \(\varepsilon_{(0,\kappa)}\sim\kappa^{4/3}\). The lecture reads the choice of limits physically: energy comes from the large scales, so its integral runs up to infinity, and dissipation occurs at the small scales, so its integral runs down to zero.

What the argument shows. The computation (11.9)–(11.10) is correct, and it gives a condition: a power law that extends to infinite wavenumber has finite energy there only if \(p\gt1\), and one that extends to zero wavenumber has finite dissipation there only if \(p\lt3\). Within the stated assumptions it excludes, for example, a spectrum \(\kappa^{-1/2}\) that continues without limit to high wavenumbers.

What it does not show. The Kolmogorov law (11.7) is stated for \(\kappa_{EI}\lt\kappa\lt\kappa_{DI}\), not for all \(\kappa\). The hypotheses for the N-point density of velocity differences L30 4:11

The lecture restates the three hypotheses of the preceding chapter, "The energy cascade and Kolmogorov's hypotheses", in terms of probability densities, in the form of the slides. Consider a domain \(\mathscr G\) within a turbulent flow, and points \(\mathbf x^{(0)},\mathbf x^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf x^{(N)}\) in it, at one time \(t\). Define the position and velocity differences relative to the reference point \(\mathbf x^{(0)}\),

\[ \mathbf y\equiv\mathbf x-\mathbf x^{(0)},\qquad\mathbf v(\mathbf y)\equiv\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)-\mathbf U(\mathbf x^{(0)},t), \tag{11.13} \]

and let \(f_N\) be the joint probability density function of \(\mathbf v\) at the \(N\) points \(\mathbf y^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf y^{(N)}\) (the \(N\)-point density of chapter 8, (8.23), applied to differences). The turbulence is locally homogeneous in \(\mathscr G\) if, for every fixed \(N\) and \(\mathbf y^{(n)}\), \(f_N\) does not depend on \(\mathbf x^{(0)}\) or on the velocity \(\mathbf U(\mathbf x^{(0)},t)\); it is locally isotropic if in addition \(f_N\) is invariant under rotations and reflections of the coordinate axes. The three hypotheses then read, as the slide states them:

  • Local isotropy. In any turbulent flow with a sufficiently large Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}=\mathcal U\mathcal L/\nu\) (here \(\mathcal U\) and \(\mathcal L\) are the flow's velocity and length scale), the turbulence is to a good approximation locally isotropic in \(\mathscr G\) if the domain is sufficiently small, \(|\mathbf y^{(n)}|\ll\mathcal L\) for all \(n\), and is not near the boundary of the flow or other singularities.
  • First similarity. For locally isotropic turbulence, \(f_N\) is uniquely determined by \(\nu\) and \(\varepsilon\).
  • Second similarity. If \(|\mathbf y^{(m)}|\) and \(|\mathbf y^{(m)}-\mathbf y^{(n)}|\) (\(m\ne n\)) are large compared with \(\eta\), then \(f_N\) is uniquely determined by \(\varepsilon\) and does not depend on \(\nu\).

This is Kolmogorov's formulation (1941, English translation 1991, Definitions 1 and 2, pp. 9–10, and the two hypotheses of similarity, p. 12). The restatement adds nothing that the preceding chapter does not have, but it makes one point that matters here. The hypotheses constrain every statistic of the velocity differences, because they constrain their joint density. A second-order statistic is one moment of \(f_2\), and the lowest nontrivial one; the energy spectrum and the structure function, which the next section defines, are two summaries of the second moments, and the hypotheses fix the form of each in the inertial subrange. They constrain nothing about the one-point statistics of the velocity itself, which depend on the large scales. The slide's conditions, a sufficiently large \(\mathrm{Re}\), a small domain and distance from boundaries, are conditions of the hypotheses, and no relation derived below holds without them.

\[ D_{ij}(\mathbf r,\mathbf x,t)\equiv\bigl\langle\bigl[u_i(\mathbf x+\mathbf r,t)-u_i(\mathbf x,t)\bigr]\bigl[u_j(\mathbf x+\mathbf r,t)-u_j(\mathbf x,t)\bigr]\bigr\rangle. \tag{11.14} \]

A length that falls in the dissipation range for one flow and in the inertial subrange for the other is not the size of the dissipative eddies; it is a length at which the correlation function of the velocity bends, and it combines \(u'\) and \(\nu/\varepsilon\).

The large-scale, Taylor-scale and Kolmogorov scales L30 24:10

The slide defines the length \(L\equiv k^{3/2}/\varepsilon\) for the large eddies and the Reynolds number built on it,

\[ \mathrm{Re}_L\equiv\frac{k^{1/2}L}{\nu}=\frac{k^2}{\varepsilon\nu}. \tag{11.25} \]

The slide then gives the ratios of the small to the large scale and the Taylor-scale Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\equiv u'\lambda_g/\nu\) (the slide writes \(R_\lambda\); the book writes \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\)). All of them follow from the definitions and (11.24) by algebra. With \(u'^2=\tfrac23k\) (isotropy), (11.24) gives \(\lambda_g^2=15\nu u'^2/\varepsilon=10\nu k/\varepsilon\), so \(\lambda_g^2/L^2=10\nu\varepsilon/k^2=10/\mathrm{Re}_L\); and \(\eta/L=(\nu\varepsilon/k^2)^{3/4}=\mathrm{Re}_L^{-3/4}\). Then

\[ \frac{\lambda_g}{L}=\sqrt{10}\,\mathrm{Re}_L^{-1/2},\qquad\frac{\eta}{L}=\mathrm{Re}_L^{-3/4},\qquad\frac{\lambda_g}{\eta}=\sqrt{10}\,\mathrm{Re}_L^{1/4},\qquad\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=\Bigl(\frac{20}{3}\mathrm{Re}_L\Bigr)^{1/2}, \tag{11.26} \]

the last because \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda^2=u'^2\lambda_g^2/\nu^2=\tfrac23k\cdot10\nu k/(\varepsilon\nu^2)=\tfrac{20}3\mathrm{Re}_L\). The slide's \(\lambda_g=\sqrt{10}\,\eta^{2/3}L^{1/3}\) is the first two relations combined. At high Reynolds number \(\lambda_g\) lies between \(\eta\) and \(L\): \(\lambda_g/L\) tends to 0 and \(\lambda_g/\eta\) tends to infinity as \(\mathrm{Re}_L\to\infty\); the relation \(\lambda_g=\sqrt{10}\,\eta^{2/3}L^{1/3}\) makes \(\lambda_g\), up to the factor \(\sqrt{10}\), the geometric mean of \(\eta\) and \(L\) with weights \(\tfrac23\) and \(\tfrac13\). The ratio \(\lambda_g/u'=\sqrt{15}\,\tau_\eta\) of the previous section is the slide's statement that the ratio "correctly characterizes the timescale of the small eddies". It is the same statement as chapter 5's remark that the root-mean-square velocity derivative is \(1/(\sqrt{15}\,\tau_\eta)\), since \(u'/\lambda_g\) is that derivative by (11.23).

The running case. For the jet, with \(\varepsilon=u_0^3/\ell_0\) and \(u'=u_0\): \(L=k^{3/2}/\varepsilon=0.184\) m, \(\mathrm{Re}_L=3.08\times10^4\), \(\lambda_g=3.31\) mm, \(\lambda_g/\eta=41.9\), \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=453\). The ratio \(\mathrm{Re}_L/\mathrm{Re}_0=2.25\) is an identity of these two assumptions (\(k^2/\varepsilon\nu=\tfrac94u_0^4\ell_0/(u_0^3\nu)\)), as is \(L=1.84\,\ell_0\); with another constant in \(\varepsilon\sim u_0^3/\ell_0\) the numbers change, \(L\) in proportion to \(C_\varepsilon^{-1}\). For the atmospheric layer, \(L=1837\) m, \(\mathrm{Re}_L=4.5\times10^7\), \(\lambda_g=0.866\) m, \(\lambda_g/\eta=259\), \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=1.73\times10^4\). Equation (11.26) gives the same values.

A comparison with measured and simulated inertial ranges. Under the chapter's identification, \(\mathrm{Re}_0=\mathrm{Re}_\lambda^2/15\), so the lecture's nominal range limits predict the width of the inertial subrange as \(\log_{10}[\mathrm{Re}_0^{3/4}/360]\) decades, which the program evaluates. At \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=600\) it is 0.73 decade and at 1500 it is 1.33 decades, against the compensated-spectrum readings of Saddoughi's boundary layer: slightly less than one decade at 600 (p. 247) and more than one decade, though less than the two decades of his logarithmic plot, at 1500 (pp. 246, 249). The nominal 0.73 lies below "slightly less than one"; the nominal 1.33 lies inside the interval from one to two decades. The order agrees at both Reynolds numbers. The Reynolds number at which the nominal subrange first has nonzero width (\(\ell_{EI}=\ell_{DI}\)) is \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=196\), or 131 if \(\ell_0\) is identified with \(L\); Yeung and Zhou's simulations show an inertial range once \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) exceeds about 140 (abstract, p. 1). The comparison checks the order of magnitude of the demarcations; it does not validate the factors \(\tfrac16\) and 60, because \(\ell_0\) and \(L\) are different definitions, the boundary layer is not isotropic, and the measured widths come from slopes read by the authors of the sources.

Two judgments in the lecture. The slide states that the Taylor scale has no clear physical interpretation but is well defined and often used, and that \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) is "traditionally used to characterize grid turbulence". The lecture adds that \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) "applies only to grid turbulence" and cannot be used for jet or channel flows, and that the Taylor scale is a purely theoretical construct of little practical use (L30, about 1500 s: it "can only be defined for isotropic turbulence"). These are the lecturer's judgments; they are stronger than the slide, and they overstate. The Taylor scale is overstated in the same way: \(\lambda_f\) and \(\lambda_g\) are defined by \(f\) and \(g\), whose relation (11.22) needs isotropy, but the single length \(\lambda=u'/\langle(\partial u_1/\partial x_1)^2\rangle^{1/2}\) is defined in any flow with a finite derivative variance, and Saddoughi (p. 242) uses it in a boundary layer. The quantity \(u'\lambda_g/\nu\) can be evaluated in any flow in which \(u'\) and the longitudinal derivative are measured, and Saddoughi (p. 242 and Table 1, p. 243) reports \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) of about 600 and 1500 at the mid-layer of a turbulent boundary layer. What is restricted to isotropic or locally isotropic turbulence is the use of (11.24) to infer \(\varepsilon\) from one derivative, and of (11.26) to relate \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) to \(\mathrm{Re}_L\); in a shear flow, those formulas hold only to the accuracy of the isotropy assumption. The slide's reason for the traditional use is that grid turbulence is the nearest laboratory approximation to homogeneous isotropic turbulence, and that is the sense in which the lecture's statement is correct.

The velocity-derivative skewness L30 26:25

The last slide defines the skewness of the longitudinal velocity derivative,

\[ S\equiv\frac{\langle(\partial u_1/\partial x_1)^3\rangle}{\langle(\partial u_1/\partial x_1)^2\rangle^{3/2}}. \tag{11.27} \]

The lecture states only the definition. It is the standardized third moment of chapter 7 for the variable \(\partial u_1/\partial x_1\), and chapter 7 (section "Mean, variance and standardized moments") reports its measured value in simulations: Kerr (1983) finds \(S=-0.505\pm0.005\) in his forced isotropic runs F11 to F27, whose Taylor-scale Reynolds numbers run from about 28.5 to 82.9 (NASA TM 84407, p. 15; Table 3, p. 35). He states that it shows no Reynolds-number dependence over a wide range (p. 15), while noting the small range of scales a simulation can contain. The table also lists lower Reynolds numbers: \(S=-0.502\) at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=9.0\), \(-0.523\) at 12.3, and \(-0.32\), \(-0.40\), \(-0.44\) for the decaying runs at 2.33, 4.07 and 6.20, so Kerr's constancy is a statement about \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) of 28.5 and above, with the table values at 18.5 and 24.0 (\(-0.506\) and \(-0.507\)) in the same band, not about every simulation. A Gaussian variable has \(S=0\), so the value \(-0.5\) is a measured departure from Gaussian small-scale statistics. The lecture lists the skewness among "different applications" of the Kolmogorov hypotheses (L30, about 1585 to 1620 s) and gives no reason for the listing; the next paragraph shows that the hypotheses do not fix its value.

Symmetry does not require \(S\) to vanish, in contrast with the transverse derivative. A reflection of the \(x_1\)-axis changes the signs of both \(u_1\) and \(\partial/\partial x_1\), so \(\partial u_1/\partial x_1\) is unchanged; invariance under that reflection places no constraint on its odd moments. (For a transverse derivative such as \(\partial u_1/\partial x_2\), reflecting \(x_2\) changes its sign, and local isotropy forces its odd moments to vanish.) Kerr notes that, by an isotropic relation between the skewness and the mean cube of the rate of strain, a negative skewness implies that the principal rate of strain of largest magnitude is compressive (negative). Because the trace of the rate of strain is zero, this is a statement about the strain of largest absolute value; the algebraically largest principal strain is never negative. In the 0.5 % of grid points that he samples on the magnitude of the rate of strain, the principal strain of largest absolute value is negative in every case (NASA TM 84407, p. 23). Local isotropy and the similarity hypotheses are statements about universal forms; they do not give the value of \(S\), and none of the results of this chapter does.

def exponents():
    """Return (a, b) with E = eps**a * kappa**b in m^3/s^2."""
    dims_eps, dims_kappa, dims_e = (2., -3.), (-1., 0.), (3., -2.)
    return np.linalg.solve(np.column_stack([dims_eps, dims_kappa]), dims_e)


def energy_above(a_c, p, kappa):
    """Energy above kappa for E = a_c*kappa**-p; needs p > 1."""
    if p <= 1:
        raise ValueError("energy integral diverges for p <= 1")
    return a_c*kappa**(1 - p)/(p - 1)


def dissipation_below(a_c, p, kappa, nu):
    """Dissipation below kappa for E = a_c*kappa**-p; needs p < 3."""
    if p >= 3:
        raise ValueError("dissipation integral diverges for p >= 3")
    return 2*nu*a_c*kappa**(3 - p)/(3 - p)

What the statements establish and what they do not

The status of the statements. The chapter contains four kinds of statement. The integral relations (11.2)–(11.4), the isotropic tensor forms (11.15) and (11.17), the continuity relation (11.18) and its consequences (11.19), (11.20), (11.22) and (11.24), and the identities (11.26) are derivations: exact given homogeneity, incompressibility and, where stated, isotropy. The forms (11.6), (11.7) and (11.16) are dimensional consequences of the similarity hypotheses and exact given them, with undetermined constants. The hypotheses themselves are hypotheses about the limit of high Reynolds number, supported by the measurements described above; Kolmogorov (1941, p. 10) states that the local isotropy hypothesis, in its general formulation, cannot be rigorously proved. The constants \(C\), \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) are empirical values with a spread between sources of 10 to 20 %, and \(S\) is an empirical value that Kerr gives as \(-0.505\pm0.005\) in simulations of limited Reynolds number, and the nominal demarcations \(\tfrac16\) and 60 are values the lecture assumes without justification; every share and width computed from them is conditional on them. The relation \(C=\tfrac{55}{18}C_1\) and all the relations of the correlation sections require isotropy, the equality \(D_{NN}=\tfrac43D_{LL}\) requires in addition the inertial law, and none of them is exact for a shear flow.

The shares. The measured spectra have a bump near the end of the inertial range (Saddoughi p. 247), and the simulated inertial range of Yeung and Zhou ends near \(\kappa\eta\approx0.05\), half of the nominal \(\kappa_{DI}\eta=0.105\); the shares computed above are therefore upper estimates of what a true inertial range holds, and the conclusion that most of the dissipation lies outside it is stronger than the numbers suggest. The energy share needs the universal number of (11.6) to be of order one for the conclusion that most energy lies in the energy-containing range; the hypotheses do not supply it.

Intermittency. The exponent \(-\tfrac53\) belongs to the 1941 hypotheses, which use the mean dissipation rate. Kolmogorov's 1962 refinement (J. Fluid Mech. 13, p. 84, equation (10)) replaces \(\bar\varepsilon\) by the dissipation averaged over a sphere of radius \(r\) and gives, for the mean-square longitudinal velocity difference, a factor \((\mathcal L/r)^{\chi n(n-3)/2}\) for the moment of order \(n\) (the paper writes \(p\) for the order), with \(n=2\), that is \((r/\mathcal L)^{\chi}\), where \(\mathcal L\) is the external scale and \(\chi\) a universal constant of the refinement (the paper writes \(k\)). Then \(D_{LL}\propto r^{2/3+\chi}\) and the spectrum exponent is \(-(\tfrac53+\chi)\); the paper gives no value of \(\chi\). Yeung and Zhou state that the effect on the second-order spectrum exponent is believed small and hardly measurable (report p. 1). The refinement matters for higher moments, as the preceding chapter shows, and this chapter's second-order statistics are the least affected.

The jet. The jet is not homogeneous; the numbers of the chapter treat the station as if it were homogeneous and isotropic with \(u'=u_0\) and unit constant. A measured spectrum on the jet's axis would give \(E_{11}\) after Taylor's hypothesis (later chapter), not \(E\), and would estimate \(\varepsilon\) from (11.24) only to the accuracy of local isotropy.

What the chapter leaves to later chapters. The shape of the spectrum in the energy-containing and dissipation ranges (the model spectra, chapter "Kolmogorov, model, and dissipation spectra"), the derivation of the relation between one- and three-dimensional spectra (chapter "Spectral energy: Fourier modes, the velocity spectrum tensor, and one-dimensional spectra") and the spectral energy balance that defines \(\mathcal T(\kappa)\) (chapter "Reynolds-number effects and the energy-spectrum balance") are not treated; where the text uses a result of those chapters, it states it as an input.

What the reader can now do. The reader can now (i) compute the energy and dissipation held between two wavenumbers for a given spectrum and state the assumption behind each share; (ii) derive the form of \(E(\kappa)\) and \(D_{LL}(r)\) by dimensional analysis and say which hypothesis each uses; (iii) obtain \(L_{22}\), \(\lambda_g\), \(\varepsilon\) and \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) from \(f\) under isotropy; and (iv) say which of these statements fail without isotropy.

Exercise 1 (derive and convert). (a) Show that \((\varepsilon\nu^5)^{1/4}\) has the units of \(E(\kappa)\), and that the first similarity hypothesis implies \(E(\kappa)/(\varepsilon\nu^5)^{1/4}=\Psi(\kappa\eta)\). (b) In the inertial subrange the one-dimensional spectrum satisfies \(E_{11}/(\varepsilon\nu^5)^{1/4}=C_1(\kappa_1\eta)^{-5/3}\). Evaluate the right side at \(\kappa_1\eta=10^{-2}\) for \(C_1=0.53\). (c) Convert each of the following to the other constant by (11.8): \(C_1=0.53\), \(C_1=0.60\) and \(C=1.5\). By what percentage does the pair \((C_1,C)=(0.60,1.62)\) of the simulations of Yeung and Zhou depart from (11.8)?

Solution

(a) \((\varepsilon\nu^5)^{1/4}=(\mathrm{m^2s^{-3}}\cdot\mathrm{m^{10}s^{-5}})^{1/4}=(\mathrm{m^{12}s^{-8}})^{1/4}=\mathrm{m^3s^{-2}}\), the units of \(E\). The quantities \(\varepsilon\), \(\nu\), \(\kappa\) have two independent dimensions, so they form one dimensionless group, which can be taken as \(\kappa\eta=\kappa(\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}\); \(E\) divided by the quantity of its own units is dimensionless and so depends only on that group, which is (11.6).

(b) \(C_1(\kappa_1\eta)^{-5/3}=0.53\times(10^{-2})^{-5/3}=0.53\times2154=1.14\times10^3\).

(c) \(C_1=0.53\) gives \(C=\tfrac{55}{18}\times0.53=1.62\); \(C_1=0.60\) gives \(C=1.83\); \(C=1.5\) gives \(C_1=\tfrac{18}{55}\times1.5=0.491\). For the pair \((0.60,1.62)\), (11.8) would give \(C_1=\tfrac{18}{55}\times1.62=0.530\) from \(C\), and the measured \(C_1\) is 13 % larger; equivalently (11.8) would give \(C=1.83\) from \(C_1\), and the measured \(C\) is 12 % smaller. Yeung and Zhou attribute the inconsistency to departures from isotropy at low wavenumbers and to numerical and statistical limits. The exercise shows that a quoted constant must be accompanied by the statement whether it belongs to the one- or the three-dimensional spectrum.

Repeat the shares of the section "Where the energy and the dissipation lie" for the jet with \(C_\varepsilon=0.468\) (so \(\varepsilon=0.468\,u_0^3/\ell_0\); the value comes from simulations whose velocity and length scale have the simulation authors' definitions and not the lecture's \(u_0\) and \(\ell_0\)) and \(C=1.62\), keeping the nominal range limits. (a) Compute \(\varepsilon\), \(\eta\), \(\kappa_{EI}\), \(\kappa_{DI}\) and the two shares. (b) Compare with the values 7.6 % and 9.0 % for \(C_\varepsilon=1\), \(C=1.5\), and explain which change in the assumptions moves each share.

Solution

(a) \(\varepsilon=0.468\times80=37.4\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\); \(\eta=(\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}=95.5\ \mu\mathrm m\); \(\kappa_{EI}=12\pi/\ell_0=377\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\) is unchanged; \(\kappa_{DI}=2\pi/(60\eta)=1096\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\). From (11.11), \(k_{(\kappa_{EI},\kappa_{DI})}/k=0.0442\) and \(\varepsilon_{(\kappa_{EI},\kappa_{DI})}/\varepsilon=0.0910\). The inertial subrange is narrower: \(\kappa_{DI}/\kappa_{EI}=2.91\) instead of 3.52.

(b) The energy share falls from 7.6 % to 4.4 %, a factor 0.58, and the dissipation share is nearly unchanged (9.0 % to 9.1 %). The energy share contains the factor \(C\,C_\varepsilon^{2/3}\) in its upper bound: \(1.08\times0.468^{2/3}=1.08\times0.603=0.65\), because the inertial-range energy scales as \(\varepsilon^{2/3}\) while \(k\) is fixed, and the narrower range lowers the share further. The dissipation share is normalized by \(\varepsilon\) itself, so its upper bound does not contain \(C_\varepsilon\): the factor \(\tfrac32C\) rises by 8 %, and the bracket \((2\pi/60)^{4/3}-(\kappa_{EI}\eta)^{4/3}\) falls by 7 %, because \((\kappa_{EI}\eta)^{4/3}\) grows from 0.0092 to 0.0119 when \(\eta\) increases from 79.0 to 95.5 \(\mu\)m.

Exercise 3 (find a counterexample). A student argues: "A spectrum \(E=A\kappa^{-p}\) has finite energy and finite dissipation only for \(1\lt p\lt3\), so every spectrum that occurs in a turbulent flow has a power-law range with an exponent between 1 and 3, and \(\tfrac53\) is the most natural." (a) Construct a spectrum that has \(p=\tfrac12\) over a range of wavenumbers and finite energy and dissipation. (b) Do the same for \(p=4\). (c) State precisely what the convergence conditions exclude, and what selects \(\tfrac53\).

Solution

(a) Let \(E(\kappa)=\kappa^{-1/2}\) for \(1\le\kappa\le10\) and \(E=0\) elsewhere (units omitted). The energy is \(\int_1^{10}\kappa^{-1/2}d\kappa=2(\sqrt{10}-1)=4.32\) and the dissipation is \(2\nu\int_1^{10}\kappa^{3/2}d\kappa=\tfrac45\nu(10^{5/2}-1)=252\,\nu\); both are finite although \(p=\tfrac12\lt1\).

(b) For \(E=\kappa^{-4}\) on \([1,10]\), the energy is \(\tfrac13(1-10^{-3})=0.333\) and the dissipation is \(2\nu\int_1^{10}\kappa^{-2}d\kappa=1.8\,\nu\); both are finite although \(p=4\gt3\).

(c) The conditions (11.9) and (11.10) exclude a power law that continues to infinite wavenumber with \(p\le1\) (energy infinite) or to zero wavenumber with \(p\ge3\) (dissipation infinite). They say nothing about a power law over a finite range, and a turbulent spectrum follows a power law only over a finite range. The exponent \(\tfrac53\) is selected by dimensional analysis once the spectrum is assumed to depend only on \(\varepsilon\) and \(\kappa\). A measurement of \(p\ne\tfrac53\) in a range of wavenumbers would contradict that hypothesis, not the convergence of any integral.

Exercise 4 (derive and compute). (a) From (11.18), derive \(D_{NN}(r)=D_{LL}(r)+\tfrac r2\,dD_{LL}/dr\) and its two limits, \(D_{NN}/D_{LL}\to2\) for small \(r\) and \(\tfrac43\) for \(D_{LL}\propto r^{2/3}\). (b) For the jet, take \(C_2=1.97\) and the separation \(r=10\) mm. Check that \(r\) lies in the inertial subrange by the nominal limits, and compute the root-mean-square longitudinal and transverse velocity differences. (c) Compare the longitudinal one with the value for uncorrelated velocities at the two points.

Solution

(a) With \(R_{ij}\) even, \(D_{LL}=2u'^2(1-f)\) and \(D_{NN}=2u'^2(1-g)\). Subtracting, \(D_{NN}-D_{LL}=2u'^2(f-g)=-u'^2rf'\) by (11.18). Since \(dD_{LL}/dr=-2u'^2f'\), \(-u'^2rf'=\tfrac r2dD_{LL}/dr\), which is the relation. For small \(r\), \(D_{LL}\propto r^2\) (the velocity is smooth), so \(\tfrac r2dD_{LL}/dr=D_{LL}\) and \(D_{NN}=2D_{LL}\). For \(D_{LL}\propto r^{2/3}\), \(\tfrac r2dD_{LL}/dr=\tfrac13D_{LL}\) and \(D_{NN}=\tfrac43D_{LL}\). Kolmogorov (1941, p. 13) states both limits as (25) and (24).

(b) The nominal limits are \(\ell_{DI}=4.7\) mm and \(\ell_{EI}=16.7\) mm, and 10 mm lies between them. \(D_{LL}=C_2(\varepsilon r)^{2/3}=1.97\times(80\times0.01)^{2/3}=1.97\times0.862=1.70\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\), so the root-mean-square longitudinal difference is 1.30 m/s. \(D_{NN}=\tfrac43\times1.70=2.27\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\), root-mean-square 1.51 m/s.

(c) For uncorrelated velocities, \(D_{LL}=2u'^2=8\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\), root-mean-square 2.83 m/s. The value 1.70 is 21 % of it, so the two points at 10 mm are strongly correlated, consistent with \(f(r)\) near 1 at \(r\ll L_{11}\). The values rest on the nominal limits, the unit constant and \(u'=u_0\) for a flow that is not isotropic.

Exercise 5 (compute and check an assumption). A hot-wire in grid turbulence in air (\(\nu=1.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\)) gives \(u'=0.40\ \mathrm{m/s}\) and a root-mean-square streamwise derivative \(\langle(\partial u_1/\partial x_1)^2\rangle^{1/2}=150\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) (assume the derivative has been obtained from the time derivative with Taylor's hypothesis, a later chapter). (a) Assuming isotropy, compute \(\varepsilon\), \(\lambda_g\), \(\lambda_f\), \(\eta\), \(\tau_\eta\), \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\), \(L\), \(\mathrm{Re}_L\) and check \(\lambda_g/\eta=\sqrt{10}\,\mathrm{Re}_L^{1/4}\). (b) A second probe measures the transverse derivative and gives \(\langle(\partial u_1/\partial x_2)^2\rangle=1.6\) times the longitudinal value, not 2. If the other transverse derivatives keep this ratio, what is \(\varepsilon\) from (11.4), and how does it compare with (a)? (c) By the lecture's nominal limits, does this flow have an inertial subrange?

Solution

(a) \(\varepsilon=15\nu\times150^2=15\times1.5\times10^{-5}\times22500=5.06\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\). \(\lambda_g=u'/150=2.67\) mm, \(\lambda_f=\sqrt2\lambda_g=3.77\) mm. \(\eta=(\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}=0.161\) mm and \(\tau_\eta=(\nu/\varepsilon)^{1/2}=1.72\) ms; check \(\lambda_g/u'=1/150=6.67\) ms \(=\sqrt{15}\,\tau_\eta\). \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=u'\lambda_g/\nu=0.40\times2.67\times10^{-3}/1.5\times10^{-5}=71.1\). With \(k=\tfrac32u'^2=0.24\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\), \(L=k^{3/2}/\varepsilon=23.2\) mm and \(\mathrm{Re}_L=k^2/(\varepsilon\nu)=758\); \(\sqrt{10}\,\mathrm{Re}_L^{1/4}=3.162\times5.25=16.6\), and \(\lambda_g/\eta=2.67/0.161=16.6\). Also \((\tfrac{20}3\mathrm{Re}_L)^{1/2}=71.1\).

(b) With \(A=150^2\), (11.4) gives \(\varepsilon=\nu(3A+6\times1.6A)=12.6\,\nu A=4.25\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\), against 5.06: 16 % lower. The test of the factor 2 is a test of the isotropy that (11.24) assumes; a measured ratio of 1.6 would put the error of the one-derivative estimate at that order, if the assumed pattern held for all six transverse terms, which one measurement cannot show.

(c) With the chapter's identification, \(\mathrm{Re}_0=\mathrm{Re}_\lambda^2/15=337\) and \(\ell_0/\eta=337^{3/4}=79\), well below the threshold 360 for \(\ell_{EI}\gt\ell_{DI}\). The nominal limits give no inertial subrange. This agrees in order with the finding of Yeung and Zhou that an inertial range appears in simulations only above \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) of about 140. A value \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=71\) is close to the 72 of the grid-turbulence data of Comte-Bellot and Corrsin in the legend of Saddoughi's Figure 3 (p. 245), and lies below the Reynolds number at which an inertial range is expected.

Exercise 6 (find a counterexample). A student proposes the longitudinal correlation \(f(r)=e^{-r/L_{11}}\) for isotropic turbulence, because it is simple and \(\int_0^\infty f\,dr=L_{11}\). (a) Find \(g(r)\) from (11.18), verify \(L_{22}=\tfrac12L_{11}\), and find where \(g\) changes sign. (b) Show that this \(f\) is not the correlation of a differentiable velocity field and give the consequence for \(D_{LL}\) and \(\varepsilon\). (c) Compute \(L_{11}\), \(\lambda_f\) and \(\lambda_g\) for the Gaussian \(f(r)=e^{-\pi r^2/4L_{11}^2}\) instead.

Solution

(a) \(f'=-f/L_{11}\), so \(g=f+\tfrac r2f'=e^{-r/L_{11}}(1-r/2L_{11})\). \(\int_0^\infty g\,dr=L_{11}-\tfrac1{2L_{11}}\int_0^\infty re^{-r/L_{11}}dr=L_{11}-\tfrac1{2L_{11}}L_{11}^2=\tfrac12L_{11}\), as (11.19) requires, since \(rf\to0\). The function \(g\) changes sign at \(r=2L_{11}\) and is negative beyond it; the area under the whole curve, the positive part minus the negative loop, is \(\tfrac12L_{11}\).

(b) The slope at the origin is \(f'(0)=-1/L_{11}\ne0\), whereas a field with a mean-square derivative has \(f'(0)=0\). Then \(D_{LL}=2u'^2(1-e^{-r/L_{11}})\approx2u'^2r/L_{11}\) for small \(r\), linear in \(r\) and not proportional to \(r^2\); \(\langle(\partial_1u_1)^2\rangle=\lim D_{LL}/r^2\) is infinite, and by (11.4) and (11.24) so is \(\varepsilon\) for any \(\nu\gt0\); the Taylor microscale of (11.21) is zero. The exponential correlation describes at most the range of large separations. It is acceptable for a process whose derivative is not needed, as in chapter 8, and is not admissible for a velocity field with a finite dissipation rate.

(c) \(\int_0^\infty e^{-\pi r^2/4L_{11}^2}dr=\tfrac{\sqrt\pi}2\cdot\tfrac{2L_{11}}{\sqrt\pi}=L_{11}\). The expansion \(f=1-\pi r^2/4L_{11}^2+\cdots\) gives \(\lambda_f^2=4L_{11}^2/\pi\), so \(\lambda_f=2L_{11}/\sqrt\pi=1.128\,L_{11}\) and \(\lambda_g=\lambda_f/\sqrt2=0.798\,L_{11}\). The program's finite-difference curvatures give \(\lambda_f/\lambda_g=1.4142\). For a Gaussian \(f\) the microscale is comparable to the integral scale, which is the behaviour of a flow at low Reynolds number; at high Reynolds number \(\lambda_g/L=\sqrt{10}\,\mathrm{Re}_L^{-1/2}\ll1\) by (11.26), and a single Gaussian cannot represent \(f\) over all separations.
