Random variables and probability density functions: from repeated experiments to joint and conditional statistics

A hot-wire probe sits on the centreline of an air jet, at the station where the mean axial velocity is 8 m/s. The experiment is run again, under conditions that the operator sets as carefully as possible, and the velocity recorded at the same position and the same time after the start is different each time. A prediction of the next value is not possible. A prediction of the probability that the next value is below 10 m/s is, and so is a prediction of the mean, the spread and the shape of the distribution of values. This chapter defines the objects that carry such predictions for one velocity component, the cumulative distribution function, the probability density function and its moments, and then for two components at once, the joint, marginal and conditional densities and the correlation coefficient. It also shows, with a deterministic system of three equations whose output can be computed, why equations without random terms produce outcomes that must be described this way.

The objects of the chapter and the running case

The formal model of this chapter is a repeatable experiment. An experiment is run under a set of conditions \(\mathcal C\) (the slides use this symbol): the geometry, the fluid, the inflow and the procedure, as far as the operator can specify them. In each run, one number \(U\) is recorded: one component of the velocity \(\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)\) at a fixed position \(\mathbf x\) and a fixed time \(t\). The number varies from run to run. A random variable is, in this sense, a quantity whose value in a run under \(\mathcal C\) cannot be predicted, so that a statement such as "\(U\) is below 10 m/s" is true in some runs and false in others. "Random" here describes the outcome of the repeated experiment. It does not mean that the governing equations contain random terms; the next two sections show how deterministic equations produce such outcomes.

The value that \(U\) takes is distinguished from the variable of the functions that describe it. The slides write \(V\) for this second variable and call it the sample-space variable: \(V\) ranges over all values that \(U\) could take, and a function such as \(F(V)\) answers a question about \(U\) for each candidate value \(V\). An event is a set of outcomes, for example \(\mathcal A=\{U\lt10\ \mathrm{m/s}\}\). Its probability \(P\{\mathcal A\}\) is a number in \([0,1]\). The interpretation used here is the relative frequency: in \(N\) runs, let \(N_{\mathcal A}\) be the number of runs in which \(\mathcal A\) occurs; then \(P\{\mathcal A\}\) is the value that \(N_{\mathcal A}/N\) approaches as \(N\) grows. Every average in this chapter is an average over runs, an ensemble average. Chapter 8 states the assumptions under which an average over time in one long run gives the same value.

The running case is the air jet of chapter 6, at its downstream station: \(U\) is the axial velocity on the centreline, with mean \(8\ \mathrm{m/s}\) and root-mean-square fluctuation \(2\ \mathrm{m/s}\). Chapter 6 chose these values; they are not measurements of a particular jet. Where a full distribution is needed, this chapter uses the Gaussian model of the section "Standard PDFs" with that mean and that root-mean-square value. The model is an assumption whose quality is discussed with the evidence in the section "Mean, variance and standardized moments". The second velocity component, radial on the centreline, enters with the joint distributions. Next to the jet, the chapter uses a system that can be computed exactly as often as needed: an ensemble of solutions of Lorenz's three equations, introduced in the section "Lorenz's equations".

Repeated experiments make the velocity a random variable L17 4:08

The lecture's first figure is a sketch of the value \(U^{(n)}\), in m/s, of one velocity component measured at the same position and time in the \(n\)-th repetition of a turbulent-flow experiment, for about 40 repetitions. The values lie between about 5 and 16 m/s. The slide's statement is the definition above.

The definition says what a theory of turbulence can aim at. It cannot give the value \(U^{(n+1)}\) of the next run. It can give probabilities of events, such as \(P\{\mathcal A\}\), and averages over many runs. For the running case under the Gaussian model, \(P\{U\lt10\ \mathrm{m/s}\}=0.841\) (computed in the section "Standard PDFs"): in a long series of runs, about 84 % of the recorded values fall below 10 m/s. A single run that gives 11 m/s does not contradict this number. A series of 100 runs of which only 50 fall below 10 m/s would contradict it, because the relative frequency in 100 runs that do not influence one another (independence is defined in the section "Conditional PDFs and independence"; Exercise 5(a) derives the standard error) has a standard deviation of \((0.841\times0.159/100)^{1/2}=0.037\), so 0.50 lies more than 9 standard deviations from 0.841.

The lecture also states what this does not mean. For a laminar flow, the Navier–Stokes equations can be used to calculate \(U\), and the answer is accurate: repeated runs give the same value within the measurement error. For a turbulent flow the value is unpredictable, and the slide asks whether the theory of the Navier–Stokes equations is therefore wrong. Its answer, which this chapter adopts, is no: the theory can be used to predict probabilities of events, and the remaining sections build the tools for that.

Deterministic equations, incomplete conditions and sensitivity L17 7:17

The Navier–Stokes equations of chapter 6 are deterministic: they contain no random terms, and the lecture's premise is that for given initial conditions, boundary conditions and fluid properties they give one solution. The slide asks how this is consistent with the random outcomes of the previous section, and gives two reasons that act together:

  1. Perturbations in the initial conditions, the boundary conditions and the material properties. The conditions \(\mathcal C\) are incomplete. They do not specify small perturbations such as vibrations of the apparatus, the detailed finish of a nominally smooth surface, or small inhomogeneities of temperature or impurities that change the fluid properties. The lecture's examples are the roughness that remains on the inside of a precision-machined pipe and the impurities that remain in distilled water. These perturbations differ from run to run and are not controlled.
  2. Sensitivity to such perturbations. Perturbations exist in laminar flows too, so their presence alone does not explain random outcomes. The slide's statement is that at high Reynolds number the evolution of the flow field is extremely sensitive to them. The lecture's explanation is that in a laminar flow viscous diffusion damps the perturbations, whereas in a turbulent flow the viscous effects are small compared with inertia, so the perturbations persist and are amplified.

The lecture's contrast between laminar and turbulent flows needs one qualification from chapter 4. A laminar flow damps every small enough disturbance only while it is stable. Chapter 4 (section "Turbulence begins as an instability of a laminar flow") describes how a laminar flow becomes unstable above some Reynolds number, after which some disturbances grow. The precise statement is therefore that a stable laminar flow returns to the same state after small perturbations, and that a turbulent flow does not.

The same two reasons apply to a numerical simulation, with a different source of perturbation. A computer represents each number to a finite number of significant digits. An initial condition entered as 0.1 is stored as a nearby binary number, and every arithmetic operation rounds its result. In a flow that is sensitive to perturbations, these rounding differences grow in the same way as the physical perturbations of an experiment. The section "Executable ensemble" measures this effect at one time: the same 4000 initial states integrated with time steps 0.01 and 0.005 give, at \(t^*=100\), members that differ by up to 32.4, comparable to the width of the distribution, about 38 (last printed line). The two computations differ only in their truncation error, and that error has grown to the size of the signal.

Lorenz's equations: sensitivity to the initial state in three variables L17 12:38

The lecture illustrates sensitivity with a system much simpler than the Navier–Stokes equations, studied by Lorenz (1963). It has three state variables \(x(t)\), \(y(t)\), \(z(t)\) of a dimensionless time \(t\) (here \(x\) is a state variable, not a component of the position \(\mathbf x\)), which evolve according to

\[ \frac{dx}{dt}=\mathrm{Pr}\,(y-x),\qquad \frac{dy}{dt}=r\,x-y-xz,\qquad \frac{dz}{dt}=xy-\beta z. \tag{7.1} \]

The slides write \(\sigma\), \(\rho\) and \(\beta\) for the three constants, and Lorenz writes \(\sigma\), \(r\) and \(b\). This chapter writes \(\mathrm{Pr}\), \(r\) and \(\beta\), because \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation and \(\rho\) the correlation coefficient below. Lorenz obtained (7.1) as a three-term truncation of the equations of two-dimensional thermal convection in a fluid layer heated from below (p. 135). There \(x\) is proportional to the intensity of the convective motion, \(y\) to the temperature difference between the ascending and descending currents, and \(z\) to the distortion of the vertical temperature profile from a linear one. The constant \(\mathrm{Pr}\) is the Prandtl number, \(r\) is the Rayleigh number divided by its critical value for the onset of convection, and \(\beta\) is a geometric factor (p. 135). The slides use Lorenz's values \(\mathrm{Pr}=10\), \(\beta=8/3\) and \(r=28\) (p. 136).

Lorenz states the limit of the model himself: the solutions of (7.1) may be realistic when the Rayleigh number is slightly supercritical, but they cannot be expected to resemble those of the full convection equations when strong convection occurs, in view of the extreme truncation (p. 135). Equation (7.1) is therefore not a model of turbulence. It is used here for one purpose: it is a deterministic system that can be solved on any computer, and it shows the behaviour that the previous section attributed to the Navier–Stokes equations.

The experiment on the slides. Equation (7.1) is solved twice. The first initial condition is \([x(0),y(0),z(0)]=[0.1,0.1,0.1]\). The second differs only in \(x\): \([0.100001,0.1,0.1]\), a change of \(10^{-6}\), one part in \(10^{5}\) of \(x(0)\). The slide's figure shows the two histories \(x(t)\) and \(\hat x(t)\) and their difference \(\hat x(t)-x(t)\) for \(0\le t\le100\). The two histories are indistinguishable at first, and by \(t=35\) they are quite different; in the figure the difference becomes visible near \(t=30\), and after that it is of the same size as the signals themselves (which stay within about \(\pm20\)), with peaks near \(\pm30\) on an axis that runs to \(\pm40\). The computation in the section "Executable ensemble" repeats this: the difference first exceeds 1 at \(t=31.0\) with a time step of 0.01, and at \(t=29.0\) with a time step of 0.005. This agreement with the slide's figure is a consistency check, not a validation of either computation, and the 2-unit change with the time step is itself a result: the truncation error of the integration acts as a further perturbation of the same kind.

Lorenz's abstract states the general finding: for systems of this kind with bounded solutions, nonperiodic solutions are ordinarily unstable with respect to small modifications, so that slightly differing initial states can evolve into considerably different states (p. 130). The slide draws the consequence that matters here: beyond some time, the state of the system cannot be predicted. A perturbation of \(10^{-6}\) is not a special choice. Any perturbation that grows, however small, eventually reaches the size of the signal, and a smaller perturbation only delays that time.

The behaviour depends on the constants. The second slide statement is that the qualitative behaviour depends on the coefficients: below a critical value \(\rho^*\approx25\) (in this chapter's notation, \(r\)) the system goes to stable fixed points, and for larger values chaotic behaviour ensues. Lorenz's linear stability analysis gives the precise values for \(\mathrm{Pr}=10\), \(\beta=8/3\). The state of no convection, \(x=y=z=0\), is stable for \(r\lt1\) (p. 135). For \(r\gt1\) there are two further steady states, \(x=y=\pm[\beta(r-1)]^{1/2}\), \(z=r-1\), representing steady convection (p. 135), and they lose their stability at

\[ r=\frac{\mathrm{Pr}\,(\mathrm{Pr}+\beta+3)}{\mathrm{Pr}-\beta-1}=\frac{10\times(10+8/3+3)}{10-8/3-1}=\frac{470}{19}=24.74 \tag{7.2} \]

(Lorenz's equation (34) and p. 136). The stable fixed point is the origin only for \(r\lt1\). For \(1\lt r\lt24.74\) the stable steady states are the two convecting ones, at \(x=\pm[\beta(r-1)]^{1/2}\ne0\). The value 24.74 is the result of a linear analysis of those two states. It states where they stop attracting nearby trajectories; it does not by itself describe every trajectory of (7.1). At \(r=28\) the two steady states are \((\pm6\sqrt2,\pm6\sqrt2,27)\) (p. 136), both unstable, and the computed solutions are the nonperiodic ones of the slide's figure.

From one trajectory to a random variable. The slide experiment compares two runs. The probabilistic description needs many. This chapter's computed ensemble repeats the experiment \(N=4000\) times. Run \(n\), for \(n=0,1,\ldots,N-1\), starts from \(x(0)=0.1+10^{-5}(n/N-\tfrac12)\), \(y(0)=z(0)=0.1\): the initial values of \(x\) are spread evenly over a window of width \(10^{-5}\) around 0.1, which plays the role of the uncontrolled perturbations of an experiment. Each run is integrated with the fourth-order Runge–Kutta method and time step 0.01 to a fixed time \(t^*\). The random variable is \(U=x(t^*)\), and its \(N\) values are the ensemble. No random-number generator is used anywhere. At \(t^*=20\) the standard deviation of the \(N\) values is \(2.9\times10^{-5}\), so the outcome is still predictable to four digits. At \(t^*=100\) the values are spread between about \(-19\) and \(19\) with standard deviation 7.85. The next sections use this ensemble, at the four fixed settings \(N=4000\), window \(10^{-5}\), time step 0.01 and \(t^*=100\), as the computable example of every definition.

The cumulative distribution function and the probability density function L18 1:58

The slides consider two events in the sample space of \(U\): \(\mathcal B=\{U\lt V_b\}\) and \(\mathcal E=\{V_a\le U\lt V_b\}\), for two values \(V_a\lt V_b\). (The slides write \(B\) and \(C\); \(\mathcal E\) is used here because \(\mathcal C\) denotes the conditions of the experiment.) Both probabilities follow from one function. The cumulative distribution function (CDF) of \(U\) is

\[ F(V)\equiv P\{U\lt V\}, \tag{7.3} \]

a function of the sample-space variable \(V\). The event \(\{U\lt V_b\}\) is the union of the two disjoint events \(\{U\lt V_a\}\) and \(\{V_a\le U\lt V_b\}\), and the probability of a union of disjoint events is the sum of their probabilities. Hence

\[ P\{V_a\le U\lt V_b\}=F(V_b)-F(V_a). \tag{7.4} \]

Three properties follow. \(F(-\infty)=0\), because \(\{U\lt-\infty\}\) is impossible. \(F(\infty)=1\), because \(\{U\lt\infty\}\) is certain. And \(F(V_b)\ge F(V_a)\) for \(V_b\gt V_a\), because the difference (7.4) is a probability and cannot be negative. The slide calls this third property "always increasing"; the precise word is non-decreasing, because \(F\) is constant over any interval of values that \(U\) never takes. The strict inequality in (7.3) is a convention of the slides, and it matters only when \(U\) takes one value with nonzero probability (the section "Standard PDFs" has an example).

When \(F\) is differentiable, the probability density function (PDF) of \(U\) is its derivative, and (7.4) becomes an integral:

\[ f(V)\equiv\frac{dF(V)}{dV},\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(V)\,dV=1,\qquad P\{V_a\le U\lt V_b\}=\int_{V_a}^{V_b}f(V)\,dV. \tag{7.5} \]

The PDF is non-negative, because \(F\) is non-decreasing. The normalization condition in (7.5) is \(F(\infty)-F(-\infty)=1\). The slide adds \(f(-\infty)=f(\infty)=0\). That holds for every example of this chapter, but it is not a consequence of (7.5): an integrable function can have narrower and narrower spikes far out on the axis without tending to zero. The statement that is guaranteed is the integral one: the probability of \(\{|U|\gt V\}\) tends to zero as \(V\to\infty\).

A density is not a probability. The probability that \(U\) lies in a short interval \([V,V+dV)\) is \(f(V)\,dV\). The PDF itself is a probability per unit of \(V\), and it has the units of \(1/U\). For the running case under the Gaussian model, the largest value of the PDF, at \(V=8\ \mathrm{m/s}\), is \(1/(2\sqrt{2\pi})=0.199\ \mathrm{s/m}\). If the same velocity is expressed in km/h, the standard deviation is 7.2 km/h and the largest value of the PDF is \(0.055\ \mathrm{h/km}\): the number changes with the unit, and a probability cannot. A PDF can also exceed 1. The Lorenz ensemble at \(t^*=20\) has its values spread over an interval of order \(10^{-4}\), so its PDF is of order \(10^{4}\) per unit of \(x\) there. A statement such as "\(f(8\ \mathrm{m/s})=0.2\), so the velocity is 8 m/s in 20 % of runs" is wrong for both reasons: the probability of any single value of a continuous variable is zero, and 0.2 is not a probability. Exercise 1 develops this.

The slide states that the PDF fully characterizes the random variable: it gives the probability of every event of the form (7.5). Two or more random variables with the same PDF are called identically distributed, or statistically identical. They need not be equal in any run, and they need not be related at all.

Estimating \(F\) and \(f\) from an ensemble. With \(N\) runs, the empirical CDF \(F_N(V)\) is the fraction of runs with \(U^{(n)}\lt V\). It is the relative-frequency estimate of (7.3), and it is a non-decreasing step function from 0 to 1. A histogram estimates \(f\): divide the \(V\) axis into bins of width \(\Delta V\), count the runs in each bin, and divide the count by \(N\Delta V\). For the Lorenz ensemble at \(t^*=100\), \(F_N(0)=0.489\), and a histogram with bins of width 2 has its largest value 0.052 per unit of \(x\), in the bin \([0,2)\). The histogram is an estimate with two errors: the bin width smooths the PDF, and each bin count has a sampling error. Figure 7.1 shows it.

Mean, variance and standardized moments L18 11:39

The PDF contains all the information about \(U\), but a few numbers computed from it are often all that a model or an experiment can use. The mean, or expectation, of \(U\), and more generally of a function \(Q(U)\), are

\[ \langle U\rangle\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}V\,f(V)\,dV,\qquad \langle Q(U)\rangle\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}Q(V)\,f(V)\,dV. \tag{7.6} \]

The angle brackets denote this expectation throughout the book. The integrand contains the sample-space variable \(V\), not \(U\): the integral weights each candidate value by its probability density. The fluctuation is \(u\equiv U-\langle U\rangle\). Its moments are the central moments of \(U\):

\[ \sigma_u^2=\mathrm{var}(U)\equiv\langle u^2\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(V-\langle U\rangle)^2f(V)\,dV,\qquad \mu_n\equiv\langle u^n\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(V-\langle U\rangle)^nf(V)\,dV. \tag{7.7} \]

The variance \(\sigma_u^2\) is the second central moment, and the standard deviation \(\sigma_u\), also called the root-mean-square (r.m.s.) fluctuation, has the units of \(U\). The first three central moments are \(\mu_0=1\) (the normalization condition), \(\mu_1=0\) (because \(\langle U-\langle U\rangle\rangle=\langle U\rangle-\langle U\rangle\)) and \(\mu_2=\sigma_u^2\). Expanding the square in (7.7) gives \(\sigma_u^2=\langle U^2\rangle-\langle U\rangle^2\), the subject of Exercise 8. For the running case, \(\langle U\rangle=8\ \mathrm{m/s}\) and \(\sigma_u=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\) (chapter 6 writes this r.m.s. value \(u_0\); the two symbols name the same number); the relative fluctuation \(\sigma_u/\langle U\rangle\) is 25 %.

Standardized variable and standardized PDF. The standardized random variable is \(\hat U\equiv(U-\langle U\rangle)/\sigma_u\), which has zero mean and unit variance and no units. Its PDF \(\hat f\) follows from the CDF. Because \(\sigma_u\gt0\), the event \(\{\hat U\lt\hat V\}\) is the event \(\{U\lt\langle U\rangle+\sigma_u\hat V\}\), so the CDF of \(\hat U\) is \(F(\langle U\rangle+\sigma_u\hat V)\). Differentiating with respect to \(\hat V\) with the chain rule gives the slide's result:

\[ \hat f(\hat V)=\sigma_u\,f(\langle U\rangle+\sigma_u\hat V),\qquad \hat\mu_n\equiv\frac{\mu_n}{\sigma_u^n}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat V^{\,n}\hat f(\hat V)\,d\hat V. \tag{7.8} \]

The factor \(\sigma_u\) in front of \(f\) is the Jacobian of the change of variable. It is what keeps \(\hat f\) normalized, and it removes the units: \(f\) has units of \(1/U\), \(\sigma_u\) has the units of \(U\). The standardized moments \(\hat\mu_n\) are therefore pure numbers that compare the shapes of distributions with different means and spreads. By construction \(\hat\mu_0=1\), \(\hat\mu_1=0\) and \(\hat\mu_2=1\). The third, \(\hat\mu_3\), is the skewness: it is zero for a PDF symmetric about its mean, positive when large positive fluctuations are more probable than large negative ones, and negative in the opposite case. The fourth, \(\hat\mu_4\), is called the kurtosis on the slides; this book calls it the flatness, the usual name in turbulence. It measures the weight of the tails: large values of \(\hat\mu_4\) mean that rare, large fluctuations contribute much of \(\langle u^4\rangle\).

When moments exist. The integrals (7.6) and (7.7) are numbers only if they converge. If \(f(V)\) decays like \(|V|^{-m}\) for large \(|V|\), then \(\langle|U|^n\rangle\) converges only if \(\int^\infty V^{n-m}\,dV\) does, that is if \(m\gt n+1\). A PDF with \(m=2\), such as \(f(V)=1/[\pi(1+V^2)]\) (the Cauchy distribution), is normalized but has no mean and no variance. Every distribution in Table 7.1 has tails that decay faster than any power, so all its moments exist. For measured turbulence data, the existence of a high moment is an assumption about tails that no finite sample can confirm: a sample always has finite moments. Exercise 4 shows what goes wrong when the assumption fails.

The characteristic function. The slide defines (calling it the "characterization function")

\[ \Psi(s)\equiv\langle e^{iUs}\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(V)\,e^{iVs}\,dV, \tag{7.9} \]

where \(i\) is the imaginary unit and \(s\) a real variable with the units of \(1/U\) (\(s\) is local to this paragraph; chapter 8 uses it for a time lag). Up to the sign convention and a factor \(2\pi\), (7.9) is a Fourier transform, so \(\Psi\) and \(f\) form a Fourier-transform pair and either determines the other. Two properties follow directly from (7.9): \(\Psi(0)=1\) by normalization, and \(|\Psi(s)|\le1\) because \(|e^{iVs}|=1\) and \(f\ge0\). When \(\langle|U|^n\rangle\) exists, differentiating under the integral \(n\) times at \(s=0\) gives \(\Psi^{(n)}(0)=i^n\langle U^n\rangle\): the moments are the Taylor coefficients of \(\Psi\) at the origin. For the Cauchy PDF, \(\Psi(s)=e^{-|s|}\), which has no derivative at \(s=0\), consistent with the missing mean.

The Lorenz ensemble. For \(U=x(t^*)\) at \(t^*=100\), the ensemble gives \(\langle U\rangle=0.16\), \(\sigma_u=7.85\), \(\hat\mu_3=0.00\) (the program prints \(-0.00\), a rounding of a small negative value) and \(\hat\mu_4=2.31\), with a standard error of 0.12 for the mean. The mean and the skewness are consistent with zero, as the symmetry argument of the section "Covariance and the correlation coefficient" leads one to expect. The flatness 2.31 is below the Gaussian value 3, because the values are confined to about \(|x|\lt19\) (Figure 7.1): a distribution with short tails has small flatness. The same ensemble at \(t^*=60\) gives \(\sigma_u=7.60\) and \(\hat\mu_4=2.48\). The variances at the two times, 57.8 and 61.6, differ by 3.8, about 2.4 times the standard error of their difference, 1.6, if the standard error 1.1 printed for \(t^*=100\) is taken for both times and the two estimates are treated as independent (the neighbour-correlation check of the section "Executable ensemble" supports independence across members, not across times; the assumption is a teaching simplification). The statistics of the ensemble still depend on \(t^*\) at these times. Whether they settle to values independent of \(t^*\) is the question of statistical stationarity, the subject of chapter 8.

What turbulence measurements and simulations show. The Gaussian distribution of the section "Standard PDFs" has \(\hat\mu_3=0\) and \(\hat\mu_4=3\), and these are the reference values against which turbulence statistics are compared. Two facts from the literature set the scope of the running case's Gaussian model.

  • The velocity itself is close to Gaussian, but not exactly. For the single-point PDF of one velocity component in simulations of homogeneous isotropic turbulence, Wilczek, Daitche and Friedrich (2011) find that the PDF is similar to a Gaussian but deviates from it significantly, notably with sub-Gaussian tails, that is, tails that fall off faster than those of a Gaussian, as other experiments and simulations had also found (arXiv preprint p. 14; summary p. 25). They also note that the usual argument for a Gaussian velocity, the central limit theorem applied to many independently fluctuating contributions, is "rather hand-waving", because the assumed independence is violated (p. 2). This chapter therefore offers no derivation of the Gaussian shape. The result concerns isotropic turbulence; a jet centreline is not isotropic, and the Gaussian model of the running case is an assumption, not a measurement.
  • Velocity derivatives are far from Gaussian. In direct numerical simulations of forced isotropic turbulence, Kerr (1983) finds the skewness of the derivative \(\partial u_1/\partial x_1\) (the derivative of a velocity component along its own direction) to be \(-0.505\pm0.005\) over his runs F11 to F27 (NASA TM 84407, p. 15), and states that it does not increase with Reynolds number (abstract, p. 1). The corresponding flatness rises from 3.39 to 5.11 as the Reynolds number based on the Taylor microscale, a length defined in later chapters, increases from 9.0 to 82.9 (Table 3, p. 35; the flatness \(F_1\) of that table equals the velocity-derivative flatness under isotropy, p. 17). Kerr notes that the limited range of length scales in a simulation restricts the Reynolds number to very low values (p. 2). Yeung, Sreenivasan and Pope (2018) state that longitudinal velocity gradients are negatively skewed, while transverse gradients are symmetric but more strongly non-Gaussian, and that the PDF of the dissipation rate has fluctuations up to several thousand times its mean (p. 064603-6).

The consequence for this chapter is a rule of use: the reference values \(\hat\mu_3=0\) and \(\hat\mu_4=3\) describe the Gaussian distribution, they are a fair first description of a single velocity component in isotropic turbulence, and they are wrong for velocity derivatives, whose flatness exceeds 3 and grows with Reynolds number in the cited simulations.

Standard PDFs: uniform, exponential, Gaussian, log-normal, gamma and discrete L18 16:33

The lecture's examples are distributions that can be written in closed form. Each is defined below with its parameters, and Table 7.1 collects their moments. The moments are standard results; each entry follows from (7.6)–(7.8) by direct integration, and the uniform mean and variance, the raw moments of the exponential distribution and the standardized moments of the Gaussian are derived in Exercises 7, 9 and 10. The remaining entries follow in the same way; for example the uniform fourth central moment is \(\frac{1}{b-a}\cdot\frac25\bigl(\frac{b-a}{2}\bigr)^5=(b-a)^4/80\), and dividing by \(\sigma_u^4=(b-a)^4/144\) gives the flatness \(144/80=9/5\).

Uniform. On an interval \([a,b)\), \(f(V)=1/(b-a)\) for \(a\le V\lt b\) and zero elsewhere. The CDF rises linearly from 0 at \(V=a\) to 1 at \(V=b\). Every subinterval of a given length has the same probability.

Exponential. With a scale \(\Lambda\gt0\), \(f(V)=\Lambda^{-1}e^{-V/\Lambda}\) for \(V\ge0\) and zero for \(V\lt0\); \(F(V)=1-e^{-V/\Lambda}\) for \(V\gt0\). (The slides write \(\lambda\); this book reserves the bare \(\lambda\) for the Taylor microscale of later chapters.) The PDF is largest at \(V=0\), where it equals \(1/\Lambda\), and falls by the factor \(e\) over each interval of length \(\Lambda\).

Gaussian (normal). With mean \(\langle U\rangle\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\),

\[ f(V)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(V-\langle U\rangle)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Bigr],\qquad \hat f(\hat V)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\hat V^2/2}. \tag{7.10} \]

The slides write the mean as \(\mu\). The second form is the standardized Gaussian, obtained from (7.8) with \(\sigma_u=\sigma\). Its CDF has no closed form; the slide figure marks \(F(-1)=0.159\) and \(F(-2)=0.023\) for the standardized variable, so 68.3 % of the probability lies within one standard deviation of the mean and 95.4 % within two. For the running case, \(P\{U\lt10\ \mathrm{m/s}\}=F(\hat V=1)=1-0.159=0.841\), the number used in the section "Repeated experiments", and \(P\{6\le U\lt10\ \mathrm{m/s}\}=0.683\). The same model gives \(P\{U\lt0\}=3.2\times10^{-5}\): flow reversal on the centreline once in about 32 000 runs. That number is a property of the model's tail four standard deviations from the mean, where the model has not been tested; by the first item of the previous list, a sub-Gaussian tail would make it smaller.

Log-normal. If \(U\) is Gaussian with mean \(\langle U\rangle\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), then \(Y=e^U\) is log-normally distributed. Its PDF is

\[ f_Y(y)=\frac{1}{y\,\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(\ln y-\langle U\rangle)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Bigr],\qquad y\gt0, \tag{7.11} \]

where \(y\) is the sample-space variable of \(Y\) (the slide's notation). The factor \(1/y\) is the Jacobian \(dU/dY\), by the same argument as (7.8). The slide figure shows three log-normal PDFs with \(\langle Y\rangle=1\) and \(\mathrm{var}(Y)=1/20\), \(1/2\) and 5. Since \(\langle Y\rangle=e^{\langle U\rangle+\sigma^2/2}\) and \(\mathrm{var}(Y)=(e^{\sigma^2}-1)\langle Y\rangle^2\), these correspond to \(\sigma^2=\ln(1+\mathrm{var}\,Y)=0.049\), 0.405 and 1.79. The slide's remark that large \(\sigma^2\) gives a PDF with a long tail is quantified by Table 7.1: at \(\mathrm{var}(Y)=5\) the skewness is 17.9 and the flatness 1833.

Gamma. With mean \(\langle U\rangle\gt0\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), the slide's form of the gamma distribution is

\[ f(V)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\Bigl(\frac{\alpha}{\langle U\rangle}\Bigr)^{\alpha}V^{\alpha-1}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\alpha V}{\langle U\rangle}\Bigr),\quad V\ge0,\qquad \alpha\equiv\Bigl(\frac{\langle U\rangle}{\sigma}\Bigr)^2, \tag{7.12} \]

where \(\Gamma(\alpha)=\int_0^\infty x^{\alpha-1}e^{-x}\,dx\) is the gamma function (here \(x\) is an integration variable, and \(\Gamma\) with an argument is not the diffusivity of chapter 6). At \(V\to0\) the PDF behaves like \(V^{\alpha-1}\). It is therefore zero at the origin for \(\alpha\gt1\), equal to \(1/\langle U\rangle\) for \(\alpha=1\) (the exponential distribution with \(\Lambda=\langle U\rangle\)) and infinite for \(\alpha\lt1\); the slide states the first and last cases as "large" and "small" \(\alpha\). The slide figure uses \(\langle U\rangle=1\) and \(\sigma^2=1/20\), \(1/2\) and 5, that is \(\alpha=20\), 2 and 0.2. The last of these is a PDF that is infinite at \(V=0\) and still integrates to 1: the value of a density at a point is not bounded by 1, and only its integrals are probabilities.

Discrete. Suppose that \(U\) takes the value \(a\) with probability \(p\) and the value \(b\gt a\) with probability \(1-p\). With (7.3), \(F(V)=0\) for \(V\le a\), \(F(V)=p\) for \(a\lt V\le b\), and \(F(V)=1\) for \(V\gt b\). (The slide writes \(a\lt V\lt b\) for the middle case and leaves \(V=b\) unassigned; the strict inequality in (7.3) gives \(F(b)=P\{U\lt b\}=p\).) In terms of the Heaviside step function \(H\), \(F(V)=p\,H(V-a)+(1-p)\,H(V-b)\), with the convention \(H(0)=0\) that the strict inequality in (7.3) requires. The CDF jumps at \(a\) and \(b\), so it has no ordinary derivative there. Its derivative in the sense of generalized functions is

\[ f(V)=p\,\delta(V-a)+(1-p)\,\delta(V-b), \tag{7.13} \]

where \(\delta\) is the Dirac delta function, defined by \(\int\delta(V-a)g(V)\,dV=g(a)\) for every continuous \(g\). With this rule, (7.6) gives \(\langle U\rangle=pa+(1-p)b\), and the probability of the single value \(a\) is \(p\), not zero. A random variable whose possible values form a finite or countably infinite set, each taken with nonzero probability, is a discrete random variable; its CDF is a step function and its PDF is a sum of delta functions, one per value. A random variable whose CDF is differentiable, as in every other example of this section, is a continuous one. A sure variable, which takes the value \(a\) with probability one, has \(F(V)=H(V-a)\) and \(f(V)=\delta(V-a)\). The Lorenz ensemble at \(t^*=20\), with standard deviation \(2.9\times10^{-5}\), is close to a sure variable; at \(t^*=100\) it is not.

Two random variables: joint CDF, joint PDF and marginals L19 2:18

Velocity is a vector, so a single random variable describes only one of its components. The slides write \(\mathbf U=\{U_1,U_2,U_3\}\) for the three velocity components at a fixed position and time, and \(\mathbf V=\{V_1,V_2,V_3\}\) for the corresponding sample-space variables. Everything needed here appears with two components. For the running case, \(U_1\) is the axial and \(U_2\) the radial velocity on the jet centreline. The joint CDF of \(U_1\) and \(U_2\) is the probability that both events occur:

\[ F_{12}(V_1,V_2)\equiv P\{U_1\lt V_1,\,U_2\lt V_2\},\qquad F_{12}(\infty,V_2)=F_2(V_2),\qquad F_{12}(V_1,\infty)=F_1(V_1). \tag{7.14} \]

It is the probability that the sample point \((U_1,U_2)\) lies in the quadrant below and to the left of \((V_1,V_2)\) in the \(V_1\)–\(V_2\) plane. As in one variable, \(F_{12}\) is non-decreasing in each argument, and \(F_{12}(-\infty,V_2)=P\{U_1\lt-\infty,U_2\lt V_2\}=0\). The two relations in (7.14) hold because \(\{U_1\lt\infty\}\) is certain, so the joint event reduces to \(\{U_2\lt V_2\}\). The CDF \(F_2(V_2)\) of the single variable \(U_2\), obtained this way from the joint CDF, is called the marginal CDF; the word "marginal" is used only when a joint distribution is present.

When \(F_{12}\) has a mixed second derivative, the joint PDF is

\[ f_{12}(V_1,V_2)\equiv\frac{\partial^2F_{12}(V_1,V_2)}{\partial V_1\,\partial V_2},\qquad P\{V_{1a}\le U_1\lt V_{1b},\,V_{2a}\le U_2\lt V_{2b}\}=\int_{V_{1a}}^{V_{1b}}\!\!\int_{V_{2a}}^{V_{2b}}f_{12}(V_1,V_2)\,dV_2\,dV_1. \tag{7.15} \]

It is non-negative and integrates to 1 over the plane. It has the units of \(1/(U_1U_2)\): for the jet, \(\mathrm{s^2/m^2}\). The marginal PDF of one variable is obtained by integrating the joint PDF over the other variable,

\[ f_2(V_2)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_{12}(V_1,V_2)\,dV_1,\qquad \langle Q(U_1,U_2)\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}Q(V_1,V_2)\,f_{12}(V_1,V_2)\,dV_1\,dV_2. \tag{7.16} \]

The second relation is the mean of a function of the two variables, the two-variable form of (7.6). The slide's version of it has two errors: its integrand is written with \(U_1,U_2\) in place of \(V_1,V_2\), and it ends with "\(=1\)". The lecture's explanation corrects the second; a mean equals 1 only for \(Q=1\), where (7.16) is the normalization condition. The means \(\langle U_1\rangle\), \(\langle U_2\rangle\) and the variances \(\langle u_1^2\rangle\), \(\langle u_2^2\rangle\), with the fluctuations \(u_1=U_1-\langle U_1\rangle\) and \(u_2=U_2-\langle U_2\rangle\), follow from (7.16), or equally from the marginal PDFs, because for a \(Q\) that depends on \(U_1\) alone the integral over \(V_2\) can be done first.

The slide illustrates (7.14) with a scatter plot of 100 samples of a jointly normal pair, with \(\langle U_1\rangle=2\), \(\langle U_2\rangle=1\), \(\langle u_1^2\rangle=1\), \(\langle u_2^2\rangle=5/16\) and correlation coefficient \(\rho_{12}=1/\sqrt5\) (the last is defined in the next section). Each sample is one point \((U_1^{(n)},U_2^{(n)})\) in the \(V_1\)–\(V_2\) plane, and the fraction of points in the quadrant of (7.14) estimates \(F_{12}\), as \(F_N\) estimated \(F\) in one variable. The marginal CDF counts points to the left of a vertical line, whatever their height.

A marginal PDF contains less information than the joint PDF. Two pairs can have the same two marginals and different joint PDFs; the next two sections show what the joint PDF adds: how the two variables vary together, and what one of them says about the other.

Covariance and the correlation coefficient L19 14:28

The covariance of \(U_1\) and \(U_2\) is the mixed second moment of their fluctuations, and the correlation coefficient is the covariance divided by the two standard deviations:

\[ \mathrm{cov}(U_1,U_2)=\langle u_1u_2\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(V_1-\langle U_1\rangle)(V_2-\langle U_2\rangle)\,f_{12}\,dV_1\,dV_2,\qquad \rho_{12}\equiv\frac{\langle u_1u_2\rangle}{\bigl(\langle u_1^2\rangle\langle u_2^2\rangle\bigr)^{1/2}}. \tag{7.17} \]

The correlation coefficient is defined only when both variances are nonzero. It has no units, and it does not change if either variable is multiplied by a positive constant or shifted by a constant. For the jet, \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle\) is a momentum flux per unit mass, in \(\mathrm{m^2/s^2}\); chapter 9 develops its role as a Reynolds stress.

The slide states that \(\rho_{12}\gt0\) when positive values of \(u_1\) are associated with positive values of \(u_2\), \(\rho_{12}\lt0\) when positive \(u_1\) goes with negative \(u_2\), and that in general \(-1\le\rho_{12}\le1\), by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. The derivation is short. For any real number \(\gamma\), the mean of a square is non-negative:

\[ 0\le\bigl\langle(u_1+\gamma u_2)^2\bigr\rangle=\langle u_1^2\rangle+2\gamma\langle u_1u_2\rangle+\gamma^2\langle u_2^2\rangle. \tag{7.18} \]

The right side is a quadratic in \(\gamma\) that never becomes negative, so it has at most one real root, and its discriminant is not positive: \(4\langle u_1u_2\rangle^2-4\langle u_1^2\rangle\langle u_2^2\rangle\le0\). Dividing by \(4\langle u_1^2\rangle\langle u_2^2\rangle\) gives \(\rho_{12}^2\le1\). Equality holds exactly when the quadratic has a double root \(\gamma_0\), that is when \(\langle(u_1+\gamma_0u_2)^2\rangle=0\). A non-negative quantity with zero mean is zero with probability one, so \(u_1=-\gamma_0u_2\) in every run except a set of probability zero. Hence \(|\rho_{12}|=1\) means that the fluctuations are exactly proportional, with \(\rho_{12}=1\) for a positive and \(\rho_{12}=-1\) for a negative factor of proportionality.

The interpretation of the values follows. \(\rho_{12}=0\) means that \(U_1\) and \(U_2\) are uncorrelated. \(\rho_{12}=1\) means that they are perfectly correlated, and \(\rho_{12}=-1\) that they are perfectly negatively correlated. The slide on the correlation coefficient calls the case \(\rho_{12}=-1\) "perfectly uncorrelated"; that is an error, and the course's own practice-problem slide (Exercise 11) uses the correct term, "perfectly negatively correlated". The inequality also bounds the covariance: \(|\langle u_1u_2\rangle|\le\sigma_1\sigma_2\), where \(\sigma_1=\langle u_1^2\rangle^{1/2}\) and \(\sigma_2=\langle u_2^2\rangle^{1/2}\). For the jet station, \(\sigma_1=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\); if the radial r.m.s. fluctuation is also taken as \(2\ \mathrm{m/s}\) (an assumption; chapter 6 gives only the axial value), no covariance of the two components can exceed \(4\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\) in magnitude.

Symmetry can force a zero covariance. On the axis of a round jet, the statistics are assumed unchanged by the reflection that reverses the radial direction, \(U_2\to-U_2\), with \(U_1\) unchanged. If \((U_1,U_2)\) and \((U_1,-U_2)\) have the same joint PDF, then \(\langle U_2\rangle=-\langle U_2\rangle\), so \(\langle U_2\rangle=0\) and \(u_2=U_2\); then \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle=\langle u_1(-u_2)\rangle\), so \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle=0\) and \(\rho_{12}=0\) on the axis. Away from the axis the reflection maps the point to a different point, the argument does not apply, and the jet has a nonzero shear stress there. The Lorenz equations have the same kind of symmetry. Replacing \((x,y,z)\) by \((-x,-y,z)\) in (7.1) leaves all three equations unchanged: the first and second change sign on both sides, and the third contains \(x\) and \(y\) only through the product \(xy\). If the ensemble's joint PDF has this symmetry, then \(\langle x\rangle=0\) and \(\langle xz\rangle-\langle x\rangle\langle z\rangle=-(\langle xz\rangle-\langle x\rangle\langle z\rangle)\), so the covariance of \(x\) and \(z\) is zero. The ensemble starts from a non-symmetric set of initial values near \(x=0.1\), so the symmetry is not exact at finite \(t^*\); at \(t^*=100\) the computation gives \(\rho_{xz}=0.023\), against a sampling standard error of about \(N^{-1/2}=0.016\), and \(\rho_{xy}=0.877\), a strong positive correlation that the symmetry allows.

Uncorrelated is not independent. The Lorenz ensemble at \(t^*=100\) shows how much a zero correlation can hide. Among the 815 members with \(|x|\lt2\), the mean of \(z\) is 18.2; among the 1015 members with \(|x|\gt10\), it is 31.9. Knowing \(x\) changes the expected value of \(z\) by 13.7, so the two variables are strongly dependent, although their correlation coefficient is 0.023. The correlation coefficient measures only the linear part of a relation: here \(z\) depends on \(|x|\), which is an even function of \(x\), and the positive and negative halves cancel in \(\langle xz\rangle\). The precise meaning of "dependent" is the subject of the next section. Exercise 2 gives a two-line example of the same kind.

Conditional PDFs and independence L19 18:12

The slide observes that samples with \(U_1\approx V_{1a}\) and those with \(U_1\approx V_{1b}\) are likely to have significantly different values of \(U_2\). The object that describes this is the PDF of \(U_2\) conditional on \(U_1=V_1\):

\[ f_{2|1}(V_2\mid V_1)\equiv\frac{f_{12}(V_1,V_2)}{f_1(V_1)},\qquad f_1(V_1)\gt0. \tag{7.19} \]

For fixed \(V_1\), it is the slice of the joint PDF along the line \(U_1=V_1\), divided by the marginal density of \(U_1\) at that value. The division normalizes the slice: integrating (7.19) over \(V_2\) and using (7.16) gives \(\int f_{2|1}\,dV_2=f_1(V_1)/f_1(V_1)=1\). The conditional PDF is non-negative and normalized, so it is a PDF in \(V_2\) for each \(V_1\). It is undefined where \(f_1(V_1)=0\): a value of \(U_1\) that never occurs supplies no condition. The conditional mean of a function \(Q(U_1,U_2)\), given \(U_1=V_1\), is its mean with the conditional PDF:

\[ \langle Q(U_1,U_2)\mid U_1=V_1\rangle\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}Q(V_1,V_2)\,f_{2|1}(V_2\mid V_1)\,dV_2. \tag{7.20} \]

The empirical counterparts are the ones the previous section used: the members of an ensemble with \(U_1\) in a narrow window around \(V_1\) are a sample from the conditional distribution, and their average estimates (7.20). The Lorenz numbers \(\langle z\mid|x|\lt2\rangle=18.2\) and \(\langle z\mid|x|\gt10\rangle=31.9\) are conditional means over windows of \(x\) rather than at single values.

Independence. Two random variables are independent if knowledge of the value of either provides no information about the other. In terms of PDFs, conditioning then has no effect, and the conditional and marginal PDFs are equal:

\[ f_{2|1}(V_2\mid V_1)=f_2(V_2)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f_{12}(V_1,V_2)=f_1(V_1)\,f_2(V_2). \tag{7.21} \]

The two forms are equivalent by (7.19). Independence implies zero correlation: with the product form, the double integral of (7.17) factorizes into \(\langle u_1\rangle\langle u_2\rangle=0\times0\). The converse is false, as the Lorenz pair \((x,z)\) shows.

The jointly normal case. There is one important family in which zero correlation does imply independence. Two variables are jointly normal (bivariate Gaussian) with means \(\langle U_1\rangle\), \(\langle U_2\rangle\), standard deviations \(\sigma_1\), \(\sigma_2\) and correlation coefficient \(\rho_{12}\) with \(|\rho_{12}|\lt1\), if

\[ f_{12}=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_1\sigma_2(1-\rho_{12}^2)^{1/2}}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{\hat V_1^2-2\rho_{12}\hat V_1\hat V_2+\hat V_2^2}{2(1-\rho_{12}^2)}\Bigr],\qquad \hat V_1=\frac{V_1-\langle U_1\rangle}{\sigma_1},\quad \hat V_2=\frac{V_2-\langle U_2\rangle}{\sigma_2}. \tag{7.22} \]

With \(\rho_{12}=0\), the exponent separates into \(-\hat V_1^2/2-\hat V_2^2/2\) and the prefactor into \(1/(\sigma_1\sqrt{2\pi})\times1/(\sigma_2\sqrt{2\pi})\), so \(f_{12}\) is the product of two Gaussian marginals (7.10), and (7.21) holds. The conditional PDF follows from (7.19) in two steps. First, complete the square in \(\hat V_2\): because \((\hat V_2-\rho_{12}\hat V_1)^2+(1-\rho_{12}^2)\hat V_1^2=\hat V_1^2-2\rho_{12}\hat V_1\hat V_2+\hat V_2^2\), the exponent of (7.22) is \(-(\hat V_2-\rho_{12}\hat V_1)^2/[2(1-\rho_{12}^2)]-\hat V_1^2/2\). Second, integrate (7.22) over \(V_2\) to obtain the marginal \(f_1\), as in the first relation of (7.16). With \(dV_2=\sigma_2\,d\hat V_2\), the first exponential integrates to \(\sigma_2[2\pi(1-\rho_{12}^2)]^{1/2}\) (the normalization of a Gaussian (7.10) with standard deviation \((1-\rho_{12}^2)^{1/2}\)), and the prefactor of (7.22) reduces to \(1/(\sigma_1\sqrt{2\pi})\): the marginal of \(U_1\) is the Gaussian (7.10) with mean \(\langle U_1\rangle\) and standard deviation \(\sigma_1\), whatever the value of \(\rho_{12}\). Dividing (7.22) by this \(f_1\) cancels the factor \(e^{-\hat V_1^2/2}/(\sigma_1\sqrt{2\pi})\) and leaves

\[ f_{2|1}(V_2\mid V_1)=\frac{1}{\sigma_2[2\pi(1-\rho_{12}^2)]^{1/2}}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(\hat V_2-\rho_{12}\hat V_1)^2}{2(1-\rho_{12}^2)}\Bigr], \tag{7.23} \]

a Gaussian in \(V_2\) centred at \(\hat V_2=\rho_{12}\hat V_1\) with standard deviation \(\sigma_2(1-\rho_{12}^2)^{1/2}\). Written in \(V_1\) and \(V_2\), \(U_2\) given \(U_1=V_1\) is Gaussian with

\[ \langle U_2\mid U_1=V_1\rangle=\langle U_2\rangle+\rho_{12}\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}\,(V_1-\langle U_1\rangle),\qquad \mathrm{var}(U_2\mid U_1=V_1)=\sigma_2^2\,(1-\rho_{12}^2). \tag{7.24} \]

The conditional mean is a straight line in \(V_1\), and the conditional variance does not depend on \(V_1\). Knowing \(U_1\) reduces the variance of \(U_2\) by the factor \(1-\rho_{12}^2\): by 4 % at \(\rho_{12}=0.2\), by 75 % at \(\rho_{12}=0.866\).

Because the system is sensitive to perturbations, the truncation error changes individual members completely; the check establishes that the ensemble statistics do not change by more than their sampling errors, not that any individual member is accurate. The teaching simplifications are explicit: one ensemble, one window shape (evenly spaced initial values of \(x\) only), one fixed time step, and standard errors computed as if the \(N\) members were independent samples.

import numpy as np

PR, R, BETA = 10.0, 28.0, 8.0/3.0     # Lorenz parameters, Eq. (7.1)


def rhs(s):
    """Right-hand side of (7.1); s has shape (3, number of members)."""
    x, y, z = s
    return np.array([PR*(y - x), R*x - y - x*z, x*y - BETA*z])


def advance(s, t_span, dt):
    """Advance s by t_span with the classical fourth-order Runge-Kutta."""
    if dt <= 0 or t_span < 0:
        raise ValueError("need dt > 0 and t_span >= 0")
    for _ in range(int(round(t_span/dt))):
        k1 = rhs(s)
        k2 = rhs(s + 0.5*dt*k1)
        k3 = rhs(s + 0.5*dt*k2)
        k4 = rhs(s + dt*k3)
        s = s + dt*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)/6
    return s


def moments(v):
    """Sample mean, standard deviation, skewness and flatness of v."""
    u = v - v.mean()
    sd = np.sqrt(np.mean(u**2))
    return v.mean(), sd, np.mean(u**3)/sd**3, np.mean(u**4)/sd**4

What the probabilistic description establishes and what it does not

The definitions of this chapter, (7.3)–(7.24), are exact mathematics. They hold for any random variable for which the stated derivatives and integrals exist. Three results were derived rather than defined: the standardized PDF (7.8), by a change of variable; the bound \(|\rho_{12}|\le1\) with its equality case, from (7.18); and the conditional mean and variance of the jointly normal pair (7.24). Two results are conditional on assumptions that a turbulent flow need not satisfy. Moments exist only if the tails of the PDF decay fast enough, and a finite sample cannot establish that. Zero correlation implies independence only for jointly normal variables, and the Lorenz pair \((x,z)\) is an example where it fails.

The probabilities and averages are ensemble quantities, defined over repetitions under conditions \(\mathcal C\). They depend on \(\mathcal C\) and, as the Lorenz ensemble shows between \(t^*=60\) and \(t^*=100\), on the time at which the variable is recorded. In practice a single long record is often all that is available, and its time average is used in place of the ensemble average. That substitution requires two assumptions that this chapter has not used: that the statistics do not change with time (statistical stationarity) and that a time average over one record converges to the ensemble average (ergodicity). Chapter 8 defines both.

The running case's Gaussian model is an assumption whose support and limits are set out in the section "Mean, variance and standardized moments". Its probabilities far in the tails, such as \(P\{U\lt0\}=3.2\times10^{-5}\), are untested extrapolations.

The Lorenz ensemble demonstrates a mechanism: deterministic equations, sensitive to perturbations, turn a narrow distribution of initial states into a broad distribution of later states. It is not a model of turbulence (Lorenz 1963, p. 135). Its PDF, its moments and its correlations are properties of that system and of the chosen ensemble; none of them is a statement about a turbulent flow. The chapter also leaves open whether the ensemble statistics become independent of the window width and of \(t^*\) as \(t^*\) grows; Exercise 6 discusses what evidence would be needed.

The joint and conditional distributions were developed for two velocity components at one point and one time. A turbulent velocity is a function of position and time, and its statistics include relations between values at different points and different times. Chapter 8 extends the description to random processes and random fields.

Exercise 1 (check an assumption). A student checks a computed PDF by verifying that \(f(V)\le1\) everywhere. (a) For the running case under the Gaussian model (\(\langle U\rangle=8\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(\sigma_u=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\)), compute the largest value of \(f\) in s/m and in h/km. (b) The Lorenz ensemble at \(t^*=20\) has standard deviation \(2.9\times10^{-5}\). Estimate the size of its PDF, assuming a single-peaked distribution with that spread. (c) Is \(f\le1\) a valid check? State a check that is valid.

Solution

(a) The Gaussian PDF (7.10) has its largest value at the mean: \(1/(\sigma_u\sqrt{2\pi})=1/(2\times2.5066)=0.199\ \mathrm{s/m}\). In km/h, \(\sigma_u=2\times3.6=7.2\ \mathrm{km/h}\), and the largest value is \(1/(7.2\times2.5066)=0.0554\ \mathrm{h/km}\). The two numbers differ by the factor 3.6 because \(f\) has units of 1/velocity, so its value depends on the unit; probabilities, which are integrals \(f\,dV\), do not.

(b) A single-peaked PDF of standard deviation \(\sigma\) has a peak of order \(1/\sigma\); for a Gaussian it is \(0.399/\sigma\). With \(\sigma=2.9\times10^{-5}\), the peak is of order \(0.4/2.9\times10^{-5}\approx1.4\times10^{4}\) per unit of \(x\). The PDF is correct and very much larger than 1.

Exercise 2 (counterexample). Let \(U_1\) be a standardized Gaussian variable (mean 0, variance 1) and \(U_2=U_1^2\). (a) Show that \(U_1\) and \(U_2\) are uncorrelated. (b) Show that they are not independent, using the conditional PDF of \(U_2\) given \(U_1=V_1\). (c) Explain why this does not contradict the statement that jointly normal uncorrelated variables are independent.

Solution

(a) \(\langle U_1\rangle=0\) and \(\langle U_2\rangle=\langle U_1^2\rangle=1\). The covariance is \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle=\langle U_1(U_1^2-1)\rangle=\langle U_1^3\rangle-\langle U_1\rangle=\hat\mu_3-0=0\), because the Gaussian is symmetric and its skewness is zero. Both variances are nonzero (\(\langle u_2^2\rangle=\langle U_1^4\rangle-1=3-1=2\)), so \(\rho_{12}=0\).

(b) Given \(U_1=V_1\), \(U_2\) is the sure variable \(V_1^2\): \(f_{2|1}(V_2\mid V_1)=\delta(V_2-V_1^2)\). This depends on \(V_1\), whereas the marginal \(f_2\) does not, so (7.21) fails and the variables are dependent. The conditional mean \(\langle U_2\mid U_1=V_1\rangle=V_1^2\) changes from 0 to 4 as \(V_1\) goes from 0 to 2. Knowing \(U_1\) determines \(U_2\) exactly, although their correlation is zero.

(c) The pair \((U_1,U_1^2)\) is not jointly normal: \(U_2\ge0\) always, so its marginal is not Gaussian, and the pair lies on a parabola in the \(V_1\)–\(V_2\) plane, not in the elliptical cloud of (7.22). The implication "uncorrelated, hence independent" was derived from the factorization of (7.22) at \(\rho_{12}=0\), and it is available only for that family. The Lorenz pair \((x,z)\) of the chapter is the same phenomenon in a computed system: \(z\) depends on \(|x|\), an even function of \(x\).

Exercise 3 (compute). For a jointly normal pair with \(\langle U_1\rangle=1\), \(\langle U_2\rangle=-1\), \(\langle u_1^2\rangle=2\), \(\langle u_2^2\rangle=12\), \(\rho_{12}=-\sqrt{2/3}\) (the caption values of the lecture's conditional-PDF figure): (a) compute the covariance; (b) compute the conditional mean and standard deviation of \(U_2\) at \(V_1=1\) and \(V_1=5\); (c) compute the peak heights of the conditional PDFs and of the slices \(f_{12}(V_1,\cdot)\) at the same two values; (d) decide which of the two the figure shows.

Solution

(a) \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle=\rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2=-\sqrt{2/3}\times\sqrt2\times\sqrt{12}=-\sqrt{16}=-4\).

(b) By (7.24), the slope is \(\rho_{12}\sigma_2/\sigma_1=\langle u_1u_2\rangle/\sigma_1^2=-4/2=-2\), so \(\langle U_2\mid V_1\rangle=-1-2(V_1-1)\): \(-1\) at \(V_1=1\) and \(-9\) at \(V_1=5\). The conditional variance is \(12(1-2/3)=4\), standard deviation 2, at both values.

(c) Each conditional PDF is Gaussian with standard deviation 2, so its peak is \(1/(2\sqrt{2\pi})=0.199\). The marginal of \(U_1\) is Gaussian with mean 1 and variance 2: \(f_1(1)=1/\sqrt{4\pi}=0.282\) and \(f_1(5)=0.282\,e^{-16/4}=0.282\times0.0183=0.00517\). The joint-PDF slices are \(f_1f_{2|1}\), with peaks \(0.282\times0.199=0.056\) and \(0.00517\times0.199=0.00103\), in a ratio \(e^4=54.6\).

(d) The figure's two curves have the same height, about 0.2, and peak at \(-1\) and \(-9\). They are the conditional PDFs \(f_{2|1}(V_2\mid1)\) and \(f_{2|1}(V_2\mid5)\). Slices of \(f_{12}\) would have different heights and would both be below 0.06. The general lesson: a conditional PDF is a slice of the joint PDF rescaled to unit area, and the rescaling factor \(1/f_1(V_1)\) is large where \(V_1\) is improbable.

Exercise 4 (counterexample). The Cauchy distribution has \(f(V)=1/[\pi(1+V^2)]\). (a) Show that it is normalized and symmetric about 0. (b) Show that \(\langle|U|\rangle\) is infinite, so the mean (7.6) does not exist. (c) A student estimates the "mean" from samples and reports that it is 0 because the PDF is symmetric. What does an average of \(N\) samples actually do as \(N\) grows? (The average of \(N\) independent Cauchy samples has itself the Cauchy distribution; use this fact.) (d) What does this example imply for measured turbulence statistics?

Solution

(a) \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dV/[\pi(1+V^2)]=[\arctan V]_{-\infty}^{\infty}/\pi=(\pi/2+\pi/2)/\pi=1\), and \(f(-V)=f(V)\).

(b) \(\langle|U|\rangle=2\int_0^{\infty}V\,dV/[\pi(1+V^2)]=[\ln(1+V^2)]_0^{\infty}/\pi=\infty\). The tail decays as \(|V|^{-2}\), and the condition of the text, decay faster than \(|V|^{-(n+1)}\) with \(n=1\), fails. Symmetry does not rescue the mean: the integral \(\int V f\,dV\) over \((-A,B)\) tends to \((\ln(1+B^2)-\ln(1+A^2))/(2\pi)\), whose limit depends on how \(A\) and \(B\) grow, so the integral has no value.

(c) The average of \(N\) samples has the same Cauchy distribution as one sample, whatever \(N\). It does not settle down: with \(N=10^{6}\) samples, the probability that the average exceeds 1 in magnitude is still \(1-2\arctan(1)/\pi=0.5\). The report "0" is a statement about the symmetry of \(f\), not an estimate of a mean.

(d) Every finite sample has a finite average, so a computed average does not show that the mean exists. Whether a moment exists is a property of the tail of the PDF. For the heavy-tailed quantities of turbulence, such as the dissipation rate, whose fluctuations reach several thousand times the mean in the simulations cited in the chapter, a high moment estimated from data is reliable only if its estimate is checked for convergence as the sample grows, for example by comparing halves of the sample.

Exercise 5 (design). An experimenter plans repeated runs of the jet experiment (\(\langle U\rangle=8\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(\sigma_u=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\)). (a) How many independent runs \(N\) are needed to estimate \(P\{U\lt10\ \mathrm{m/s}\}\approx0.841\) with a standard error of 0.01? (b) To estimate the mean with a standard error of 0.05 m/s? (c) Suppose the mean and variance are known exactly and \(\hat\mu_4\) is estimated as the sample average of \(\hat U^4\). For a Gaussian \(U\), how many runs give a standard error of 0.1? Use \(\hat\mu_8=105\) for the Gaussian (Exercise 10). (d) How does the answer to (c) change for a strongly non-Gaussian quantity such as a velocity derivative?

Solution

(a) The indicator of the event (1 if \(U\lt10\), 0 otherwise) has mean \(p=0.841\) and variance \(p(1-p)=0.134\). The relative frequency in \(N\) independent runs has standard error \((p(1-p)/N)^{1/2}\). Setting this to 0.01 gives \(N=0.134/10^{-4}=1340\).

(b) The sample mean has standard error \(\sigma_u/N^{1/2}\); \(2/N^{1/2}=0.05\) gives \(N=1600\).

(c) The variance of \(\hat U^4\) is \(\langle\hat U^8\rangle-\langle\hat U^4\rangle^2=\hat\mu_8-\hat\mu_4^2=105-9=96\). The sample average of \(N\) independent values has standard error \((96/N)^{1/2}\); setting it to 0.1 gives \(N=9600\). If the mean and variance are also estimated from the same sample, the standard error changes; the known-moment case is the simplest version of the calculation.

(d) The required \(N\) is \((\hat\mu_8-\hat\mu_4^2)/0.1^2\), and \(\hat\mu_8\) grows rapidly with the weight of the tails. For a velocity derivative whose flatness is about 5 (Kerr's highest Reynolds number), \(\hat\mu_8\) is much larger than 105, and the sample must be correspondingly larger; for an exponential variable, \(\hat\mu_4=9\), and the standardized eighth moment is 14 833, so the same accuracy needs about \(1.5\times10^{6}\) runs. The design rule: the accuracy of an estimated moment of order \(n\) is set by the moment of order \(2n\), and heavy tails make high moments expensive.

Exercise 6 (judge a claim). A report states: "We computed the PDF of \(x\) for Lorenz's equations at \(r=28\) from an ensemble of 4000 runs at \(t^*=60\). Its flatness is 2.48. Because the equations are deterministic and our time step is small, this is the flatness of the Lorenz system." Judge each part of the claim, using the chapter's output, and state what evidence would support a claim about "the Lorenz system".

Solution

The number 2.48 is correct for the stated ensemble at \(t^*=60\). Three parts of the claim do not follow. First, the statistics depend on \(t^*\): at \(t^*=100\) the same ensemble gives 2.31, and its variance changes from 57.8 to 61.6, about 2.4 standard errors of the difference under the independence assumption stated in the section on moments. A flatness "of the system" would have to be independent of \(t^*\), which is the property of statistical stationarity (chapter 8). Second, a small time step does not make each member accurate: halving the step changes members by up to 32.4 at \(t^*=100\). What the step-halving check supports is that the statistics are insensitive to the step (2.31 against 2.28), not that the trajectories are correct. Third, the ensemble depends on the chosen window of initial states; a statement about the system requires that the statistics be insensitive to that choice too.

Evidence for a system property would be: the statistics at several \(t^*\) (say 100, 150, 200) agreeing within their standard errors; the same statistics from ensembles with different windows and different initial points; agreement under step halving; and, if one long run is to replace the ensemble, agreement of its time averages with the ensemble averages, which tests ergodicity. Even then the result concerns Lorenz's model, not turbulence (Lorenz 1963, p. 135).

Exercise 7 (derive; adapted from the course practice problems, L57 16:18). For the uniform distribution, \(f(V)=1/(b-a)\) for \(a\le V\lt b\) and \(f(V)=0\) for \(V\lt a\) and \(V\ge b\), show that \(\mathrm{var}(U)=\tfrac1{12}(b-a)^2\).

The integrals in (7.6) and (7.7) run over the whole axis; because \(f=0\) outside \([a,b)\), they reduce to integrals from \(a\) to \(b\) for any \(a\), including negative \(a\). (An integral from 0 to \(\infty\) gives the same result only when \(a\ge0\).) The mean is \(\langle U\rangle=\int_a^bV\,dV/(b-a)=(b^2-a^2)/[2(b-a)]=(a+b)/2\). With the substitution \(w=V-(a+b)/2\), which runs from \(-(b-a)/2\) to \((b-a)/2\),

\[ \mathrm{var}(U)=\frac{1}{b-a}\int_{-(b-a)/2}^{(b-a)/2}w^2\,dw=\frac{1}{b-a}\cdot\frac{2}{3}\Bigl(\frac{b-a}{2}\Bigr)^3=\frac{(b-a)^2}{12}. \]

Exercise 8 (derive; adapted from the course practice problems, L58 0:21). With \(\langle U\rangle=\int V f(V)\,dV\) and \(\mathrm{var}(U)=\int(V-\langle U\rangle)^2f(V)\,dV\), both over the whole axis, show that \(\mathrm{var}(U)=\langle U^2\rangle-\langle U\rangle^2\). (The slide calls \(U\) a "stationary random variable"; stationarity is a property of a random process, defined in chapter 8, and is not needed here.)

Solution

Expand the square and integrate term by term, assuming \(\langle U^2\rangle\) exists:

\[ \int(V^2-2V\langle U\rangle+\langle U\rangle^2)f\,dV=\langle U^2\rangle-2\langle U\rangle\int Vf\,dV+\langle U\rangle^2\int f\,dV=\langle U^2\rangle-2\langle U\rangle^2+\langle U\rangle^2. \]

The second step uses that \(\langle U\rangle\) is a constant, the definition of the mean, and the normalization \(\int f\,dV=1\). The result is \(\langle U^2\rangle-\langle U\rangle^2\). Since the variance is non-negative, \(\langle U^2\rangle\ge\langle U\rangle^2\), with equality only for a sure variable. For the running case, \(\langle U^2\rangle=4+64=68\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\). The identity is exact but, used on data, it subtracts two large numbers when \(\sigma_u\ll|\langle U\rangle|\); computing the variance from the fluctuations, as the code does, avoids that loss of precision.

Exercise 9 (derive; adapted from the course practice problems, L60 17:39). For the exponential distribution \(f(V)=\Lambda^{-1}\exp(-V/\Lambda)\) for \(V\ge0\), \(f(V)=0\) for \(V\lt0\), with \(\Lambda\gt0\) (the slide writes \(\lambda\)): (a) check the normalization condition; (b) show \(\langle U\rangle=\Lambda\); (c) show \(\langle U^n\rangle=n!\,\Lambda^n\); (d) show \(F(V)=1-\exp(-V/\Lambda)\) for \(V\gt0\) and \(F(V)=0\) for \(V\le0\); (e) show \(P\{U\ge a\Lambda\}=e^{-a}\) for \(a\ge0\).

Solution

(a) With \(w=V/\Lambda\), \(\int_0^\infty\Lambda^{-1}e^{-V/\Lambda}dV=\int_0^\infty e^{-w}dw=1\).

(c), which contains (b) as \(n=1\). Let \(M_n=\langle U^n\rangle=\int_0^\infty V^n\Lambda^{-1}e^{-V/\Lambda}dV\). Integrate by parts with \(d(-e^{-V/\Lambda})=\Lambda^{-1}e^{-V/\Lambda}dV\): \(M_n=[-V^ne^{-V/\Lambda}]_0^\infty+\int_0^\infty nV^{n-1}e^{-V/\Lambda}dV\). The boundary term is zero for \(n\ge1\), because \(e^{-V/\Lambda}\) decays faster than any power grows. The remaining integral is \(n\Lambda M_{n-1}\). So \(M_n=n\Lambda M_{n-1}\) with \(M_0=1\), and by induction \(M_n=n!\,\Lambda^n\). In particular \(\langle U\rangle=\Lambda\) and \(\langle U^2\rangle=2\Lambda^2\), so \(\mathrm{var}(U)=\Lambda^2\) by Exercise 8, as Table 7.1 states.

(d) For \(V\le0\), \(F(V)=P\{U\lt V\}=0\) because \(f=0\) there. For \(V\gt0\), \(F(V)=\int_0^V\Lambda^{-1}e^{-V'/\Lambda}dV'=1-e^{-V/\Lambda}\).

(e) \(P\{U\ge a\Lambda\}=1-P\{U\lt a\Lambda\}=1-F(a\Lambda)=e^{-a}\) for \(a\gt0\), and for \(a=0\) both sides equal 1. The probability that \(U\) exceeds its mean (\(a=1\)) is \(e^{-1}=0.368\), less than one half: the distribution has positive skewness (2, Table 7.1).

Exercise 10 (derive; adapted from the course practice problems, T1 24:35). By considering \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d}{dV}\Bigl(\frac{V^n}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-V^2/2}\Bigr)dV\), obtain a recurrence relation for the standardized moments \(\hat\mu_n\) of the Gaussian distribution. The integral of a derivative is the difference of the function's values at the ends, and \(V^ne^{-V^2/2}\to0\) as \(|V|\to\infty\) for every \(n\ge0\), so the integral is zero. Differentiating inside instead gives

\[ 0=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigl(nV^{n-1}-V^{n+1}\bigr)\frac{e^{-V^2/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,dV=n\hat\mu_{n-1}-\hat\mu_{n+1}, \]

because \(e^{-V^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}\) is the standardized Gaussian PDF (7.10). The recurrence is \(\hat\mu_{n+1}=n\,\hat\mu_{n-1}\), for \(n\ge1\), with \(\hat\mu_0=1\) and \(\hat\mu_1=0\). Odd moments: \(\hat\mu_3=2\hat\mu_1=0\), and each further odd moment is a multiple of the previous one, so all are zero. Even moments: \(\hat\mu_2=1\cdot\hat\mu_0=1\), \(\hat\mu_4=3\hat\mu_2=3\), \(\hat\mu_6=5\hat\mu_4=15\), \(\hat\mu_8=7\hat\mu_6=105\); in general \(\hat\mu_{2k}=(2k-1)(2k-3)\cdots1=(2k)!/(2^kk!)\). The value \(\hat\mu_8=105\) is the one used in Exercise 5.

Exercise 11 (derive; adapted from the course practice problems, T1 31:52). Let \(U_1\) and \(U_3\) be uncorrelated random variables with nonzero variances, and let \(U_2=a+bU_1+cU_3\), where \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\) are constants (the slide's letters; they are unrelated to the interval ends \(a\), \(b\) of the uniform distribution), not both \(b\) and \(c\) zero. Show that

\[ \rho_{12}=\frac{b}{\bigl(b^2+c^2\langle u_3^2\rangle/\langle u_1^2\rangle\bigr)^{1/2}}, \]

and hence that \(U_1\) and \(U_2\) are (1) uncorrelated if \(b=0\) and \(c\ne0\); (2) perfectly correlated, \(\rho_{12}=1\), if \(c=0\) and \(b\gt0\); (3) perfectly negatively correlated, \(\rho_{12}=-1\), if \(c=0\) and \(b\lt0\).

Solution

Taking means, \(\langle U_2\rangle=a+b\langle U_1\rangle+c\langle U_3\rangle\), so the fluctuation is \(u_2=bu_1+cu_3\): the constant \(a\) drops out. Then \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle=b\langle u_1^2\rangle+c\langle u_1u_3\rangle=b\langle u_1^2\rangle\), because \(U_1\) and \(U_3\) are uncorrelated. Similarly \(\langle u_2^2\rangle=b^2\langle u_1^2\rangle+2bc\langle u_1u_3\rangle+c^2\langle u_3^2\rangle=b^2\langle u_1^2\rangle+c^2\langle u_3^2\rangle\), which is positive because \(b\) and \(c\) are not both zero and the variances are nonzero. By (7.17),

\[ \rho_{12}=\frac{b\langle u_1^2\rangle}{\bigl(\langle u_1^2\rangle(b^2\langle u_1^2\rangle+c^2\langle u_3^2\rangle)\bigr)^{1/2}}=\frac{b}{\bigl(b^2+c^2\langle u_3^2\rangle/\langle u_1^2\rangle\bigr)^{1/2}}. \]

(1) \(b=0\), \(c\ne0\): the numerator is zero and the denominator positive, so \(\rho_{12}=0\). (2) \(c=0\), \(b\gt0\): \(\rho_{12}=b/|b|=1\). (3) \(c=0\), \(b\lt0\): \(\rho_{12}=b/|b|=-1\). Cases (2) and (3) are the equality cases of (7.18): \(u_2=bu_1\) exactly. The hypothesis of nonzero variances is needed: if \(\langle u_1^2\rangle=0\), \(\rho_{12}\) is undefined. In case (1), \(U_1\) and \(U_2\) are uncorrelated but need not be independent, because uncorrelated \(U_1\) and \(U_3\) need not be independent.
