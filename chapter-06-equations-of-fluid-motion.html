Equations of fluid motion: continuity, momentum, pressure, scalars and vorticity

A hot-wire probe on the centreline of an air jet records a velocity that varies between about 1.5 and 6 m/s within 0.3 s, while its mean stays constant. Before that signal can be modelled, three questions must be settled: which equations govern it, under which approximations they hold for this flow, and which other experiments obey the same equations after rescaling. This chapter derives the equations for an incompressible Newtonian fluid, checks each approximation against a laboratory air jet with numbers, and derives three consequences that later chapters use repeatedly: pressure is determined by the velocity field through a Poisson equation, vorticity is amplified by stretching only in three dimensions, and two flows at the same Reynolds number obey identical scaled equations.

Grounded in three recordings of the NPTEL course Essentials of Turbulence (Prof. Abhilash J. Chandy, IIT Bombay): Lecture 14, Lecture 15 and Lecture 16. The slides of this module name one source: S. B. Pope, Turbulent Flows (Cambridge University Press, 2000). No page, equation number or value in this chapter is taken from that book. Lecture material: the centreline velocity record and the mean profile of a turbulent jet; the continuum hypothesis, Eulerian and Lagrangian fields, the material derivative and material surfaces; the continuity equation and its specific-volume form; the momentum equation, the Newtonian stress, the Navier–Stokes and Euler equations and their boundary conditions; the pressure Poisson equation; the conserved passive scalar; the vorticity equation and vortex stretching; the rates of strain and rotation; Reynolds-number similarity; invariance under translation, rotation, reflection, time reversal and uniform motion of the frame. Research extensions: the U.S. Standard Atmosphere, 1976 (air properties, the mean free path and the molecular number density), Hunt, Wray and Moin (1988, the velocity-gradient invariant that sets the sign of the pressure Laplacian) and Boffetta and Ecke (2012, the enstrophy bound of two-dimensional turbulence). The objects of the chapter and the running case

The formal model of this chapter is a fluid that obeys four idealizations, each introduced and tested in a section below: it is a continuum, its density \(\rho\) is constant, it is Newtonian with constant dynamic viscosity \(\mu\), and it carries a scalar \(\phi\) that neither changes the flow nor is created or destroyed. The fields are the velocity \(\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)\), with components \(U_i\), the pressure \(p(\mathbf x,t)\) and the scalar \(\phi(\mathbf x,t)\), where \(\mathbf x=(x_1,x_2,x_3)\) is the position in a Cartesian frame and \(t\) the time. A repeated index in a product is summed over 1, 2, 3. The kinematic viscosity is \(\nu=\mu/\rho\) and the molecular diffusivity of the scalar is \(\Gamma\), both in \(\mathrm{m^2/s}\). Upper case denotes the full velocity, as on the slides; lower case \(u_0\) is reserved below for the size of the velocity fluctuations.

The running case is a round jet of air issuing from a nozzle into still air. Two places in the jet are used. At the nozzle, the exit velocity is \(\mathcal U=56\ \mathrm{m/s}\) and the nozzle diameter is \(\mathcal L=0.025\ \mathrm m\). At a station far downstream, the mean velocity on the centreline is \(\langle U_1\rangle=8\ \mathrm{m/s}\), the root-mean-square velocity fluctuation is \(u_0=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\), and the energy-containing eddies are about \(\ell_0=0.1\ \mathrm m\) across. The values \(u_0\) and \(\ell_0\) are those of chapter 5, exercise 4, where they were written \(u\) and \(\ell\); the mean velocity 8 m/s is chosen here so that \(u_0/\langle U_1\rangle\) equals the 25 % of the next section. These numbers are chosen for this chapter; they are not measurements of a particular jet.

The air properties are the sea-level values of the U.S. Standard Atmosphere, 1976 (Table 10, p. 20): temperature 288.15 K, density \(\rho=1.2250\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\), dynamic viscosity \(\mu=1.7894\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{kg/(m\,s)}\), kinematic viscosity \(\nu=1.4607\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), speed of sound \(c=340.29\ \mathrm{m/s}\). Chapter 5 used the lecture's value \(\nu=1.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), which is 2.7 % larger. With these values the nozzle Reynolds number is \(\mathrm{Re}=\mathcal U\mathcal L/\nu=9.6\times10^{4}\). At the station, the large-eddy Reynolds number is \(\mathrm{Re}_{\ell}=u_0\ell_0/\nu=1.37\times10^{4}\), the inviscid estimate of chapter 5 with unit constant gives the dissipation rate \(\varepsilon\sim u_0^3/\ell_0=80\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\), and the Kolmogorov scales of chapter 5 are \(\eta=(\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}=79\ \mu\mathrm m\) and \(\tau_\eta=(\nu/\varepsilon)^{1/2}=0.43\ \mathrm{ms}\) (chapter 5 found \(\eta=81\ \mu\mathrm m\) with its larger \(\nu\)).

The first is the time history of the axial velocity component \(U_1(t)\) at one point on the centreline of a jet; the slide caption attributes it to an experiment of Tong and Warhaft (1995). No value in this chapter is taken from that paper; the values below are read from the slide. In the axis convention of the slide, direction 1 is the jet axis, direction 2 the radial (cross-stream) direction; the caption names these two, and direction 3 is the remaining, azimuthal, direction. Over 0.3 s the trace moves between about 1.5 and 6 m/s around a mean of about 3.4 m/s, values read from the slide figure. The slide summarizes it as fluctuations of 25 % about the mean \(\langle U_1\rangle\), without stating whether 25 % is the root-mean-square value; the running case takes it as the root-mean-square value, \(u_0/\langle U_1\rangle=2/8\). Here \(\langle\cdot\rangle\) denotes the mean; chapter 7 defines it as an expectation, and for a record like this one it is estimated by the average over a long time.

The slide adds that the record is far from periodic, varies on a wide range of time scales, and is in a sense "stable": it shows no very large excursions and no long periods far from its mean.

The second measurement is the mean axial velocity across a jet at Reynolds number 95 500, normalized by its centreline value \(\langle U_1\rangle_0\) and plotted against \(x_2/x_1\), the radial coordinate divided by the distance from the nozzle; the caption attributes it to Hussein, Capp and George (1994); the Reynolds number and the profile shape are taken from the slide, not from the paper. The question for this chapter is narrower: what equations does \(\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)\) obey? The equations derived below are deterministic. They contain no random terms, and the irregular record is a solution of them. The statistical description begins in chapter 7.

The continuum hypothesis and the two descriptions of a flow L14 11:52

The continuum hypothesis. The equations treat velocity, density and pressure as smooth functions of position. That treatment is valid when the molecular length scales are much smaller than the smallest length over which these fields change. The ratio is the Knudsen number,

\[ \mathrm{Kn}=\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm m}}{\ell_{g}},\qquad \text{continuum valid for }\mathrm{Kn}\ll1, \tag{6.1} \]

where \(\lambda_{\mathrm m}\) is the mean free path, the mean distance a molecule travels between collisions, and \(\ell_g\) is the smallest length scale of the flow. (The slide writes \(\lambda\) for the mean free path; this book reserves \(\lambda\) for the Taylor microscale of later chapters.) The slide gives \(\lambda=10^{-9}\ \mathrm m\) for air at atmospheric conditions, calls it both the "average spacing between molecules" and the "mean free path", and takes the smallest geometric length as typically \(\ell_g\gt10^{-4}\ \mathrm m\) (the slide writes \(l\)), so that \(\mathrm{Kn}\lt10^{-5}\).

The two quantities named on the slide differ by a factor of about 20. The U.S. Standard Atmosphere defines the mean free path as the mean distance travelled by the particles between successive collisions (§1.3.8, eq. (47), p. 17) and tabulates the sea-level value \(6.6328\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm m\) (Table 10, p. 20). The same table gives the number density of molecules, \(2.5470\times10^{25}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\); the volume per molecule is its reciprocal, and the mean spacing is the cube root of that volume, \((2.547\times10^{25})^{-1/3}\ \mathrm m=3.4\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm m\). The slide's value \(10^{-9}\ \mathrm m\) has the order of magnitude of the spacing; the mean free path is 66 nm. With the mean free path and the slide's length, \(\mathrm{Kn}=6.6\times10^{-4}\) instead of \(10^{-5}\). The conclusion \(\mathrm{Kn}\ll1\) holds with either value.

The smallest length of a turbulent flow is not a geometric length of the apparatus: chapter 5 showed that the velocity varies over lengths down to the Kolmogorov scale \(\eta\). At the running-case station \(\eta=79\ \mu\mathrm m\), so \(\mathrm{Kn}=6.6\times10^{-8}/7.9\times10^{-5}=8.4\times10^{-4}\). The continuum hypothesis therefore holds at the smallest scales of this jet. It fails when the mean free path is comparable to the smallest length of the flow.

Eulerian and Lagrangian fields. An Eulerian field gives a property at a fixed position: \(\rho(\mathbf x,t)\), \(\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)\). A probe fixed in the jet records Eulerian data. A Lagrangian field follows a fluid particle. Label each particle by its position \(\mathbf Y\) at a reference time \(t_0\). Its position at time \(t\) is \(\mathbf X^{+}(t,\mathbf Y)\), with \(\mathbf X^{+}(t_0,\mathbf Y)=\mathbf Y\), and its density and velocity are \(\rho^{+}(t,\mathbf Y)\) and \(\mathbf U^{+}(t,\mathbf Y)\); the superscript \(+\) marks a Lagrangian quantity, as on the slides. The two descriptions are connected by \(\partial\mathbf X^{+}/\partial t=\mathbf U^{+}(t,\mathbf Y)=\mathbf U(\mathbf X^{+}(t,\mathbf Y),t)\): the particle moves with the local fluid velocity.

The material derivative. The rate of change of an Eulerian field \(Q(\mathbf x,t)\) following a particle is found by the chain rule, \(\frac{d}{dt}Q(\mathbf X^{+}(t,\mathbf Y),t)=\partial Q/\partial t+U_j\,\partial Q/\partial x_j\). This defines the material (or substantial) derivative,

\[ \frac{D}{Dt}\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\mathbf U\cdot\nabla. \tag{6.2} \]

The first term is the local rate of change at a fixed point; the second, the convective term, is the change seen by a particle because it moves to a place where \(Q\) differs. A probe at the station can record a large \(\partial U_1/\partial t\) even if every fluid particle kept its velocity, because eddies are carried past it at the mean speed of 8 m/s.

Material lines, surfaces and volumes. A material point is a fluid particle, defined by its position \(\mathbf Y\) at \(t_0\) and by its motion with the local velocity. A material line, surface or volume is a set of material points. Every point of a material surface \(S(t)\) moves with the local fluid velocity, so no fluid crosses it: the mass flux across a material surface is zero. This property is used in the next section.

Conservation of mass and the incompressible approximation L14 18:22

Consider a fixed volume. The mass inside changes only by the flux \(\rho\mathbf U\) through its surface. Applying the divergence theorem and requiring the balance for every volume gives the continuity equation (slide eq. 1)

\[ \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf U)=0. \tag{6.3} \]

Expand the divergence: \(\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf U)=\rho\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf U+\mathbf U\cdot\nabla\rho\). The term \(\mathbf U\cdot\nabla\rho\) combines with \(\partial\rho/\partial t\) into the material derivative (6.2), so that \(D\rho/Dt+\rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf U=0\). Dividing by \(\rho\) and writing \(-\rho^{-1}D\rho/Dt=D\ln(1/\rho)/Dt\),

\[ \frac{D}{Dt}\ln\Bigl(\frac1\rho\Bigr)=\nabla\cdot\mathbf U. \tag{6.4} \]

This is slide eq. 2, which writes \(v\) for the specific volume \(1/\rho\); this book writes \(1/\rho\) because an italic \(v\) is hard to tell from \(\nu\). The form (6.4) has a direct reading. A small material volume \(\delta V\) contains a fixed mass \(\rho\,\delta V\), so \(1/\rho\) is proportional to \(\delta V\), and (6.4) states that \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U\) is the fractional rate of change of the volume of a material element, \(\delta V^{-1}D\delta V/Dt\).

The incompressible approximation. If the density of each particle does not change, \(D\rho/Dt=0\), then (6.4) gives

\[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf U=\frac{\partial U_i}{\partial x_i}=0. \tag{6.5} \]

A velocity field with zero divergence is called solenoidal. The slide states the stronger premise, \(\rho\) constant; (6.5) needs only \(D\rho/Dt=0\), which also holds in a fluid of non-uniform density, such as a layered liquid, whose particles keep their density. In this chapter \(\rho\) is constant.

No gas has exactly constant density. Equation (6.5) is an approximation, and its error can be estimated. The lecture defines incompressibility as density that does not change with pressure. For air, density changes with pressure through the speed of sound \(c\), defined by \(c^2=(\partial p/\partial\rho)_s\), the derivative at constant entropy. If the flow neither heats nor cools the particles, \(D\rho/Dt=c^{-2}Dp/Dt\). In a flow with velocity scale \(\mathcal U\) and length scale \(\mathcal L\), the pressure differences that accelerate the fluid are of order \(\rho\mathcal U^2\) (Bernoulli's balance of pressure and kinetic energy per unit volume), and they change following a particle over a time of order \(\mathcal L/\mathcal U\). Then, with the Mach number \(\mathrm{Ma}=\mathcal U/c\),

\[ \lvert\nabla\cdot\mathbf U\rvert=\Bigl\lvert\frac1{\rho c^2}\frac{Dp}{Dt}\Bigr\rvert\sim\frac{\rho\mathcal U^2}{\rho c^2}\,\frac{\mathcal U}{\mathcal L}=\mathrm{Ma}^2\,\frac{\mathcal U}{\mathcal L}, \qquad \frac{\Delta\rho}{\rho}\approx\frac{\Delta p}{\rho c^2}\approx\frac12\mathrm{Ma}^2. \tag{6.6} \]

The individual velocity gradients are of order \(\mathcal U/\mathcal L\), so the divergence is smaller than each of them by a factor of order \(\mathrm{Ma}^2\). The second relation applies to air brought to rest from speed \(\mathcal U\), whose pressure rises by \(\tfrac12\rho\mathcal U^2\). At the nozzle of the running case, \(\mathrm{Ma}=56/340.29=0.165\) and \(\tfrac12\mathrm{Ma}^2=0.014\): density differences of about 1.4 %. At the station, \(\mathrm{Ma}=8/340.29=0.024\), and the pressure fluctuations of order \(\rho u_0^2=4.9\ \mathrm{Pa}\) change the density by \(u_0^2/c^2=3.5\times10^{-5}\). Incompressibility is a low-Mach-number approximation with an error of order \(\mathrm{Ma}^2\). Estimate (6.6) does not cover density changes caused by heating, and it does not describe sound waves, which an incompressible model omits.

Momentum, the Newtonian stress and the Navier–Stokes equations L14 22:08

Newton's second law for a fluid particle relates its acceleration \(D\mathbf U/Dt\) to the forces on it per unit mass. Body forces, such as gravity, act on the volume; the lecture neglects them, and so does this chapter. Surface forces are of molecular origin and are described by the stress tensor \(\tau_{ij}(\mathbf x,t)\), the force per unit area in direction \(j\) on a surface whose normal points in direction \(i\). The tensor is symmetric, \(\tau_{ij}=\tau_{ji}\). The momentum equation (slide eq. 3) is

\[ \rho\frac{DU_j}{Dt}=\frac{\partial\tau_{ij}}{\partial x_i}. \tag{6.7} \]

For a Newtonian fluid with constant properties, the stress is split into an isotropic part, the pressure, and a part proportional to the velocity gradients (slide eq. 4):

\[ \tau_{ij}=-p\,\delta_{ij}+\mu\Bigl(\frac{\partial U_i}{\partial x_j}+\frac{\partial U_j}{\partial x_i}\Bigr), \tag{6.8} \]

where \(\delta_{ij}\) is the Kronecker delta (1 if \(i=j\), 0 otherwise). The isotropic part is the same on every surface through a point, whatever its orientation. The slide calls the second part deviatoric, which means trace-free. Its trace is \(2\mu\,\partial U_i/\partial x_i=2\mu\nabla\cdot\mathbf U\), so it is deviatoric when (6.5) holds. Equation (6.8) contains no separate term proportional to \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U\,\delta_{ij}\); in an incompressible flow such a term would vanish, so (6.8) is complete for the model of this chapter.

The Navier–Stokes equations. Substitute (6.8) into (6.7). The pressure term is \(\partial(-p\delta_{ij})/\partial x_i=-\partial p/\partial x_j\), because \(\delta_{ij}\) replaces the index \(i\) by \(j\). With \(\mu\) constant, the viscous term is

\[ \mu\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\Bigl(\frac{\partial U_i}{\partial x_j}+\frac{\partial U_j}{\partial x_i}\Bigr) =\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\Bigl(\frac{\partial U_i}{\partial x_i}\Bigr)+\mu\frac{\partial^2U_j}{\partial x_i\partial x_i}. \]

The first term on the right is the gradient of \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U\), which is zero by (6.5). This is the step where continuity enters the momentum equation. What remains is

\[ \rho\frac{D\mathbf U}{Dt}=-\nabla p+\mu\nabla^2\mathbf U, \qquad\text{or}\qquad \frac{\partial U_j}{\partial t}+U_i\frac{\partial U_j}{\partial x_i}=-\frac1\rho\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_j}+\nu\frac{\partial^2U_j}{\partial x_i\partial x_i}. \tag{6.9} \]

This is slide eq. 5, the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations. Together with (6.5) they form four equations for four unknown fields, \(U_1\), \(U_2\), \(U_3\) and \(p\). The left side is the acceleration of a particle; the right side is the pressure force and the net viscous force, per unit volume in the first form and per unit mass in the second. The convective term \(U_i\partial U_j/\partial x_i\) is quadratic in the velocity. It is the only nonlinear term, and it is the term that chapter 5 identified as the generator of small scales.

Boundary conditions. On a stationary solid wall with unit normal \(\mathbf n\), the slide states two conditions:

\[ \mathbf n\cdot\mathbf U=0\quad\text{(impermeability)},\qquad \mathbf U-\mathbf n(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf U)=0\quad\text{(no slip)}. \tag{6.10} \]

The first states that no fluid passes through the wall. The second states that the tangential velocity, the velocity minus its normal component, is zero. Together they give \(\mathbf U=0\) at the wall. A moving wall replaces zero by the wall velocity.

The Euler equations. For an inviscid fluid, \(\mu=0\), (6.9) reduces to the Euler equations, \(\rho\,D\mathbf U/Dt=-\nabla p\). They are first order in space, so they accept only the impermeability condition; the tangential velocity at a wall is in general non-zero. The slide therefore notes that solutions of the Euler equations differ from solutions of the Navier–Stokes equations with vanishing viscosity. Chapter 5, section "Inertia and viscosity act at different lengths", explained the reason: \(\nu\) multiplies the highest derivative, and as \(\nu\to0\) the viscous term survives in a layer next to the wall whose thickness tends to zero. The lecture adds that the Euler equations have long been used, and are still used, to analyse flows and to obtain a general idea of their behaviour.

Pressure is fixed by the constraint of zero divergence L15 10:42

In the incompressible model, no equation contains \(\partial p/\partial t\). The continuity equation (6.5) contains no pressure, and the momentum equation (6.9) contains only its gradient. The slide states that pressure has the same effect as a body force and that there is no connection between pressure and density. The question is how the four equations determine \(p\).

The equation for the divergence. Treat (6.9) as the law that advances the velocity in time and (6.5) as a constraint whose preservation is the question; (6.5) was used to obtain the viscous term of (6.9), but whether the solutions of (6.9) keep satisfying (6.5) has not been shown. Take the divergence of (6.9), \(\partial/\partial x_j\) of each component, without using (6.5). The time derivative gives \(\partial(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U)/\partial t\). The convective term gives, by the product rule,

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\Bigl(U_i\frac{\partial U_j}{\partial x_i}\Bigr) =\frac{\partial U_i}{\partial x_j}\frac{\partial U_j}{\partial x_i}+U_i\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U). \]

The viscous term gives \(\nu\nabla^2(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U)\), and the pressure term gives \(-\rho^{-1}\nabla^2p\). Collecting terms,

\[ \frac{D}{Dt}(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U)-\nu\nabla^2(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U) =-\frac1\rho\nabla^2p-\frac{\partial U_i}{\partial x_j}\frac{\partial U_j}{\partial x_i}. \tag{6.11} \]

Equation (6.11) holds for any velocity field that obeys (6.9). It shows that the pressure is the only term that can control the divergence.

The Poisson equation. Choose \(p\) at each instant so that the right side of (6.11) vanishes:

\[ \nabla^2p=-\rho\,\frac{\partial U_i}{\partial x_j}\frac{\partial U_j}{\partial x_i}. \tag{6.12} \]

This is slide eq. 6. The lecture obtains it by taking the divergence of (6.9) and setting \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U=0\) in the time-derivative and viscous terms. The derivation through (6.11) shows what the slide's statement means: satisfaction of the Poisson equation is a necessary and sufficient condition for a solenoidal velocity field to remain solenoidal.

  • Necessary. If \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U=0\) at all times, the left side of (6.11) is zero, so (6.12) holds.
  • Sufficient. If (6.12) holds, (6.11) becomes \(D(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U)/Dt=\nu\nabla^2(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U)\). For a given velocity field this is a linear equation for \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U\) with no source, of the same form as the scalar equation (6.15) with \(\Gamma=\nu\). If \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U=0\) initially, and on the boundary at all times, then \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U=0\) is a solution, and it is the only one: the bound (6.16), proved in the next section for any smooth velocity field (its proof does not use \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U=0\)), confines \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U\) between its largest and smallest initial and boundary values, and both are zero.

The sufficiency argument has two conditions. It requires \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U=0\) at the initial time: (6.12) keeps a solenoidal field solenoidal, but it does not remove a divergence that is already present, which instead is carried and diffused by the flow. It also requires \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U=0\) on the boundary. In a periodic domain there is no boundary and the condition is automatic. In a bounded domain the boundary condition for \(p\) must be chosen with this requirement in view; this chapter does not treat that choice. One condition that \(p\) must satisfy at a stationary no-slip wall follows from the normal component of (6.9): there \(\mathbf U=0\) and \(D\mathbf U/Dt=0\), so

\[ \frac{\partial p}{\partial n}=\mu\,\mathbf n\cdot\nabla^2\mathbf U\quad\text{at the wall}. \tag{6.13} \]

Pressure is non-local. In an unbounded domain where the velocity gradients vanish far away, the solution of (6.12) that decays at infinity is

\[ p(\mathbf x,t)=\frac{\rho}{4\pi}\int\frac{1}{\lvert\mathbf x-\mathbf y\rvert}\,\frac{\partial U_i}{\partial y_j}\frac{\partial U_j}{\partial y_i}(\mathbf y,t)\,d\mathbf y, \tag{6.14} \]

because \(\nabla^2(1/\lvert\mathbf x\rvert)=-4\pi\delta(\mathbf x)\), with \(\delta\) the Dirac delta function. The pressure at one point depends on the velocity gradients everywhere at the same instant, with a weight that decreases only as the reciprocal of the distance. This is the incompressible limit of a compressible fluid, in which pressure signals travel at the speed of sound; in the model (6.5) that speed is infinite. A pressure fluctuation at the station of the running case therefore reflects the eddies around it, not only the velocity at the point.

How the equations are solved. The lecture describes the usual procedure in words: solve the momentum equation for the velocity with an assumed pressure, then correct the pressure with the Poisson equation. The code at the end of the chapter carries out one part of this: it computes \(p\) from (6.12) for a given solenoidal field and confirms that the momentum equation with that pressure produces no change of \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U\). The lecture also states that pressure, as an isotropic stress, can be combined with body forces.

The magnitude of \(p\) follows from (6.12). If the velocity gradients are of order \(u_0/\ell_0\) over a region of size \(\ell_0\), then \(p\sim\rho u_0^2\): about 5 Pa at the station. Section "Rates of strain and rotation" rewrites the right side of (6.12) and shows which kinds of motion make \(\nabla^2p\) positive or negative.

Conserved passive scalars and their bounds L15 21:54

A scalar field \(\phi(\mathbf x,t)\) carried by the flow obeys (slide eq. 7)

\[ \frac{D\phi}{Dt}=\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\mathbf U\cdot\nabla\phi=\Gamma\nabla^2\phi, \tag{6.15} \]

where \(\Gamma\gt0\) is the molecular diffusivity: the thermal diffusivity if \(\phi\) is temperature, the mass diffusivity if \(\phi\) is the mixture fraction, the local mass fraction of fluid that came from one of two streams. The left side is advection by the flow and the right side molecular diffusion. The ratio \(\nu/\Gamma\) is the Prandtl number when \(\phi\) is temperature and the Schmidt number \(\mathrm{Sc}\) when it is a concentration.

Two properties are required for (6.15), and they are independent. The scalar is conserved if no term creates or destroys it: (6.15) has no source term. It is passive if it does not change the velocity field: \(\phi\) does not appear in (6.5) or (6.9). The slide writes that a conserved passive scalar "is passive or conserved, since there is no source or sink term"; the two properties are separated here because they fail separately. The lecture names temperature as an example of a passive scalar and calls the species mass fraction in a chemical reaction an active scalar. In the terms used here, a reacting species is not conserved, because the reaction is a source; it is also active if the reaction changes the density or the viscosity.

Temperature in the running case shows that both properties are approximations. Suppose the nozzle air is 5 K warmer than the surrounding air at 288.15 K. At constant pressure the density of a gas is inversely proportional to its absolute temperature, so the density differs by \(5/288.15=1.7\,\%\). Treating temperature as passive neglects the buoyancy force and the change of viscosity that this difference causes. Treating it as conserved neglects viscous heating, the conversion of kinetic energy into internal energy at the rate \(\varepsilon\).

Boundedness. The slide states that \(\phi\) satisfies \(\phi_{\min}\le\phi\le\phi_{\max}\). In the heated jet, the temperature can be neither above the nozzle temperature nor below the ambient temperature; a mixture fraction stays between 0 and 1. The statement has precise conditions. Let \(\phi\) obey (6.15) in a bounded region \(\mathcal D\) for \(0\lt t\le T_e\), where \(T_e\) is an end time, with \(\Gamma\gt0\) and a smooth velocity field, and let \(\phi\) be continuous on the closed region, the closure of \(\mathcal D\) times \([0,T_e]\), so that it attains its maximum and minimum there. A periodic box is also covered: it is bounded and has no boundary, so only the initial values enter. Let \(\phi_{\max}\) and \(\phi_{\min}\) be the largest and smallest values of \(\phi\) at \(t=0\) and on the boundary of \(\mathcal D\) for \(0\le t\le T_e\). Then

\[ \phi_{\min}\le\phi(\mathbf x,t)\le\phi_{\max}\quad\text{in }\mathcal D\text{ for }0\le t\le T_e. \tag{6.16} \]

Proof of the upper bound. For \(\delta\gt0\) let \(\psi=\phi-\delta t\). Then \(\partial\psi/\partial t+\mathbf U\cdot\nabla\psi-\Gamma\nabla^2\psi=-\delta\lt0\). Suppose \(\psi\) has its maximum over the closed region at a point \((\mathbf x^*,t^*)\) with \(\mathbf x^*\) inside \(\mathcal D\) and \(t^*\gt0\). At that point \(\nabla\psi=0\) and \(\nabla^2\psi\le0\), because \(\psi\) has a spatial maximum there, and \(\partial\psi/\partial t\ge0\), because \(\psi(\mathbf x^*,t)\le\psi(\mathbf x^*,t^*)\) for \(t\lt t^*\). The left side is then \(\ge0\), which contradicts \(-\delta\lt0\). So the maximum of \(\psi\) occurs at \(t=0\) or on the boundary, and \(\phi=\psi+\delta t\le\phi_{\max}+\delta T_e\). Let \(\delta\to0\). The lower bound follows by applying the same argument to \(-\phi\). ∎

The proof shows which conditions matter. A source term, such as a chemical reaction, can make \(D\phi/Dt\) positive at a maximum, and the bound fails. The proof covers boundaries on which \(\phi\) is prescribed; an insulated wall needs an additional argument that this chapter does not give. The proof uses the form (6.15), with the velocity outside the derivative. The conservative form \(\partial\phi/\partial t+\nabla\cdot(\mathbf U\phi)=\Gamma\nabla^2\phi\) is equivalent to it only when \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U=0\). The bound is a property of the differential equation; a numerical approximation of it need not inherit it, and exercise 3 constructs a discrete counterexample.

The vorticity equation and vortex stretching L16 1:14

The vorticity is the curl of the velocity (slide eq. 8),

\[ \boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf U,\qquad \omega_i=\epsilon_{ijk}\frac{\partial U_k}{\partial x_j}, \tag{6.17} \]

where \(\epsilon_{ijk}\) is the alternating symbol: \(+1\) when \((i,j,k)\) is an even permutation of (1, 2, 3), \(-1\) for an odd permutation, and 0 when two indices are equal. (It is always written with three indices; the dissipation rate \(\varepsilon\) has none.) The vorticity is twice the local angular velocity of the fluid, in \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) (the rigid-rotation field of the next section, which turns at angular velocity \(w\), has \(\omega_3=2w\)). The slide states that an essential feature of turbulent flows is that they are rotational: their vorticity is non-zero.

Derivation. Take the curl of (6.9) divided by \(\rho\). Three terms are direct. The curl of \(\partial\mathbf U/\partial t\) is \(\partial\boldsymbol\omega/\partial t\). The curl of \(\nu\nabla^2\mathbf U\) is \(\nu\nabla^2\boldsymbol\omega\), because the two operators commute. The curl of \(\rho^{-1}\nabla p\) is \(\rho^{-1}\nabla\times\nabla p-\rho^{-2}\nabla\rho\times\nabla p\). The first part is zero, because the curl of a gradient is zero; the second is zero because \(\rho\) is constant. (In a flow of non-uniform density, such as the heated jet when buoyancy matters, this second part remains and produces vorticity wherever \(\nabla\rho\) and \(\nabla p\) are not parallel.) For the convective term use the identity

\[ (\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U=\nabla\bigl(\tfrac12\lvert\mathbf U\rvert^2\bigr)-\mathbf U\times\boldsymbol\omega . \]

The curl of the gradient term is zero. For the second term,

\[ \nabla\times(\mathbf U\times\boldsymbol\omega)=\mathbf U(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\omega)-\boldsymbol\omega(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U)+(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U-(\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol\omega . \]

The divergence of a curl is zero, so \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\omega=0\), and \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U=0\) by (6.5). Collecting the remaining terms gives the vorticity equation (slide eq. 9):

\[ \frac{D\boldsymbol\omega}{Dt}=(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U+\nu\nabla^2\boldsymbol\omega. \tag{6.18} \]

The slide writes the first term on the right as \(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla\mathbf U\), with components \(\omega_j\,\partial U_i/\partial x_j\). The slide notes that the pressure term has vanished: (6.18) contains only the velocity. The term \(\nu\nabla^2\boldsymbol\omega\) is viscous diffusion of vorticity.

Vortex stretching. The term \((\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U\) changes the vorticity of a particle through the velocity gradients. Its meaning follows from a comparison with a material line element. Let \(\delta\mathbf x\) be the short vector between two neighbouring fluid particles. Each end moves with the local velocity, so \(D\delta\mathbf x/Dt=\mathbf U(\mathbf x+\delta\mathbf x)-\mathbf U(\mathbf x)\), and to first order in \(\lvert\delta\mathbf x\rvert\)

\[ \frac{D\,\delta\mathbf x}{Dt}=(\delta\mathbf x\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U. \tag{6.19} \]

For \(\nu=0\), equations (6.18) and (6.19) are the same linear equation, with the same velocity gradient as coefficient. If \(\boldsymbol\omega=\alpha\,\delta\mathbf x\) at one time, for a constant \(\alpha\), then \(\boldsymbol\omega=\alpha\,\delta\mathbf x\) at all later times: along the particle path both are solutions of the same linear ordinary differential equation with the same initial value, and such an equation has one solution. A vortex line in an inviscid flow therefore moves with the fluid, and its vorticity grows in proportion to the length of the material line that lies along it. This is the slide's statement: the strain rate produced by the velocity gradients stretches the material line aligned with \(\boldsymbol\omega\), and so increases \(\boldsymbol\omega\). Viscosity breaks the exact correspondence by diffusing vorticity.

The magnitude of the vorticity obeys an equation that follows from (6.18) after multiplication by \(\omega_i\):

\[ \frac{D}{Dt}\Bigl(\frac12\omega_i\omega_i\Bigr)=\omega_i\frac{\partial U_i}{\partial x_j}\omega_j+\nu\,\omega_i\nabla^2\omega_i. \tag{6.20} \]

The first term on the right is positive when the vorticity points along a direction in which the flow stretches fluid elements. The next section shows that only the symmetric part of the velocity gradient contributes to it.

Two dimensions. In a two-dimensional flow, \(\mathbf U=(U_1(x_1,x_2,t),U_2(x_1,x_2,t),0)\). The vorticity (6.17) has only the component \(\omega_3=\partial U_2/\partial x_1-\partial U_1/\partial x_2\), and \((\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U=\omega_3\,\partial\mathbf U/\partial x_3=0\) because nothing depends on \(x_3\). Equation (6.18) becomes

\[ \frac{D\omega_3}{Dt}=\nu\nabla^2\omega_3,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\Bigl\langle\frac12\omega_3^2\Bigr\rangle_V=-\nu\bigl\langle\lvert\nabla\omega_3\rvert^2\bigr\rangle_V, \tag{6.21} \]

where \(\langle\cdot\rangle_V\) is the average over a periodic domain. The second relation follows by multiplying the first by \(\omega_3\) and averaging: the advection term averages to zero because \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U=0\) and the domain is periodic, and the diffusion term is integrated by parts. The quantity \(\langle\frac12\omega_3^2\rangle_V\) is the enstrophy. Without forcing, the enstrophy of a two-dimensional flow cannot increase. In three dimensions the stretching term in (6.20) can increase it.

The lecture concludes from this that turbulence does not exist in two dimensions. The slide states that two-dimensional turbulence, in which vortex stretching is absent, is vastly different from three-dimensional turbulence. The literature supports the slide's statement, not the spoken one. Boffetta and Ecke (2012) review the theory, simulations and experiments of forced two-dimensional turbulence. They state that it is never realized strictly in nature or in the laboratory, but that the large-scale motions of the atmosphere and oceans are described, to first approximation, as two-dimensional turbulent fluids (p. 428). Their equation (6) is the second relation of (6.21) in spectral form; because it bounds the enstrophy from above, the energy dissipation tends to zero as \(\nu\to0\). They contrast this with three-dimensional turbulence, in which vortex stretching amplifies the enstrophy and the energy dissipation stays finite as viscosity vanishes; in two dimensions the energy is transferred to large scales by an inverse cascade (p. 430). The finite three-dimensional limit is the inviscid estimate \(\varepsilon\sim u_0^3/\ell_0\) of chapter 5, which contains no viscosity.

In the running case, the vorticity of the smallest eddies is of order \(1/\tau_\eta=2.3\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), against \(u_0/\ell_0=20\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) for the energy-containing eddies, a ratio of 117. Averaged over a periodic box, the advection part of (6.20) is zero and the viscous term equals \(-\nu\langle\lvert\nabla\boldsymbol\omega\rvert^2\rangle_V\le0\), where \(\lvert\nabla\boldsymbol\omega\rvert^2=(\partial\omega_i/\partial x_j)(\partial\omega_i/\partial x_j)\). The stretching term is therefore the only term that can increase the mean enstrophy. If the small scales of the jet are treated as locally homogeneous, as in the next section, vorticities 117 times those of the energy-containing eddies must be produced by it.

Rates of strain and rotation L16 13:32

The velocity gradients \(\partial U_i/\partial x_j\) are the nine components of a second-order tensor. Any second-order tensor can be split into an isotropic part, a symmetric trace-free part and an antisymmetric part (slide eq. 10):

\[ \frac{\partial U_i}{\partial x_j}=\frac13(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U)\,\delta_{ij}+S_{ij}+\Omega_{ij}, \tag{6.22} \]

with the rate-of-strain and rate-of-rotation tensors (slide eqs. 11 and 12)

\[ S_{ij}=\frac12\Bigl(\frac{\partial U_i}{\partial x_j}+\frac{\partial U_j}{\partial x_i}\Bigr),\qquad \Omega_{ij}=\frac12\Bigl(\frac{\partial U_i}{\partial x_j}-\frac{\partial U_j}{\partial x_i}\Bigr). \tag{6.23} \]

As written in (6.23), \(S_{ij}\) includes the isotropic part, and (6.22) holds as written only when \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U=0\), the case of this chapter; then \(S_{ii}=0\) and \(S_{ij}\) is the symmetric deviatoric tensor named on the slide. With (6.23) the Newtonian stress (6.8) is \(\tau_{ij}=-p\delta_{ij}+2\mu S_{ij}\). The viscous stress depends linearly on the rate of strain and not on the rate of rotation: a fluid in rigid rotation has \(S_{ij}=0\) and no viscous stress.

Vorticity and the rotation tensor. The slide relates them by (slide eq. 13)

\[ \omega_i=-\epsilon_{ijk}\Omega_{jk},\qquad \Omega_{ij}=-\frac12\epsilon_{ijk}\omega_k. \tag{6.24} \]

The sign can be checked. Because \(\epsilon_{ijk}\) is antisymmetric in \(j,k\), \(\epsilon_{ijk}\Omega_{jk}=\epsilon_{ijk}\,\partial U_j/\partial x_k\). Exchanging the names of \(j\) and \(k\) gives \(\epsilon_{ikj}\,\partial U_k/\partial x_j=-\epsilon_{ijk}\,\partial U_k/\partial x_j=-\omega_i\) by (6.17). The inverse follows from \(\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{klm}=\delta_{il}\delta_{jm}-\delta_{im}\delta_{jl}\): \(-\frac12\epsilon_{ijk}\omega_k=-\frac12(\partial U_j/\partial x_i-\partial U_i/\partial x_j)=\Omega_{ij}\). The slide adds that \(\Omega_{ij}\) is a tensor while \(\omega_i\) is not. Under a change of Cartesian axes described by the direction cosines \(a_{ij}=\bar{\mathbf e}_i\cdot\mathbf e_j\) between the new unit vectors \(\bar{\mathbf e}_i\) and the old \(\mathbf e_j\) (used again in section "Invariance under changes of frame and time reversal"), \(\Omega_{ij}\) transforms as a tensor. The symbol \(\epsilon_{ijk}\) keeps the same values in every frame, whereas a tensor would acquire \(a_{il}a_{jm}a_{kn}\epsilon_{lmn}=\det(a)\,\epsilon_{ijk}\). Therefore \(\omega_i\) transforms as a vector multiplied by \(\det(a)\), which is \(+1\) for a rotation and \(-1\) for a reflection. A quantity with this property is a pseudovector.

Only strain stretches vorticity. The stretching term of (6.18) is \(\omega_j\,\partial U_i/\partial x_j=(S_{ij}+\Omega_{ij})\omega_j\). By (6.24), \(\Omega_{ij}\omega_j=-\frac12\epsilon_{ijk}\omega_k\omega_j=0\), because a symmetric product is contracted with an antisymmetric symbol. Therefore

\[ (\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U=S_{ij}\,\omega_j,\qquad \omega_i\frac{\partial U_i}{\partial x_j}\omega_j=\omega_iS_{ij}\omega_j. \tag{6.25} \]

In the axes in which \(S_{ij}\) is diagonal, with principal strain rates \(s_1+s_2+s_3=0\), the enstrophy source of (6.20) is \(s_1\omega_1^2+s_2\omega_2^2+s_3\omega_3^2\). It is positive when the vorticity lies mainly along a direction of positive strain rate, that is, of stretching. The vector \(S_{ij}\omega_j\) has a component along \(\boldsymbol\omega\), which changes the magnitude of the vorticity (stretching or compression), and a component perpendicular to it, which turns the vorticity vector (tilting). The rotation tensor makes no contribution: the local rotation of the fluid is about the vorticity vector itself.

Return to the pressure. The right side of the Poisson equation (6.12) contains \(\frac{\partial U_i}{\partial x_j}\frac{\partial U_j}{\partial x_i}=(S_{ij}+\Omega_{ij})(S_{ji}+\Omega_{ji})=(S_{ij}+\Omega_{ij})(S_{ij}-\Omega_{ij})\). The cross terms cancel, because \(S_{ij}\Omega_{ij}=0\) for a symmetric and an antisymmetric tensor, and \(\Omega_{ij}\Omega_{ij}=\frac14\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{ijl}\omega_k\omega_l=\frac12\omega_k\omega_k\). Hence

\[ \frac{\partial U_i}{\partial x_j}\frac{\partial U_j}{\partial x_i}=S_{ij}S_{ij}-\frac12\omega_k\omega_k,\qquad \nabla^2p=\rho\Bigl(\frac12\omega_k\omega_k-S_{ij}S_{ij}\Bigr). \tag{6.26} \]

Where rotation dominates strain, \(\nabla^2p\gt0\); where strain dominates, \(\nabla^2p\lt0\). Since \(\nabla^2p\ge0\) at any local minimum of \(p\), a pressure minimum can occur only where \(\frac12\omega_k\omega_k\ge S_{ij}S_{ij}\). Two exact solutions check the sign and the magnitude. In rigid rotation at angular velocity \(w\), \(\mathbf U=(-wx_2,wx_1,0)\), \(S_{ij}=0\) and \(\omega_3=2w\), so (6.26) gives \(\nabla^2p=2\rho w^2\); the pressure \(p=p_0+\frac12\rho w^2(x_1^2+x_2^2)\) satisfies this and has its minimum on the axis. In plane strain, \(\mathbf U=(sx_1,-sx_2,0)\), with principal strain rates \(s,-s,0\), \(\boldsymbol\omega=0\) and \(S_{ij}S_{ij}=2s^2\), so \(\nabla^2p=-2\rho s^2\), satisfied by \(p=p_0-\frac12\rho s^2(x_1^2+x_2^2)\), which has its maximum at the stagnation point. Exercise 1 confirms both pressures from the momentum equation directly.

Hunt, Wray and Moin (1988) use the same quantity to identify eddies in simulated turbulence. They define the second invariant \(II=\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}=E_{ij}^2-\frac12\omega_i^2\), with \(E_{ij}\) the symmetric strain tensor (their notation; their \(u_i\) is the velocity field), and classify as eddy zones the regions where \(II\) is below a negative threshold, so that rotation exceeds strain. They add a second criterion, that the pressure has a minimum in the zone (p. 196, equations (2.1) and (2.2)). By (6.26), \(II=-\nabla^2p/\rho\), so their first criterion selects regions where \(\nabla^2p\) is positive.

A balance of strain and vorticity on average. In a periodic domain the average of \(\nabla^2p\) is zero. Averaging (6.26) gives

\[ \bigl\langle S_{ij}S_{ij}\bigr\rangle_V=\frac12\bigl\langle\omega_k\omega_k\bigr\rangle_V. \tag{6.27} \]

This uses \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U=0\), through (6.12). A similar step applies to the velocity fluctuation \(\mathbf u\) of a statistically homogeneous field with \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0\): by the product rule, \(\langle(\partial u_i/\partial x_j)(\partial u_j/\partial x_i)\rangle=\partial^2\langle u_iu_j\rangle/\partial x_i\partial x_j\), which is zero because the expectation does not depend on position. The pointwise identity of (6.26), written for \(\mathbf u\), then gives \(\langle s_{ij}s_{ij}\rangle=\frac12\langle\omega'_k\omega'_k\rangle\), where \(s_{ij}\) and \(\omega'_k\) are the rate of strain and the vorticity of the fluctuation. The dissipation rate of chapter 5, \(\varepsilon=2\nu\langle s_{ij}s_{ij}\rangle\), is then \(\nu\langle\omega'_k\omega'_k\rangle\). If the small scales of the jet are treated as locally homogeneous, the root-mean-square vorticity at the station is \((\varepsilon/\nu)^{1/2}=(80/1.4607\times10^{-5})^{1/2}=2.3\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), which is \(1/\tau_\eta\), consistent with the scale estimate of the previous section.

Reynolds-number similarity L16 17:34

The slides pose a question about two experiments that differ in time, location, orientation, platform motion, fluid and size, on geometrically similar apparatus: are the velocity fields the same after appropriate scaling and in appropriate coordinates? The size of the apparatus is characterized by a length scale \(\mathcal L\), and the initial and boundary conditions on the velocity by a velocity scale \(\mathcal U\). In the running case \(\mathcal L\) is the nozzle diameter and \(\mathcal U\) the exit velocity.

The scaled equations. Define dimensionless variables, marked by a hat:

\[ \hat x_i=\frac{x_i}{\mathcal L},\qquad \hat t=\frac{t\,\mathcal U}{\mathcal L},\qquad \hat U_i=\frac{U_i}{\mathcal U},\qquad \hat p=\frac{p}{\rho\,\mathcal U^2},\qquad \mathrm{Re}=\frac{\mathcal U\mathcal L}{\nu}. \tag{6.28} \]

Each derivative \(\partial/\partial x_i\) becomes \(\mathcal L^{-1}\partial/\partial\hat x_i\) and \(\partial/\partial t\) becomes \((\mathcal U/\mathcal L)\,\partial/\partial\hat t\). Substitute into (6.5), (6.9) and (6.12), and divide the momentum equation by \(\mathcal U^2/\mathcal L\):

\[ \frac{\partial\hat U_i}{\partial\hat x_i}=0,\qquad \frac{\partial\hat U_j}{\partial\hat t}+\hat U_i\frac{\partial\hat U_j}{\partial\hat x_i}=-\frac{\partial\hat p}{\partial\hat x_j}+\frac1{\mathrm{Re}}\frac{\partial^2\hat U_j}{\partial\hat x_i\partial\hat x_i},\qquad \frac{\partial^2\hat p}{\partial\hat x_i\partial\hat x_i}=-\frac{\partial\hat U_i}{\partial\hat x_j}\frac{\partial\hat U_j}{\partial\hat x_i}. \tag{6.29} \]

This is slide eq. 14. The density and the dimensional values of \(\mathcal U\), \(\mathcal L\) and \(\nu\) have disappeared; only \(\mathrm{Re}\) remains. The same substitution in (6.15), with \(\hat\phi\) the scalar divided by a reference difference, gives the coefficient \(1/(\mathrm{Re}\,\mathrm{Sc})\) in front of the diffusion term, so a scalar field needs the Schmidt (or Prandtl) number to match as well.

The similarity statement. If the two experiments have the same Reynolds number, and if their initial and boundary conditions are identical in scaled variables, then the scaled velocity fields \(\hat{\mathbf U}(\hat{\mathbf x},\hat t)\) are the same, because they are solutions of identical equations with identical conditions. This is Reynolds-number similarity. The unscaled fields are then similar, not identical: velocities, lengths and times in the two experiments are related by the ratios of their \(\mathcal U\), \(\mathcal L\) and \(\mathcal L/\mathcal U\). The slide adds that the scaled Euler equations contain no Reynolds number at all, so they are invariant under a change of scale.

The statement has three boundaries.

  1. It is a statement about the model (6.5), (6.9). Two experiments are described by that model only if both satisfy its approximations: \(\mathrm{Kn}\ll1\), \(\mathrm{Ma}^2\ll1\), no buoyancy. A half-scale model of the running-case jet in the same air needs \(\mathcal U=112\ \mathrm{m/s}\) to keep \(\mathrm{Re}=9.6\times10^4\). Its Mach number is then 0.33 and \(\tfrac12\mathrm{Ma}^2=0.054\), four times the density variation of the full-size jet, so the Mach number must be checked as a second similarity parameter. Exercise 5 develops this design question.
  2. The scaled conditions must be identical, including the geometry of the nozzle, the wall roughness and the disturbances in the incoming flow, because each of these is part of the initial and boundary data of (6.29).
  3. For a turbulent flow, the initial and boundary data of two experiments are never the same in every detail, and the two instantaneous fields are not the same in detail. The claim that is compared with measurements concerns statistics of the scaled fields, such as the scaled mean profile of the previous section; chapter 7 defines these statistics.

Invariance under changes of frame and time reversal L16 24:45

The slide figure of transformation properties shows a reference experiment and seven variations: a change of scale, a shift in space, a change of orientation, a reflection, uniform motion, rectilinear acceleration and frame rotation. An equation is invariant under a transformation if the transformed fields satisfy the same equation in the transformed variables. Invariance justifies comparing experiments made in different places and frames.

Translation in space and time. Let the second experiment be made a time \(t_s\) later, with the apparatus moved by \(\mathbf x_s\) (the slide writes \(T\) and \(\mathbf X\)). The scaled variables become \(\hat{\mathbf x}=(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_s)/\mathcal L\) and \(\hat t=(t-t_s)\mathcal U/\mathcal L\) (slide eq. 15). Derivatives are unchanged by a constant shift, so (6.29) is unchanged: the equations are invariant under translation.

Rotation and reflection. Let \(\mathbf e_i\) and \(\bar{\mathbf e}_j\) be the unit vectors of two Cartesian frames with a common origin, and \(a_{ij}=\bar{\mathbf e}_i\cdot\mathbf e_j\) the direction cosines (the slide writes \(\mathbf e_i\cdot\mathbf e_j\)). The scaled variables in the new frame are \(\hat x_i=a_{ij}x_j/\mathcal L\) and \(\hat U_i=a_{ij}U_j/\mathcal U\) (slide eq. 16 states the same transformation with the transposed index convention). Because \(a_{ik}a_{jk}=\delta_{ij}\), every term of (6.29) is a contraction of tensors and keeps its form. For the divergence, for example, \(\partial\hat U_i/\partial\hat x_i=a_{ij}a_{ik}\,(\mathcal L/\mathcal U)\,\partial U_j/\partial x_k=\delta_{jk}\,(\mathcal L/\mathcal U)\,\partial U_j/\partial x_k=(\mathcal L/\mathcal U)\,\partial U_j/\partial x_j\), because \(\partial/\partial\hat x_i\) transforms with the same \(a_{ik}\) as \(\hat x_i\); each other term is a contraction of the same kind. The equations are invariant under rotation, for which \(\det(a)=+1\), and under reflection, for which \(\det(a)=-1\). The vorticity transforms with the extra factor \(\det(a)\) found in the previous section, so a mirror-image experiment has the mirror image of the velocity field and the mirror image of the vorticity field multiplied by \(-1\).

Time reversal. Define \(\hat t=-t\,\mathcal U/\mathcal L\) and \(\hat{\mathbf U}(\hat{\mathbf x},\hat t)=-\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)/\mathcal U\) (slide eq. 17), so that a reversed film of the flow is described. The time derivative changes sign twice, once from \(\hat t\) and once from \(\hat{\mathbf U}\), and keeps its sign; the convective term is quadratic in \(\mathbf U\) and keeps its sign; the pressure is unchanged. The viscous term is linear in \(\mathbf U\) and contains no time derivative, so it changes sign:

\[ \frac{\partial\hat U_j}{\partial\hat t}+\hat U_i\frac{\partial\hat U_j}{\partial\hat x_i}=-\frac{\partial\hat p}{\partial\hat x_j}-\frac1{\mathrm{Re}}\frac{\partial^2\hat U_j}{\partial\hat x_i\partial\hat x_i}. \tag{6.30} \]

The Navier–Stokes equations are not invariant under time reversal; the Euler equations are. The physical content is the direction of energy transfer: viscosity converts kinetic energy into internal energy, and a reversed film would show a flow gaining kinetic energy from viscosity, which (6.30) describes with a negative viscosity. Exercise 6 works this out for an exact solution.

Uniform motion of the frame. Let the new frame move at constant velocity \(\mathbf V\): \(\bar{\mathbf x}=\mathbf x-\mathbf Vt\), \(\bar t=t\), \(\bar{\mathbf U}(\bar{\mathbf x},\bar t)=\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)-\mathbf V\) (slide eq. 18). By the chain rule, \(\partial/\partial t\) at fixed \(\mathbf x\) equals \(\partial/\partial\bar t-\mathbf V\cdot\bar\nabla\), and spatial derivatives are unchanged. Then

\[ \frac{\partial\mathbf U}{\partial t}+(\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U =\frac{\partial\bar{\mathbf U}}{\partial\bar t}-(\mathbf V\cdot\bar\nabla)\bar{\mathbf U}+\bigl((\bar{\mathbf U}+\mathbf V)\cdot\bar\nabla\bigr)\bar{\mathbf U} =\frac{\partial\bar{\mathbf U}}{\partial\bar t}+(\bar{\mathbf U}\cdot\bar\nabla)\bar{\mathbf U}. \tag{6.31} \]

The acceleration has the same form in both frames, and the pressure and viscous terms are unchanged, so the equations are Galilean invariant: as for all phenomena of classical mechanics, the behaviour of the fluid is the same in all inertial frames. A probe that moves downstream with the jet at 8 m/s measures a different signal from a fixed probe, but both signals are described by the same equations.

Accelerating and rotating frames. The slide figure includes two frames that the slide text does not treat. If the frame origin moves as \(\mathbf x_s(t)\) with acceleration \(\ddot{\mathbf x}_s\), the same chain-rule calculation adds the term \(-\ddot{\mathbf x}_s\) to the right side of the momentum equation. This force per unit mass is uniform in space, so it is the gradient of \(-\ddot{\mathbf x}_s\cdot\bar{\mathbf x}\), and it can be absorbed into a modified pressure \(\bar p=p+\rho\,\ddot{\mathbf x}_s\cdot\bar{\mathbf x}\). The velocity field of an incompressible flow with no free surface is then unaffected by the acceleration. If the frame rotates at constant angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\varpi\) (written \(\boldsymbol\varpi\) here because \(\Omega_{ij}\) is the rotation tensor and chapter 5 used \(\boldsymbol\Omega\) for the Earth's angular velocity), two terms appear. A vector \(\mathbf b\) that is fixed in the rotating frame turns at the rate \(\boldsymbol\varpi\times\mathbf b\) in the inertial frame, so the inertial rate of change of any vector equals its rate of change seen in the rotating frame plus \(\boldsymbol\varpi\times\) the vector. Applied to the position of a particle, with the origin on the rotation axis, this gives \(\mathbf U=\bar{\mathbf U}+\boldsymbol\varpi\times\bar{\mathbf x}\), where \(\bar{\mathbf U}\) is the velocity seen in the rotating frame. Applied once more, to \(\mathbf U\), it gives \(D\mathbf U/Dt=D\bar{\mathbf U}/D\bar t+\boldsymbol\varpi\times\bar{\mathbf U}+\boldsymbol\varpi\times\bar{\mathbf U}+\boldsymbol\varpi\times(\boldsymbol\varpi\times\bar{\mathbf x})\): one \(\boldsymbol\varpi\times\bar{\mathbf U}\) comes from differentiating \(\boldsymbol\varpi\times\bar{\mathbf x}\) in the rotating frame, the other from the rotation of \(\bar{\mathbf U}\) itself, and the last term from the rotation of \(\boldsymbol\varpi\times\bar{\mathbf x}\). The pressure gradient is the same vector in both frames, and the Laplacian does not depend on the orientation of the axes; \(\bar\nabla^2(\boldsymbol\varpi\times\bar{\mathbf x})=0\) because that field is linear in position, so \(\nu\nabla^2\mathbf U=\nu\bar\nabla^2\bar{\mathbf U}\). Substituting into (6.9) and moving the two new terms to the right gives

\[ \frac{D\bar{\mathbf U}}{D\bar t}=-\frac1\rho\bar\nabla p+\nu\bar\nabla^2\bar{\mathbf U}-2\boldsymbol\varpi\times\bar{\mathbf U}-\boldsymbol\varpi\times(\boldsymbol\varpi\times\bar{\mathbf x}). \tag{6.32} \]

The centrifugal term is the gradient of \(\frac12\lvert\boldsymbol\varpi\times\bar{\mathbf x}\rvert^2\) and can be absorbed into the pressure. The Coriolis term \(-2\boldsymbol\varpi\times\bar{\mathbf U}\) depends on the velocity and is not a gradient in general (exercise 4), so the equations are not invariant under frame rotation. Executable check of the identities and of the pressure equation

The program below tests the chapter's derivations on velocity fields in a periodic box of side \(2\pi\), in dimensionless units with \(\rho=1\) and \(\nu=0.01\). Derivatives are computed spectrally: each field is transformed by the fast Fourier transform, multiplied by \(\mathrm i\kappa_j\) for a derivative along \(x_j\), and transformed back, where \(\kappa_j\) is the wavenumber component (an integer in this box). The array A[i, j] holds \(\partial U_i/\partial x_j\) and W holds \(\Omega_{ij}\).

The program builds a random three-dimensional field from Fourier modes of wavenumber magnitude at most 4 and removes the component of each mode parallel to its wavenumber vector, which makes the field solenoidal. It then reports seven checks: \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U=0\); the sign relation (6.24); the identity (6.26); the stretching identity (6.25); the average balance (6.27); the rate of change of \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U\) given by (6.11) with the Poisson pressure (6.12) and with \(p=0\); and, for the Taylor–Green vortex, an exact two-dimensional solution introduced in exercise 6, the difference between the pressure from (6.12) and the exact pressure \(p=\frac{\rho}4(\cos2x_1+\cos2x_2)\) at \(t=0\). It finally compares the stretching term of the two-dimensional Taylor–Green field with that of the three-dimensional random field. Residuals are divided by the largest velocity gradient (or its square), so that a value near \(10^{-16}\) is round-off. The program reads no files, writes only to standard output and requires NumPy. The modes are limited to \(\lvert\boldsymbol\kappa\rvert\le4\) so that products of two fields contain only wavenumbers the 32-point grid represents; with more modes the identities would still hold pointwise, but the spectral products would be aliased. The checks establish that the algebra of the chapter is right for these fields. They are not a simulation of turbulence: the random field has no dynamics and no turbulent statistics.

import numpy as np

n, rho, nu = 32, 1.0, 0.01          # points per side, density, viscosity
x = 2*np.pi*np.arange(n)/n          # periodic box of side 2 pi
k1 = np.fft.fftfreq(n, 1/n)
K = np.array(np.meshgrid(k1, k1, k1, indexing="ij"))
K2 = (K**2).sum(0)
K2[0, 0, 0] = 1.0                   # the mean mode is handled separately


def ddx(f, j):
    """Spectral derivative of a periodic field f along axis j."""
    return np.fft.ifftn(1j*K[j]*np.fft.fftn(f)).real


def gradient(U):
    """A[i, j] = dU_i/dx_j."""
    return np.array([[ddx(U[i], j) for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)])


def poisson_pressure(A):
    """Solve lap p = -rho (dU_i/dx_j)(dU_j/dx_i), mean of p zero."""
    rhs = -rho*np.einsum("ij...,ji...->...", A, A)
    ph = -np.fft.fftn(rhs)/K2
    ph[0, 0, 0] = 0.0
    return np.fft.ifftn(ph).real


def divergence_tendency(U, p):
    """d(div U)/dt implied by the momentum equation with pressure p."""
    A = gradient(U)
    R = [-(U*A[j]).sum(0) - ddx(p, j)/rho
         + nu*sum(ddx(ddx(U[j], m), m) for m in range(3)) for j in range(3)]
    return sum(ddx(R[j], j) for j in range(3))


def vorticity(A):
    return np.array([A[2, 1] - A[1, 2], A[0, 2] - A[2, 0], A[1, 0] - A[0, 1]])


# Random solenoidal field: Fourier modes with |kappa| <= 4, projected.
rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
Uh = np.fft.fftn(rng.standard_normal((3, n, n, n)), axes=(1, 2, 3))
Uh[:, K2 > 16] = 0.0                # products stay below the grid limit
Uh -= K*(K*Uh).sum(0)/K2            # remove the part parallel to kappa
U = np.fft.ifftn(Uh, axes=(1, 2, 3)).real
A = gradient(U)
g = np.abs(A).max()                 # velocity-gradient scale for ratios
S = 0.5*(A + A.transpose(1, 0, 2, 3, 4))
W = 0.5*(A - A.transpose(1, 0, 2, 3, 4))
omega = vorticity(A)
alt = np.zeros((3, 3, 3))           # alternating symbol eps_ijk
for i, j, k in ((0, 1, 2), (1, 2, 0), (2, 0, 1)):
    alt[i, j, k], alt[i, k, j] = 1.0, -1.0
AA = np.einsum("ij...,ji...->...", A, A)
SS = np.einsum("ij...,ij...->...", S, S)
stretch = np.einsum("j...,ij...->i...", omega, A)
checks = {
    "div U": np.einsum("ii...->...", A)/g,
    "omega_i + eps_ijk W_jk": (omega + np.einsum("ijk,jk...->i...",
                                                 alt, W))/g,
    "AA - (SS - |omega|^2/2)": (AA - SS + 0.5*(omega**2).sum(0))/g**2,
    "(omega.grad)U - S omega": (stretch - np.einsum("ij...,j...->i...",
                                                    S, omega))/g**2,
}
for name, r in checks.items():
    print(f"{name:25s} max relative residual {np.abs(r).max():.1e}")
print(f"box means: S_ij S_ij {SS.mean():.6f}, "
      f"|omega|^2/2 {0.5*(omega**2).sum(0).mean():.6f}")

p = poisson_pressure(A)
for label, q in (("Poisson p", p), ("p = 0", 0*p)):
    rate = np.abs(divergence_tendency(U, q)).max()/g**2
    print(f"d(div U)/dt with {label:9s} {rate:.1e}")

# Taylor-Green vortex: an exact two-dimensional solution, at t = 0.
X, Y, Z = np.meshgrid(x, x, x, indexing="ij")
tg = np.array([np.sin(X)*np.cos(Y), -np.cos(X)*np.sin(Y), 0*X])
At = gradient(tg)
p_exact = rho/4*(np.cos(2*X) + np.cos(2*Y))
err = np.abs(poisson_pressure(At) - p_exact).max()
print(f"Taylor-Green pressure error {err:.1e}")
s2 = np.einsum("j...,ij...->i...", vorticity(At), At)
print(f"stretching, 2D field {np.abs(s2).max():.1e}, "
      f"3D field {np.abs(stretch).max()/g**2:.2f} (relative)")

The sixth and seventh lines carry the main result of section "Pressure is fixed by the constraint of zero divergence": with the Poisson pressure the divergence does not change, and without it the momentum equation would create a divergence at a rate of order \(\lvert\nabla\mathbf U\rvert^2\). The Taylor–Green line checks the Poisson solver against an exact pressure, independently of the random field. The last line shows the two-dimensional result of (6.21): the stretching term is exactly zero for a field that does not depend on \(x_3\), and of order \(\lvert\nabla\mathbf U\rvert^2\) for a three-dimensional field.

A useful failure test is to delete the line Uh -= K*(K*Uh).sum(0)/K2, so that the random field is not solenoidal. The divergence residual then becomes 1.3, and the average balance (6.27) fails (0.141832 against 0.069810; the difference is the mean square of \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U\)). The rate of change of the divergence with the Poisson pressure becomes 1.4 instead of \(10^{-14}\): equation (6.12) keeps a solenoidal field solenoidal but does not make a non-solenoidal field solenoidal, as the sufficiency argument requires. The identities (6.24), (6.25) and the pointwise form of (6.26) still hold, because they are algebra on the velocity gradient and do not use \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U=0\).

What the equations assume and what they do not establish

The equations of this chapter rest on four approximations, each with a measure of its error. The continuum hypothesis requires \(\mathrm{Kn}\ll1\) at the smallest scale of the flow; at the running-case station \(\mathrm{Kn}=8.4\times10^{-4}\) at the Kolmogorov scale. Incompressibility requires \(\mathrm{Ma}^2\ll1\) and no density changes from heating; its error is of order \(\mathrm{Ma}^2\), 1.4 % at the nozzle of the running case and \(3.5\times10^{-5}\) at the station. The Newtonian stress (6.8) is a constitutive model, linear in the rate of strain; it does not describe fluids whose stress is not a linear function of the rate of strain. A passive conserved scalar requires negligible buoyancy and no sources, which for temperature means small temperature differences and negligible viscous heating.

Within the model, the derived results have different status. The Poisson equation (6.12) and the vorticity equation (6.18) are exact consequences of (6.5) and (6.9). The sufficiency of (6.12) needs a solenoidal initial field and a boundary condition that this chapter does not construct for bounded domains. The boundedness (6.16) is proved for prescribed boundary values and holds for the differential equation, not automatically for its discretizations. The enstrophy bound (6.21) is exact for unforced two-dimensional flow in a periodic domain; real flows are never exactly two-dimensional (Boffetta and Ecke 2012, p. 428). Reynolds-number similarity is exact for the model with identical scaled conditions; for turbulent flows its tested content concerns statistics.

The reader can now write the four equations for an incompressible Newtonian flow, test their approximations with \(\mathrm{Kn}\), \(\mathrm{Ma}\) and a temperature difference, compute the pressure that keeps a velocity field solenoidal, read a velocity gradient as strain plus rotation, and decide which of two experiments obey the same scaled equations. The equations are deterministic, and nothing in this chapter explains why their solutions at high Reynolds number are irregular, or predicts the statistics of the jet record. Chapters 7 and 8 introduce the statistical description, and chapter 9 averages (6.9) to obtain the equations for the mean velocity.

Exercises

Exercise 1 (derive and check a limit). (a) For the steady velocity fields \(\mathbf U=(-wx_2,wx_1,0)\) (rigid rotation) and \(\mathbf U=(sx_1,-sx_2,0)\) (plane strain), with constants \(w\) and \(s\), show that each satisfies (6.5) and that the viscous term of (6.9) vanishes. (b) Find the pressure of each from the momentum equation (6.9) directly, and confirm that it satisfies (6.26). (c) The Poisson equation determines \(p\) only up to a function \(g\) with \(\nabla^2g=0\). Which equation removes this freedom here?

Solution

(a) Rotation: \(\partial U_1/\partial x_1+\partial U_2/\partial x_2=0+0=0\). Strain: \(s-s=0\). Both fields are linear in \(\mathbf x\), so all second derivatives vanish and \(\nu\nabla^2\mathbf U=0\).

(b) For steady flow, (6.9) reduces to \((\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U=-\rho^{-1}\nabla p\). Rotation: \((\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)U_1=U_2\,\partial U_1/\partial x_2=wx_1(-w)=-w^2x_1\) and \((\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)U_2=U_1\,\partial U_2/\partial x_1=-wx_2\,w=-w^2x_2\). So \(\nabla p=\rho w^2(x_1,x_2,0)\) and \(p=p_0+\frac12\rho w^2(x_1^2+x_2^2)\): the centripetal acceleration is supplied by a pressure that increases outward. Then \(\nabla^2p=2\rho w^2\), and (6.26) gives \(\rho(\frac12(2w)^2-0)=2\rho w^2\). Strain: \((\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)U_1=sx_1\cdot s=s^2x_1\), \((\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)U_2=(-sx_2)(-s)=s^2x_2\), so \(p=p_0-\frac12\rho s^2(x_1^2+x_2^2)\), \(\nabla^2p=-2\rho s^2\), and (6.26) gives \(\rho(0-S_{ij}S_{ij})=-\rho(s^2+s^2)=-2\rho s^2\). The rotation has a pressure minimum on its axis, the strain a pressure maximum at its stagnation point, in agreement with the sign rule of (6.26).

(c) The momentum equation itself: it fixes \(\nabla p\), not only \(\nabla^2p\). A harmonic addition \(g\), for example \(g=b\,x_1\), would add the force \(-\rho^{-1}\nabla g\), which the computed acceleration does not contain. In a general flow the boundary conditions on \(p\) play this role.

Exercise 2 (compute and check assumptions). At the running-case station (\(\langle U_1\rangle=8\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(u_0=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(\ell_0=0.1\ \mathrm m\), sea-level air), evaluate: (a) the Knudsen number at the Kolmogorov scale with the mean free path of the Standard Atmosphere and with the slide's value \(10^{-9}\ \mathrm m\); (b) the Mach number of the mean flow and of a velocity three standard deviations above the mean; (c) the relative density change caused by pressure fluctuations of order \(\rho u_0^2\). State which approximation of the chapter each number tests.

Solution

(a) \(\varepsilon\sim u_0^3/\ell_0=8/0.1=80\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\); \(\eta=((1.4607\times10^{-5})^3/80)^{1/4}=7.9\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm m\). With \(\lambda_{\mathrm m}=6.63\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm m\), \(\mathrm{Kn}=8.4\times10^{-4}\); with \(10^{-9}\ \mathrm m\), \(\mathrm{Kn}=1.3\times10^{-5}\). Both are much less than 1: the continuum hypothesis (6.1) holds at the smallest scale. The slide's value understates \(\mathrm{Kn}\) by a factor of 66, which does not change the conclusion here but would change it for a flow whose smallest scale is about \(10^{-7}\ \mathrm m\).

(b) \(\mathrm{Ma}=8/340.29=0.024\). A velocity three standard deviations above the mean is \(8+3\times2=14\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(\mathrm{Ma}=0.041\), \(\mathrm{Ma}^2=1.7\times10^{-3}\). These test the incompressible approximation (6.5) through (6.6).

(c) \(p'\sim\rho u_0^2=1.225\times4=4.9\ \mathrm{Pa}\); \(\Delta\rho/\rho\approx p'/(\rho c^2)=u_0^2/c^2=4/340.29^2=3.5\times10^{-5}\). This tests the same approximation for the fluctuations. For comparison, a 5 K temperature excess changes the density by 1.7 %, about 500 times more: at this station, heating, not pressure, is the larger cause of density variation, and it is the buoyancy and passivity assumptions of section "Conserved passive scalars and their bounds" that must be checked.

Exercise 3 (counterexample). The steady one-dimensional form of (6.15), \(U\,d\phi/dx=\Gamma\,d^2\phi/dx^2\) on \(0\le x\le L\) with \(\phi(0)=0\) and \(\phi(L)=1\), is the model problem of chapter 5, equation (5.7). Its exact solution lies between 0 and 1, as (6.16) requires. Discretize it on the nodes \(x_i=ih\), \(i=0,\dots,4\), \(h=L/4\), with central differences, \(U(\phi_{i+1}-\phi_{i-1})/(2h)=\Gamma(\phi_{i+1}-2\phi_i+\phi_{i-1})/h^2\), and take the cell Péclet number \(P=Uh/\Gamma=4\). Solve for \(\phi_1,\phi_2,\phi_3\) and show that the discrete solution violates the bound. For which \(P\) does the oscillation disappear?

Solution

Multiply by \(h^2/\Gamma\): \((1-\frac P2)\phi_{i+1}-2\phi_i+(1+\frac P2)\phi_{i-1}=0\). Try \(\phi_i=r^i\): \((1-\frac P2)r^2-2r+(1+\frac P2)=0\), with roots \(r=1\) and \(r=(1+P/2)/(1-P/2)\) (their product is \((1+P/2)/(1-P/2)\) and their sum \(2/(1-P/2)\), as required). For \(P=4\), \(r=3/(-1)=-3\). The general solution is \(\phi_i=A+B(-3)^i\). The conditions \(\phi_0=0\) and \(\phi_4=1\) give \(A=-B\) and \(B(81-1)=1\), so \(\phi_i=((-3)^i-1)/80\): \(\phi_1=-4/80=-0.05\), \(\phi_2=8/80=0.10\), \(\phi_3=-28/80=-0.35\). Check the equation at \(i=2\): \(-\phi_3-2\phi_2+3\phi_1=0.35-0.20-0.15=0\). The values \(\phi_1\) and \(\phi_3\) are below 0, the smallest boundary value, so the discrete solution violates (6.16).

The root \(r\) is positive when \(1-P/2\gt0\), that is \(P\lt2\); then \(\phi_i=(r^i-1)/(r^4-1)\) increases monotonically from 0 to 1. The maximum principle proof fails for the scheme because at a discrete maximum the central-difference advection term need not vanish. The lesson: (6.16) is a property of the equation; a discretization must be designed to keep it.

Exercise 4 (derive). (a) For an incompressible velocity field and a constant angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\varpi\), show that \(\nabla\times(2\boldsymbol\varpi\times\mathbf U)=-2(\boldsymbol\varpi\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U\). (b) Hence show that the Coriolis term can be written as the gradient of a pressure-like function only if the velocity does not change along the rotation axis. (c) Give a velocity field with \(\boldsymbol\varpi=\varpi\,\mathbf e_3\) for which the Coriolis term is not a gradient, and one for which it is.

Solution

(a) Use \(\nabla\times(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)=\mathbf a(\nabla\cdot\mathbf b)-\mathbf b(\nabla\cdot\mathbf a)+(\mathbf b\cdot\nabla)\mathbf a-(\mathbf a\cdot\nabla)\mathbf b\) with \(\mathbf a=2\boldsymbol\varpi\) and \(\mathbf b=\mathbf U\). The terms \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\varpi\) and \((\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol\varpi\) vanish because \(\boldsymbol\varpi\) is constant, and \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U=0\). What remains is \(-2(\boldsymbol\varpi\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U\).

(b) A gradient has zero curl. If \(2\boldsymbol\varpi\times\mathbf U=\nabla q\) for some \(q\), then \((\boldsymbol\varpi\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U=0\): the velocity does not change along the direction of \(\boldsymbol\varpi\). Conversely, in a simply connected region a field with zero curl is a gradient, so the condition is also sufficient.

(c) \(\mathbf U=(\gamma x_3,0,0)\), with \(\gamma\) a constant shear rate, is solenoidal and gives \((\boldsymbol\varpi\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U=\varpi\gamma\,\mathbf e_1\neq0\): the Coriolis term \(-2\varpi\gamma x_3\,\mathbf e_2\) is not a gradient and changes the flow. \(\mathbf U=(\gamma x_2,0,0)\) gives \((\boldsymbol\varpi\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U=0\); the Coriolis force per unit mass, \(-2\boldsymbol\varpi\times\mathbf U=-2\varpi\gamma x_2\,\mathbf e_2\), equals \(-\nabla(\varpi\gamma x_2^2)\) and is absorbed into the pressure. A rotating frame affects an incompressible flow only through velocity variations along the rotation axis.

Exercise 5 (design). A laboratory wants to reproduce the running-case nozzle flow (\(\mathcal L=0.025\ \mathrm m\), \(\mathcal U=56\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(\mathrm{Re}=9.6\times10^4\), sea-level air) in a model at half scale. (a) What exit velocity does a half-scale model in the same air need, and what are its Mach number and its estimate \(\frac12\mathrm{Ma}^2\)? (b) The laboratory accepts density variations of at most 2 %. Is the half-scale air model acceptable? (c) If instead the model fluid has kinematic viscosity \(\nu_m\) and speed of sound \(c_m\), what condition on \(\nu_m\) and \(c_m\) keeps both \(\mathrm{Re}\) and the incompressible approximation at least as good as in the full-size jet?

Solution

(a) \(\mathrm{Re}\) is fixed and \(\mathcal L\) halves, so \(\mathcal U\) doubles: 112 m/s. \(\mathrm{Ma}=112/340.29=0.33\) and \(\frac12\mathrm{Ma}^2=0.054\).

(b) No. The estimate (6.6) gives density variations of about 5.4 %, above the 2 % limit and four times the 1.4 % of the full-size jet. The scaled incompressible equations (6.29) would be identical, but the model would no longer be described by them to the accepted accuracy.

(c) Keeping \(\mathrm{Re}\) at half scale requires \(\mathcal U_m=\mathrm{Re}\,\nu_m/(0.0125\ \mathrm m)\). The Mach number \(\mathcal U_m/c_m\) must not exceed 0.165, so \(\mathrm{Re}\,\nu_m/(0.0125\,c_m)\le0.165\), that is \(\nu_m/c_m\le0.165\times0.0125/(9.6\times10^4)=2.15\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm m\). Air has \(\nu/c=1.4607\times10^{-5}/340.29=4.3\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm m\), twice the limit, which is the result of part (b) in another form. A model fluid with at most half the ratio \(\nu/c\) of air is needed. The values of \(\nu_m\) and \(c_m\) for a candidate fluid must be taken from a property table for that fluid at the operating temperature, and the other approximations of the chapter (no buoyancy, a Newtonian stress) must be checked for it as well.

Exercise 6 (judge a claim). The Taylor–Green vortex is \(\mathbf U=F(t)\,(\sin x_1\cos x_2,\,-\cos x_1\sin x_2,\,0)\) with \(F(t)=e^{-2\nu t}\) in dimensionless units with \(\rho=1\). (a) Show that it satisfies (6.5) and (6.9) with \(p=\frac14(\cos2x_1+\cos2x_2)F^2\). (b) Its kinetic energy decays. A student argues: "The Navier–Stokes equations are invariant under every transformation on the slide figure, so the vortex played backwards in time is also a solution." Judge the claim using the transformation (6.30).

Solution

(a) \(\partial U_1/\partial x_1+\partial U_2/\partial x_2=F\cos x_1\cos x_2-F\cos x_1\cos x_2=0\). Each component is an eigenfunction of the Laplacian: \(\nabla^2U_j=-2U_j\). So \(\nu\nabla^2\mathbf U=-2\nu\mathbf U=\partial\mathbf U/\partial t\), because \(F'=-2\nu F\). The time derivative and the viscous term therefore cancel, and (6.9) requires \((\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U=-\nabla p\). For the first component, \(U_1\partial U_1/\partial x_1+U_2\partial U_1/\partial x_2=F^2(\sin x_1\cos x_1\cos^2x_2+\cos x_1\sin x_1\sin^2x_2)=\frac12F^2\sin2x_1\), and \(-\partial p/\partial x_1=\frac12F^2\sin2x_1\). For the second component, \(U_1\partial U_2/\partial x_1+U_2\partial U_2/\partial x_2=F^2(\sin^2x_1\cos x_2\sin x_2+\cos^2x_1\sin x_2\cos x_2)=\frac12F^2\sin2x_2=-\partial p/\partial x_2\). The program of this chapter checks the same pressure through (6.12).

(b) The claim is false. Under (6.30) the reversed field is \(\hat{\mathbf U}(\mathbf x,\hat t)=-\mathbf U(\mathbf x,-\hat t)=-e^{2\nu\hat t}(\sin x_1\cos x_2,-\cos x_1\sin x_2,0)\), which grows in time. Then \(\partial\hat{\mathbf U}/\partial\hat t=2\nu\hat{\mathbf U}\), while \(\nu\nabla^2\hat{\mathbf U}=-2\nu\hat{\mathbf U}\): the reversed field satisfies the momentum equation with viscosity \(-\nu\), not \(\nu\). The slide figure lists transformations to be examined, not transformations under which the equations are invariant; time reversal is the one that fails, because the viscous term is the only term of odd order in \(\mathbf U\) without a time derivative. The kinetic energy per unit mass of the vortex, averaged over the box, is \(\frac14F^2\), which decays at the rate \(4\nu\) in the forward direction and would grow at that rate in the reversed film.
