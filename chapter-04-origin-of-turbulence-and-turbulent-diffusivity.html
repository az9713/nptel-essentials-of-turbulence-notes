The origin of turbulence and turbulent diffusivity

A radiator raises the temperature of the air next to it by about 10 K in a room 5 m across. If the air in the room did not move, the heat would spread through the room by molecular diffusion alone, and the scale estimate of this chapter gives a spreading time of about \(10^{6}\ \mathrm s\), or 13 days. The radiator's own buoyancy drives an irregular circulation of only about \(5\ \mathrm{cm/s}\), and this circulation spreads the same heat in about \(100\ \mathrm s\). The two times differ by a factor of about \(10^{4}\). This chapter asks two questions about that factor. First, under what conditions does a smooth, laminar flow become turbulent and stay turbulent? Second, how large is the mixing advantage of turbulence, and in what sense can it be described as a diffusion with an enlarged diffusivity?

Grounded in two recordings of the NPTEL course Essentials of Turbulence (Prof. Abhilash J. Chandy, IIT Bombay): Lecture 8 and Lecture 9. Lecture material: turbulence as the result of instabilities of laminar flow; critical Reynolds numbers for pipe flow (about 2100) and for the zero-pressure-gradient boundary layer (about 600, based on the displacement thickness); the inviscid instability of free shear flows; shear and buoyancy as energy sources, the decay of grid turbulence and laminarization; primary and secondary instabilities and turbulent spots; diffusivity as the outstanding characteristic of turbulence; the heated room with the molecular time \(L^2/\Gamma\), the turbulent time \(L/u\) and the buoyancy estimate of \(u\); the time ratio as the inverse Péclet and Reynolds numbers; the reading of the Reynolds number as a ratio of times, which the lecture attributes to Tennekes and Lumley, A First Course in Turbulence (1972) (no statement in this chapter relies on that book); and the eddy diffusivity \(K\sim uL\). Research extensions: Reynolds (1883) on the sensitivity of the critical velocity to disturbances and on the two critical velocities; Schubauer and Skramstad (1948) on the difference between instability and transition in a boundary layer; Meseguer and Trefethen (2003) on the linear stability of pipe flow; Rayleigh's inflection-point criterion in the reconstruction of Darrigol (2002); Blasius (1908) for the flat-plate profile; Taylor (1922) on diffusion by continuous movements; the 1976 U.S. Standard Atmosphere for the properties of air. Original teaching constructions: the heated room as the running case of every section, the air properties at 300 K computed from the Standard Atmosphere formulas, the slowest-mode check of the molecular time, the computation of the Blasius displacement thickness and of the inflection-point test, the application of Taylor's result to the room, Figure 4.1, the code and the six exercises.

The objects of the chapter and the running case

The running case is a room with a heating element (a radiator). Its size is measured by one characteristic length \(L\); for a room whose sides differ, the lecture takes \(L\) of the order of the cube root of the room volume, and uses \(L=5\ \mathrm m\). The temperature field is \(\theta(\mathbf x,t)\), in kelvin, where \(\mathbf x=(x_1,x_2,x_3)\) is position and \(t\) is time. The reference temperature of the air is \(\theta_0=300\ \mathrm K\), and \(\Delta\theta\) is a characteristic temperature difference in the room. When the air moves, its motion is irregular, and \(u\) denotes the root-mean-square (r.m.s.) amplitude of its velocity fluctuations, in \(\mathrm{m/s}\). (Chapter 5 uses the same letter for the velocity scale of the largest eddies.)

Two molecular properties of the air enter. The kinematic viscosity \(\nu\) (\(\mathrm{m^2/s}\)) is the diffusivity of momentum. The heat diffusivity \(\Gamma=k_t/(\rho c_p)\) (\(\mathrm{m^2/s}\)) is the diffusivity of temperature, where \(k_t\) is the thermal conductivity, \(\rho\) the density and \(c_p\) the specific heat at constant pressure. The slides write \(\gamma\) for this diffusivity and on two slides call it a conductivity; a conductivity has units of \(\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}K^{-1}}\), and the quantity in the equations of this chapter is the diffusivity. Their ratio is the Prandtl number \(\mathrm{Pr}=\nu/\Gamma\).

For air at \(300\ \mathrm K\) and atmospheric pressure, this chapter computes the properties from the defining equations of the U.S. Standard Atmosphere, 1976: the viscosity from its equation (51) and the thermal conductivity from its equation (53) (p. 19), the density from the ideal-gas law with the gas constant \(R=287\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}K^{-1}}\) that follows from the sea-level pressure, density and temperature of its Table 10 (101 325 Pa, 1.2250 kg/m³ and 288.15 K; p. 20), and \(c_p=3.5R\), the value for a ratio of specific heats of 1.40 (p. 18). The results are \(\nu=1.57\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), \(\Gamma=2.22\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) and \(\mathrm{Pr}=0.71\). The lecture gives \(\mathrm{Pr}=0.73\) for air; the 3 % difference has no effect on any order-of-magnitude conclusion below. The code in section "Executable checks" repeats this computation.

The chapter uses three times. \(T_m\) is the time for heat to spread through the room by molecular diffusion, \(T_t\) the time for the turbulent motion to spread it, and \(T_L\) a Lagrangian correlation time defined in section "When an eddy diffusivity is a diffusivity". The symbol \(\sim\) between two quantities means that they have the same order of magnitude: their ratio is a number that the argument does not determine. An equals sign is used only for definitions and exact results. The next three sections, on the origin of turbulence, use a second model: a laminar flow with velocity \(U(y)\) parallel to a wall or to a free stream. Its objects are defined there.

Turbulence begins as an instability of a laminar flow L8 1:27

In flows that start laminar, turbulence arises from instabilities. A laminar flow is a solution of the equations of motion. It is stable if every small enough disturbance added to it decays, and unstable if some small disturbance grows. A growing disturbance starts at some place and some time, and whether it spreads through the flow depends on the Reynolds number. The lecture gives two values at which particular flows change state.

For flow in a pipe of diameter \(D\) with bulk velocity \(U_b\) (the volume flow rate divided by the cross-section area), and for a boundary layer on a flat plate with free-stream velocity \(U_0\), the Reynolds numbers are

\[ \mathrm{Re}_D=\frac{U_bD}{\nu},\qquad \mathrm{Re}_{\delta^*}=\frac{U_0\delta^*}{\nu},\qquad \delta^*=\int_0^\infty\Bigl(1-\frac{U(y)}{U_0}\Bigr)dy. \tag{4.1} \]

Here \(y\) is the distance from the plate and \(U(y)\) the velocity profile of the layer. The displacement thickness \(\delta^*\) is the distance by which the plate would have to be moved into a uniform stream of speed \(U_0\) to remove the same volume flow as the layer removes. The lecture states that laminar pipe flow becomes turbulent at \(\mathrm{Re}_D\approx2100\), and that the boundary layer in zero pressure gradient becomes unstable at \(\mathrm{Re}_{\delta^*}\approx600\). It adds that the pipe value depends on the condition of the pipe, and that roughness makes transition occur sooner.

The pipe value depends on the disturbance. Reynolds's 1883 experiments, in which a band of coloured water was introduced into water flowing through glass tubes (p. 941), established both the existence of a critical velocity and its sensitivity: "the critical velocity was very sensitive to disturbance in the water before entering the tubes", consistent results needed still water at uniform temperature, and large disturbances made the steady motion unstable well below the critical velocity (Reynolds 1883, p. 943; Exercise 6 quotes the passage). He also found a second, lower critical velocity, below which eddies already present in the entering water die out downstream (p. 946). Comparing his colour-band and pipe-resistance experiments, he gives the two as \(1/43.79\) and \(1/278\) times the same viscosity-to-diameter factor (pp. 943, 948): steady motion in still water persisted to \(278/43.79=6.3\) times the lower value. He relates the pipe surface only to the resistance above the critical velocity (p. 948); the remark about roughness is the lecture's.

A second line of evidence comes from the linearized equations. Meseguer and Trefethen linearized the equations of motion about the laminar pipe profile, keeping only terms of first order in a small disturbance, and computed the eigenvalues of the result for Reynolds numbers from \(10^{2}\) to \(10^{7}\). An eigenvalue is the complex growth rate of a disturbance of fixed spatial form, and a negative real part means that the disturbance decays. They use a Reynolds number based on the centreline velocity and the radius (preprint pp. 1, 4); for the laminar (parabolic) profile the centreline velocity is twice the bulk velocity, so this Reynolds number equals \(U_bD/\nu\). For the disturbance modes they examine, the rightmost eigenvalues have negative real parts at every Reynolds number computed, so these modes decay (pp. 10–11); they describe the linearized operator as "eigenvalue stable". They also show that the same linearized equations amplify some disturbances transiently before they decay: for the streamwise modes the amplification is by a factor of order \(R\) on a time scale of order \(R\), with \(R\) their Reynolds number (p. 11), which is one route by which a finite disturbance can reach the size at which nonlinearity acts. They state that "transition to turbulence, like turbulence itself, depends on nonlinearity" (p. 15). They place the Reynolds number at which transition may be observed in practice at about 2000 for pipe flow (pp. 15–16) (Meseguer and Trefethen 2003). Taken together: \(\mathrm{Re}_D\approx2000\)–\(2100\) is the lower end of the range in which a pipe flow can be turbulent. In the cases computed it is not a Reynolds number at which an exponentially growing small disturbance appears. Above it, the state of the flow depends on the size and form of the disturbances at the inlet.

The boundary-layer value is an instability, not a transition. The flat-plate experiments of Schubauer and Skramstad, made in a wind tunnel whose free-stream turbulence had been reduced with damping screens, separate the two events. They state that "transition involves two things": the disturbance and its behaviour in the boundary layer, and the effect of the disturbance in bringing about turbulent flow; "sufficiently small disturbance cannot produce transition, but a small disturbance may grow according to stability theory until it is sufficiently large to cause the flow to become turbulent" (Schubauer and Skramstad 1948, p. 2). Their measurements give \(\mathrm{Re}_{\delta^*}=450\) as the experimentally established lower limit of the range in which oscillations are amplified, and show that when the initial disturbances are small, transition is delayed to \(\mathrm{Re}_{\delta^*}\approx2000\) or more (p. 30). Their Figure 7 (p. 7) shows the dependence on the free-stream turbulence level, defined as \(100\,[\tfrac13(u'^2+v'^2+w'^2)]^{1/2}/U_0\) per cent with \(u',v',w'\) the r.m.s. velocity fluctuations of the stream: read from the figure, the beginning of transition lies at \(\mathrm{Re}_x=U_0x/\nu\approx2.8\times10^{6}\) for turbulence levels below about 0.08 %, and at about \(1.5\times10^{6}\) at 0.34 %, where \(x\) is the distance from the leading edge. They also write that "if no disturbances were present, theoretically transition should not occur" (p. 30).

To compare \(\mathrm{Re}_{\delta^*}\) with \(\mathrm{Re}_x\), a relation between \(\delta^*\) and \(x\) is needed. For the laminar flat-plate profile computed by Blasius, Schubauer and Skramstad give (p. 2)

\[ \delta^*=1.72\Bigl(\frac{\nu x}{U_0}\Bigr)^{1/2},\qquad\text{so}\qquad \mathrm{Re}_{\delta^*}=1.72\,\mathrm{Re}_x^{1/2}. \tag{4.2} \]

The code in section "Executable checks" solves Blasius's equation and reproduces the coefficient as 1.7208. With (4.2), the lecture's \(\mathrm{Re}_{\delta^*}\approx600\) corresponds to \(\mathrm{Re}_x=1.2\times10^{5}\), the measured lower limit 450 to \(\mathrm{Re}_x=6.8\times10^{4}\), and the beginning of transition at low free-stream turbulence, \(\mathrm{Re}_x\approx2.8\times10^{6}\), to \(\mathrm{Re}_{\delta^*}\approx2900\). At the lowest free-stream turbulence, disturbances begin to grow 23 to 41 times closer to the leading edge than the place where transition begins (12 to 22 times at 0.34 %). The lecture's figure of a flat-plate boundary layer shows this sequence: a laminar region near the leading edge, a transition region, and a turbulent region downstream, with the turbulent velocity profile fuller near the wall than the laminar one, so that its velocity gradient at the wall is larger. The figure marks the laminar region "low mixing" and the turbulent region "high mixing"; chapter 5 estimates the consequence for the drag on the plate.

Neither number is a property of the fluid, and neither is a universal threshold. Each critical Reynolds number belongs to one flow, one definition of the length scale (\(D\) or \(\delta^*\)) and one level of disturbance.

From instability to developed turbulence. L8 10:24 The lecture describes the sequence that experiments show, and states that the mathematical details of transition are not well understood. Transition is commonly initiated by a primary instability, which in simple cases is two-dimensional. The primary instability introduces secondary motions, which are generally three-dimensional and become unstable themselves. This sequence generates intense, localized three-dimensional disturbances, called turbulent spots, which arise at random positions and random times. The spots grow, merge with each other when they become large and numerous, and form a field of developed turbulent flow. Reynolds's observation that in still water the eddies appeared in a "full-blown manner" (1883, p. 943), rather than gradually, is consistent with this picture, in which a localized region becomes turbulent before its surroundings.

Free shear layers can be unstable without viscosity L8 3:24

The lecture states that free shear flows, such as the mixing layer between two streams of different speed, become unstable at very low Reynolds number, because of an inviscid instability mechanism that does not operate in the boundary layer or in pipe flow. A free shear flow has no solid surface; its velocity gradient is created by the two streams, not by a wall. The precise form of this distinction is a criterion due to Rayleigh (1880). The derivation below follows Darrigol's reconstruction of Rayleigh's argument (Darrigol 2002, pp. 49–50), in this book's notation.

Setting. The base flow is a steady, two-dimensional, parallel flow \(U(y)\) in the \(x\) direction, of an incompressible fluid without viscosity, between two walls at \(y=y_1\) and \(y=y_2\) or in an unbounded region. A small disturbance is written with a stream function \(\psi'(x,y,t)\): its velocity components are \(\partial\psi'/\partial y\) in the \(x\) direction and \(-\partial\psi'/\partial x\) in the \(y\) direction, which satisfy continuity. In two-dimensional inviscid flow the vorticity \(\omega\) of each fluid particle, the component of the curl of the velocity normal to the plane of the flow, is constant in time. To see this, write the total velocity as \(\mathbf U=(U+\partial\psi'/\partial y,\,-\partial\psi'/\partial x)\), with \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf U=0\), and take the curl of the inviscid momentum equation \(\partial\mathbf U/\partial t+(\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U=-\nabla p/\rho\), where \(p\) is the pressure and \(\rho\) the density. The curl of \(\nabla p/\rho\) is zero because \(\rho\) is constant, and the result is \(\partial\boldsymbol\omega/\partial t+(\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol\omega=(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U\), where \(\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf U\) points normal to the plane and has normal component \(\omega\). The right side is \(\omega\,\partial\mathbf U/\partial z\), with \(z\) the coordinate normal to the plane, and it vanishes because the flow does not vary in that direction. Hence \(\partial\omega/\partial t+(\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\omega=0\): the vorticity of each particle is conserved (Darrigol 2002, eq. (19), p. 49; chapter 6 derives the general equation, in which the right side, vortex stretching, does not vanish). The base vorticity is \(-U'(y)\), where a prime on \(U\) denotes \(d/dy\), and the disturbance vorticity is \(-\nabla^2\psi'\). Keeping only terms of first order in the disturbance,

\[ \Bigl(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+U\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\Bigr)\nabla^2\psi'-U''\frac{\partial\psi'}{\partial x}=0 . \]

The second term is the \(y\) component of the disturbance velocity carrying the base vorticity across its gradient. Each Fourier component in \(x\) evolves independently, so take \(\psi'\) to be the real part of \(\hat\psi(y)\,e^{i\kappa(x-ct)}\), where \(\kappa\gt0\) is the wavenumber (radians per metre), \(\hat\psi(y)\) the complex amplitude, and \(c=c_r+ic_i\) a complex phase speed. The disturbance grows as \(e^{\kappa c_it}\), so \(c_i\gt0\) means instability. Substitution gives Rayleigh's stability equation

\[ (U-c)\bigl(\hat\psi''-\kappa^2\hat\psi\bigr)-U''\hat\psi=0, \tag{4.3} \]

with \(\hat\psi=0\) at the walls (no flow through them) or \(\hat\psi\to0\) as \(\lvert y\rvert\to\infty\). Darrigol's equation (21) is the same equation written for the amplitude of the \(y\) component of the disturbance velocity, which is \(-i\kappa\hat\psi\).

The hinge step. Suppose a growing mode exists, \(c_i\gt0\). Then \(U-c\neq0\) for every real \(U\), and (4.3) can be divided by \(U-c\). Multiply by the complex conjugate \(\hat\psi^*\) and integrate across the flow. Integration by parts, with the boundary conditions, turns \(\int\hat\psi^*\hat\psi''dy\) into \(-\int\lvert\hat\psi'\rvert^2dy\), and

\[ \int\Bigl(\lvert\hat\psi'\rvert^2+\kappa^2\lvert\hat\psi\rvert^2\Bigr)dy+\int\frac{U''\lvert\hat\psi\rvert^2}{U-c}\,dy=0. \tag{4.4} \]

The first integral is real. Writing \(1/(U-c)=(U-c_r+ic_i)/\lvert U-c\rvert^2\), the imaginary part of (4.4) is

\[ c_i\int\frac{U''\,\lvert\hat\psi\rvert^2}{\lvert U-c\rvert^2}\,dy=0. \tag{4.5} \]

With \(c_i\gt0\), the integral must vanish. Its weight \(\lvert\hat\psi\rvert^2/\lvert U-c\rvert^2\) is non-negative and not identically zero, so \(U''\) must take both signs in the flow. A necessary condition for an inviscid, two-dimensional, linear instability of a parallel flow is that the velocity profile has a point of inflection, where \(U''\) changes sign. Darrigol (p. 50) records that Rayleigh drew the same conclusion and noted that the criterion gives no answer for a jet, whose profile has inflection points.

Three profiles. A model of the mixing layer is \(U=U_0\tanh(y/\delta)\), with \(U_0\) half the velocity difference and \(\delta\) a thickness. Its second derivative is \(U''=-(2U_0/\delta^2)\tanh(y/\delta)\,\mathrm{sech}^2(y/\delta)\), which changes sign at \(y=0\); the criterion permits an inviscid instability. Plane Poiseuille flow between walls, \(U=U_c(1-y^2/d^2)\) with centreline speed \(U_c\) and half-width \(d\), has \(U''=-2U_c/d^2\lt0\) everywhere; the criterion excludes an inviscid instability. For the Blasius boundary layer, Blasius writes the velocity as \(U=\tfrac12U_0\zeta'(\xi)\), with \(\xi=\tfrac12\bigl(U_0/(\nu x)\bigr)^{1/2}y\), and the profile function satisfies \(\zeta\zeta''=-\zeta'''\) with \(\zeta(0)=\zeta'(0)=0\) and \(\zeta'\to2\) far from the plate (Blasius 1908, NACA TM 1256, p. 7). Then \(U''\) has the sign of \(\zeta'''=-\zeta\zeta''\). The stream function \(\zeta\) is non-negative and the shear \(\zeta''\) is positive in the computed solution, so \(U''\le0\) everywhere, with equality at the wall: no inflection point. The code checks this sign on the computed profile.

Boundary of the result. The criterion is necessary, not sufficient: an inflection point permits an instability but does not prove one. It is inviscid, and viscosity can create an instability that the inviscid equation excludes. Plane Poiseuille flow, which has no inflection point, has an eigenvalue instability at a Reynolds number of about 5772 (Meseguer and Trefethen 2003, p. 15; the page does not state the velocity and length in that Reynolds number, so the value is quoted as an order of magnitude), and the oscillations that Schubauer and Skramstad measured in the Blasius layer are of this viscous kind: they compare their measurements with the stability theory of Tollmien and Schlichting, in which the stability depends on the kinematic viscosity, the boundary-layer thickness, the outer speed and the frequency or wavelength of the disturbance (p. 1), and they report "good agreement with theory" (p. 30). The lecture's boundary-layer value \(\mathrm{Re}_{\delta^*}\approx600\) therefore refers to a viscous instability, which requires a Reynolds number of several hundred, whereas the inflectional instability of a free shear layer requires no minimum Reynolds number in the inviscid theory. Finally, the criterion concerns linear disturbances of a parallel flow; it says nothing about the finite disturbances that decide transition in a pipe.

Turbulence needs a supply of energy L8 4:15

An instability starts turbulence; it does not keep it going. Turbulence cannot maintain itself. It depends on its environment for energy, and the common sources are shear (a mean velocity gradient) and buoyancy (density differences in a gravitational field). If turbulence arrives in an environment with no shear or other maintenance mechanism, it decays: its Reynolds number decreases and the flow tends to become laminar again. Chapter 5 estimates the rate at which turbulent kinetic energy is dissipated.

The heated room shows both cases. The radiator is a buoyancy source: the air it heats is lighter than the surrounding air and rises, and this sets the room air in motion. If the radiator is switched off, the source is removed, the motion decays, and the Reynolds number of the motion decreases with it. The lecture's laboratory example is turbulence produced by a grid of bars in the uniform flow of a wind tunnel. Behind the grid there is no mean shear and no buoyancy to supply energy, so the turbulence decays with distance from the grid; the lecture recalls Taylor's hypothesis in connection with this experiment: the turbulence is carried past a fixed probe by the mean flow, so a distance \(x\) downstream of the grid corresponds to an elapsed time \(x/U_0\), with \(U_0\) the mean speed of the tunnel flow.

A flow can also be made laminar, or prevented from becoming turbulent, by a mechanism that removes turbulent kinetic energy. The lecture names two: an imposed magnetic field in an electrically conducting fluid at low magnetic Reynolds number (the Reynolds number formed with the magnetic diffusivity of the conductor in place of \(\nu\), which compares the transport of the magnetic field by the flow with its diffusion through the conductor), and a stable density stratification in the atmosphere, in which denser fluid lies below lighter fluid and vertical motion must work against gravity. The second has a direct counterpart in the room, offered here as an illustration: a heater near the ceiling makes the warm, light air collect at the top, which is a stable stratification, whereas a radiator near the floor heats the air from below and drives the circulation.

Without air motion, heat crosses the room in days L8 12:32

The lecture calls diffusivity the outstanding characteristic of turbulent motion: its ability to transport and mix momentum, kinetic energy and contaminants such as heat, particles and moisture. The rates of transfer and mixing are several orders of magnitude greater than the rates due to molecular diffusion; the lecture's example is the combustion in a fire, whose heat-transfer and combustion rates exceed those in the laminar flame of a candle by orders of magnitude. The heated room makes the comparison quantitative. It is a problem with an imposed length scale: the room size \(L\) is fixed, and the unknown is the time.

If the air does not move, heat spreads only by molecular diffusion, and the temperature obeys the heat equation

\[ \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}=\Gamma\,\frac{\partial^2\theta}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_i}, \tag{4.6} \]

with summation over the repeated index \(i\). There is no advection term because there is no motion. To estimate the time without solving (4.6), replace each term by its order of magnitude. A temperature change of order \(\Delta\theta\) occurs over a time \(T_m\) and over a distance \(L\), so the left side is of order \(\Delta\theta/T_m\) and the right side of order \(\Gamma\Delta\theta/L^2\). Equating them,

\[ T_m\sim\frac{L^2}{\Gamma}=\frac{(5\ \mathrm m)^2}{2.22\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}}=1.1\times10^{6}\ \mathrm s\approx310\ \mathrm h. \tag{4.7} \]

The subscript \(m\) marks the molecular process. The lecture states the result as "of the order of \(10^{6}\ \mathrm s\) (more than 100 hrs)", and concludes that molecular diffusion is ineffective in distributing heat through a room. The estimate needs only the room size and a property of the fluid.

What the estimate leaves out. An exact solution shows the size of the omitted constant. Model the room as a cube of side \(L\) with walls that conduct no heat, so that \(\partial\theta/\partial x_i=0\) on the walls. Separation of variables gives solutions of (4.6) of the form \(\cos(n_1\pi x_1/L)\cos(n_2\pi x_2/L)\cos(n_3\pi x_3/L)\,e^{-\pi^2(n_1^2+n_2^2+n_3^2)\Gamma t/L^2}\) with non-negative integers \(n_i\); each cosine has zero slope at both walls. The mode with all \(n_i=0\) is the final uniform temperature. Every other mode decays, and the slowest has one \(n_i=1\), so any initial non-uniformity decays at least as fast as

\[ e^{-t/\tau_1},\qquad \tau_1=\frac{L^2}{\pi^2\Gamma}=\frac{T_m}{\pi^2}=1.1\times10^{5}\ \mathrm s\approx32\ \mathrm h. \tag{4.8} \]

The e-folding time of the slowest mode is a factor \(\pi^2\approx9.9\) shorter than \(L^2/\Gamma\). Another definition of "spreading time", such as the time for the temperature differences to fall to 10 % of their initial value, gives another constant: \(\tau_1\ln10=73\ \mathrm h\) for the slowest mode. The scaling \(L^2/\Gamma\) is common to all of them, and the constant is fixed only after the definition is fixed. The insulated-wall model is an idealization; real walls exchange heat with the outside.

A weak buoyant stir mixes the room in minutes L8 21:21

Even weak motions, such as those generated by small density differences, disperse the heat through the room quickly. Suppose that the turbulent motion of the air can also be characterized by the length \(L\), although motions of scales much smaller than \(L\) are present too, and by the r.m.s. velocity \(u\). The time for the motion to carry heat across the room is then

\[ T_t\sim\frac{L}{u}. \tag{4.9} \]

Unlike \(T_m\), this time cannot be computed from the size of the room and the properties of air. It requires \(u\), a property of the flow.

An estimate of \(u\). The lecture estimates \(u\) from the buoyancy of the heated air. Air heated by \(\Delta\theta=10\ \mathrm K\) above \(\theta_0=300\ \mathrm K\) is lighter than its surroundings by the fraction \(\Delta\theta/\theta_0\) (for an ideal gas at constant pressure, density is inversely proportional to absolute temperature), and it experiences a buoyant acceleration

\[ g\frac{\Delta\theta}{\theta_0}=9.81\times\frac{10}{300}=0.33\ \mathrm{m/s^2},\qquad gh\frac{\Delta\theta}{\theta_0}=0.033\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}, \tag{4.10} \]

with \(g\) the acceleration of gravity. The second quantity is the work per unit mass done by the buoyant acceleration over the height \(h=0.1\ \mathrm m\) of the radiator, which becomes kinetic energy of the rising air. The lecture converts it to a velocity of \(17\ \mathrm{cm/s}\), which is \((gh\Delta\theta/\theta_0)^{1/2}=0.18\ \mathrm{m/s}\); setting the kinetic energy per unit mass equal to \(\tfrac12V^2\) gives instead \(V=(2gh\Delta\theta/\theta_0)^{1/2}=0.26\ \mathrm{m/s}\). The factor \(\sqrt2\) between them is below the resolution of the argument. The lecture then takes \(u\approx5\ \mathrm{cm/s}\), about one third of the plume speed, as a reasonable r.m.s. value averaged over the whole room. This step is an assumption, not a derivation. With \(u=0.05\ \mathrm{m/s}\) and \(L=5\ \mathrm m\), (4.9) gives \(T_t=100\ \mathrm s\), about 2 minutes.

The molecular estimate (4.7) and the turbulent estimate (4.9) differ by \(1.1\times10^{6}/100\approx1.1\times10^{4}\). Even with the factor \(\pi^2\) of (4.8) applied to the molecular time and a factor of several applied to the uncertain \(u\), the turbulent time is shorter by three to four orders of magnitude.

Molecular diffusion still finishes the mixing. The turbulence does not replace molecular diffusion. The lecture states that the turbulence in the room generates eddies as small as about 1 cm, an estimate from the dissipation equations that the course develops later (chapter 5 derives the length scale of the smallest eddies in general; the room's value is the lecture's). The temperature differences across such an eddy are smoothed by molecular diffusion in a time of order \(\ell^2/\Gamma\), with \(\ell\) the eddy size: for \(\ell=1\ \mathrm{cm}\), \(\ell^2/\Gamma=(0.01)^2/(2.22\times10^{-5})=4.5\ \mathrm s\). The mechanism therefore has two steps. The large-scale motion carries warm and cool air across the room in a time of order \(L/u\) and interleaves them in thin layers; molecular diffusion removes the temperature differences between neighbouring layers in seconds, because the distance it must cover has been reduced from 5 m to 1 cm. The rate of the whole process is set by the slow step, the transport by the large eddies.

The ratio of the two times is a Péclet number L9 6:01

The ratio of the two time scales is

\[ \frac{T_t}{T_m}\sim\frac{L}{u}\,\frac{\Gamma}{L^2}=\frac{\Gamma}{uL}=\frac1{\mathrm{Pe}},\qquad \mathrm{Pe}\equiv\frac{uL}{\Gamma}=\mathrm{Pr}\,\mathrm{Re},\qquad \mathrm{Re}\equiv\frac{uL}{\nu}. \tag{4.11} \]

The Péclet number \(\mathrm{Pe}\) has the form of a Reynolds number with the heat diffusivity in place of the momentum diffusivity. The identity \(\mathrm{Pe}=\mathrm{Pr}\,\mathrm{Re}\) follows from the definitions. For gases \(\mathrm{Pr}\) is of order one, so \(T_t/T_m\sim1/\mathrm{Re}\), which is the slide's form of the result. For the room, \(\mathrm{Pe}=0.25/(2.22\times10^{-5})=1.1\times10^{4}\) and \(\mathrm{Re}=0.25/(1.57\times10^{-5})=1.6\times10^{4}\); the lecture gives \(\mathrm{Re}=15000\). At fixed \(L\), a more vigorous motion (larger \(u\)) raises \(\mathrm{Re}\) and lowers \(T_t/T_m\): the advantage of turbulent over molecular mixing grows in proportion to the Reynolds number.

Equation (4.11) holds only while the motion is turbulent and fills the room. It compares an advection time with a diffusion time, and it would give \(T_t=T_m\) at \(\mathrm{Pe}=1\), that is at \(u=\Gamma/L=4.4\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m/s}\); a motion that slow is not turbulent (exercise 1 examines this limit). Chapter 5 treats the reverse problem, with an imposed time and an unknown length, for the atmospheric boundary layer.

The Reynolds number as a ratio of times. The slides state that "\(\mathrm{Re}\) may be interpreted as a ratio of a turbulence time scale to a molecular time scale that would prevail in the absence of turbulence in a problem with the same length scale". With \(\nu\) as the molecular diffusivity, \(L^2/\nu\) is the time for momentum to diffuse across the room, and \((L^2/\nu)/(L/u)=uL/\nu=\mathrm{Re}\). The lecture attributes to Tennekes and Lumley the view that this reading is often more reliable than the usual reading of \(\mathrm{Re}\) as the ratio of the inertial term \((\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U\) to the viscous term \(\nu\nabla^2\mathbf U\) in the Navier–Stokes equations, where \(\mathbf U\) is the velocity field. Their reason, as the slide states it, is that at high Reynolds number viscous and other diffusion effects operate on smaller length scales than inertia effects. In the lecture's reasoning, the viscous term is important at the smallest eddies, about 1 cm across in the room, and the inertial term at the large and intermediate scales, so a ratio of the two formed with one length \(L\) compares terms that act at different scales; the time-ratio reading uses only \(u\), \(L\) and a molecular diffusivity. This chapter adopts neither reading as the definition; the judgement about reliability is the lecture's, attributed to Tennekes and Lumley.

The eddy diffusivity L9 14:32

Since turbulence mixes as diffusion does, only faster, it is tempting to describe it by the heat equation with an enlarged diffusivity:

\[ \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}=K\,\frac{\partial^2\theta}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_i}, \tag{4.12} \]

where \(K\) is a representative, or effective, diffusivity, often called the eddy diffusivity. The lecture names the cost of this step: it treats turbulence as a property of the fluid, like \(\Gamma\), rather than as a property of the flow, which is "conceptually very dangerous" but "mathematically much easier to understand". The lecture calls the process that (4.12) describes a hypothetical turbulent diffusion process, because the actual transport is advection by eddies, not the molecular process that a second derivative represents.

For (4.12) to represent the room even crudely, \(K\) must be chosen so that the time scale of the hypothetical diffusion equals that of the actual mixing. The time scale of (4.12) follows as in (4.7), \(T\sim L^2/K\), and setting it equal to \(T_t\sim L/u\) gives

\[ K\sim uL,\qquad \frac K\Gamma\sim\frac{uL}{\Gamma}=\mathrm{Pe},\qquad \frac K\nu\sim\frac{uL}\nu=\mathrm{Re}. \tag{4.13} \]

For the room, \(K\sim0.05\times5=0.25\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), \(1.1\times10^{4}\) times \(\Gamma\) and \(1.6\times10^{4}\) times \(\nu\). The ratio of the eddy diffusivity to the molecular diffusivity is the Reynolds (or Péclet) number of the motion, so a higher Reynolds number means a larger eddy diffusivity relative to the molecular one. Chapter 5 uses the same estimate, with the eddy size in place of \(L\), for the atmospheric boundary layer.

Equation (4.13) is a calibration, not a prediction. \(K\) was chosen to reproduce \(T_t\), which was itself an estimate; (4.12) then adds a claim that the estimate did not contain, namely that the heat spreads in the way molecular diffusion does, with a spreading distance that grows as the square root of time. Whether that claim holds is a separate question, and the next section answers it.

When an eddy diffusivity is a diffusivity

Taylor examined the question in a paper read to the London Mathematical Society in 1920, starting from the observation that "turbulent motion is capable of diffusing heat and other diffusible properties through the interior of a fluid in much the same way that molecular agitation gives rise to molecular diffusion" (Taylor 1922, p. 196). His setting is a fluid whose turbulent motion is uniformly distributed, so that the average conditions are the same at every point (p. 206), and whose mean square velocity does not change with time (p. 207). This chapter applies it with no mean flow. Follow one fluid particle from \(t=0\). Let \(u_p(t)\) be its velocity in the \(x\) direction and \(X(t)=\int_0^tu_p(t')\,dt'\) its displacement. Assume that the statistics of \(u_p\) are stationary, that is, unchanged by a shift of the time origin. Then \(\langle u_p^2\rangle=u^2\) is constant, where \(\langle\cdot\rangle\) is the average over many particles, and the Lagrangian autocorrelation \(R_L(s)=\langle u_p(t)u_p(t+s)\rangle/u^2\), the correlation between the velocity of the particle at one instant and its velocity a time \(s\) later, depends on \(s\) only, not on \(t\), with \(R_L(0)=1\).

The effective diffusivity. Let the temperature at \(t=0\) increase uniformly with \(x\), \(\theta(x,0)=\theta_0+\beta x\) with gradient \(\beta\), and let each particle keep its temperature (molecular diffusion is neglected over the time considered). A particle that crosses the plane at \(x\) at time \(t\) started at \(x-X\), so its temperature is \(\theta_0+\beta(x-X)\), which differs by \(-\beta X\) from the initial temperature at the plane. The heat it carries per unit volume, relative to the plane, is \(-\rho c_p\beta X\), and the mean rate at which heat crosses unit area of the plane is the mean of the velocity times this excess, \(-\rho c_p\beta\,\langle u_pX\rangle\) (Taylor 1922, p. 197; he writes \(\sigma\) for the specific heat). Since \(u_p=dX/dt\), \(u_pX=\tfrac12\,d(X^2)/dt\), and averaging over particles gives \(\langle u_pX\rangle=\tfrac12\,d\langle X^2\rangle/dt\) (p. 197). A diffusion process with diffusivity \(K\) in the same gradient would carry the heat flux \(-\rho c_pK\beta\). Equating the two defines the effective diffusivity

\[ K(t)=\frac12\frac{d\langle X^2\rangle}{dt}. \tag{4.14} \]

\(K\) is a constant, as in (4.12), only if \(\langle X^2\rangle\) grows linearly with time, and the correlation decides whether it does. Write \(X(t)=\int_0^tu_p(t')\,dt'\) inside the average: \(\langle u_p(t)X(t)\rangle=\int_0^t\langle u_p(t)u_p(t')\rangle\,dt'=u^2\int_0^tR_L(t-t')\,dt'=u^2\int_0^tR_L(s)\,ds\), where the second step uses stationarity: the correlation depends only on the separation of the two times, so \(\langle u_p(t)u_p(t')\rangle=u^2R_L(t'-t)\). Stationarity also makes \(R_L\) even, because \(\langle u_p(t)u_p(t+s)\rangle=\langle u_p(t-s)u_p(t)\rangle\) gives \(R_L(s)=R_L(-s)\), so \(R_L(t'-t)=R_L(t-t')\). The third step substitutes \(s=t-t'\). With \(\langle u_pX\rangle=\tfrac12\,d\langle X^2\rangle/dt\), this gives \(d\langle X^2\rangle/dt=2u^2\int_0^tR_L(s)\,ds\); integrating once more from \(0\) to \(t\), with \(X(0)=0\), gives Taylor's central result (p. 207, eqs. (15)–(18)),

\[ \langle X^2\rangle=2u^2\int_0^t\!\!\int_0^{t'}R_L(s)\,ds\,dt'. \tag{4.15} \]

Its two limits answer the question (pp. 207–208). For times so short that \(R_L\approx1\), \(\langle X^2\rangle=u^2t^2\): the particle moves in a straight line and its r.m.s. displacement grows in proportion to \(t\). If the correlation falls off so that \(T_L=\int_0^\infty R_L(s)\,ds\) is finite (Taylor writes \(I\)), then for times long compared with \(T_L\)

\[ \frac{d\langle X^2\rangle}{dt}\to2u^2T_L,\qquad K\to u^2T_L,\qquad \langle X^2\rangle^{1/2}\propto t^{1/2}. \tag{4.16} \]

Only in this second limit does turbulent dispersion behave as a diffusion. The Lagrangian integral time \(T_L\) measures how long a particle remembers its velocity, and \(K=u^2T_L\) is the product of a velocity and the distance \(uT_L\) that a particle travels while it remembers. The estimate \(K\sim uL\) of (4.13) is the same statement with \(uT_L\sim L\), that is with \(T_L\sim L/u\). Both factors are properties of the flow, which is the precise sense of the lecture's warning that turbulence is not a property of the fluid.

An example with all times. Take \(R_L(s)=e^{-\lvert s\rvert/T_L}\), an exponential correlation (a teaching choice; Taylor's result does not require it). The inner integral of (4.15) is \(T_L(1-e^{-t'/T_L})\), and the outer integral gives

\[ \langle X^2\rangle=2u^2T_L\Bigl[t-T_L\bigl(1-e^{-t/T_L}\bigr)\Bigr]. \tag{4.17} \]

Expanding the exponential to second order recovers \(u^2t^2\) at small \(t\), and the bracket tends to \(t-T_L\) at large \(t\). At \(t=T_L\), (4.17) gives \(\langle X^2\rangle=0.74\,u^2T_L^2\), whereas the diffusion formula \(2Kt\) with \(K=u^2T_L\) gives \(2u^2T_L^2\), 2.7 times more, and the r.m.s. spreads differ by a factor of 1.65. At \(t=10T_L\) the two values of \(\langle X^2\rangle\) are \(18.0\) and \(20\) in units of \(u^2T_L^2\), so the diffusion formula overstates the r.m.s. spread by 5 %. Figure 4.1 shows the whole curve.

The room. No measurement of \(T_L\) in the room is available, but the estimate \(K\sim uL\) corresponds to \(T_L\sim L/u=T_t\approx100\ \mathrm s\). The diffusion description is then accurate to 5 % in the r.m.s. spread only after about \(10T_L\approx1000\ \mathrm s\), and in that time a particle moving at \(5\ \mathrm{cm/s}\) travels 50 m, ten room lengths, so it has met the walls many times. The room never reaches the regime in which \(K\) is a diffusivity in Taylor's sense; there, \(K\sim uL\) is a calibrated mixing rate, and (4.12) predicts the time of mixing to within its undetermined constant but not the shape of the temperature field while the mixing proceeds. This is the precise content of the lecture's description of (4.12) as a hypothetical diffusion process. Taylor's result applies in its own form to dispersion over distances large compared with \(uT_L\), for example a contaminant spreading far from its source in a large flow. Its assumptions are statistical homogeneity, stationarity and the absence of a mean flow; the room satisfies none of them exactly, and a mean shear, in particular, changes the dispersion in ways that (4.15) does not include.

    """RK4 for Blasius's z''' = -z z'' with z(0)=z'(0)=0, z''(0)=s."""
    d, f = xi_max/n, rhs

    y[0] = 0.0, 0.0, s

    return np.linspace(0, xi_max, n + 1), y

What the arguments establish and what they do not

The chapter supplies three skills: to classify a critical Reynolds number by its flow, its length scale and its disturbance level; to estimate a mixing time from \(L\), \(u\) and \(\Gamma\), with its undetermined constant; and to decide from \(T_L\) whether an eddy diffusivity is a diffusivity. The critical Reynolds numbers are empirical: each belongs to one flow, one length scale in its definition and one disturbance environment, the pipe value near 2000 is not a threshold of linear instability in the cases computed, and the boundary-layer values 450 and 600 mark the growth of small waves, not transition.

Rayleigh's criterion is a derivation, with stated assumptions: inviscid, two-dimensional, parallel, linear; it is necessary, not sufficient, for instability.

The room estimates are scale arguments that fix \(T_m\propto L^2/\Gamma\), \(T_t\propto L/u\) and \(K\propto uL\) and leave the constants undetermined. The ratio \(T_m/T_t\) is \(1.1\times10^{4}\) with the scale estimate (4.7) and \(1.1\times10^{3}\) with the slowest-mode time (4.8), and each value carries the uncertainty of the assumed \(u\). The eddy-diffusivity equation (4.12) is a model, a diffusion only for times long compared with \(T_L\) in homogeneous, stationary turbulence; later chapters introduce the turbulent viscosity \(\nu_T\) and the turbulent diffusivity \(\Gamma_T\) as models of the same kind.

Exercise 1 (check an assumption). Equation (4.11) suggests that turbulent mixing loses its advantage when \(\mathrm{Pe}\) falls to 1. (a) \(T_t=T_m\) means \(L/u=L^2/\Gamma\), so \(u=\Gamma/L=2.22\times10^{-5}/5=4.4\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m/s}\), about 4 μm/s. Its Reynolds number is \(uL/\nu=\Gamma/\nu=1/\mathrm{Pr}=1.4\).

A motion with Reynolds number 1.4 is far below the critical values of section "Turbulence begins as an instability of a laminar flow" (hundreds to thousands for the flows considered there) and is not turbulent. At \(\mathrm{Pe}\sim1\) the comparison is between molecular diffusion and a slow, smooth motion, and the formula describes neither the turbulent mixing it was built for nor the details of that motion. The break-even velocity lies outside the domain of the assumption that produced it.

(c) In a steady roll the fluid moves along closed streamlines. Advection carries heat around each streamline in a time of order \(L/u\), but it does not carry heat from one streamline to the next; that step is done by molecular diffusion across the streamlines, over distances that remain of order \(L\) unless the motion reduces them. The turbulent motion reduces them: it carries warm and cool air into layers about 1 cm apart, which molecular diffusion equalizes in about 4.5 s (section "A weak buoyant stir mixes the room in minutes"). The Péclet number alone does not decide the mixing time; the irregular, multi-scale character of the motion does.

Exercise 3 (counterexample). Plane Couette flow is the flow between two parallel walls, one at rest at \(y=0\) and one moving at speed \(U_w\) at \(y=d\), with the linear profile \(U=U_wy/d\). (a) \(U''=0\), so the second integral in (4.4) vanishes for any \(c\) with \(c_i\neq0\), and (4.4) becomes \(\int_0^d(\lvert\hat\psi'\rvert^2+\kappa^2\lvert\hat\psi\rvert^2)\,dy=0\). Both terms are non-negative, so \(\hat\psi=0\) everywhere: no mode with \(c_i\gt0\) exists. The inflection-point criterion gives the same conclusion, since \(U''\) does not change sign; here the stronger statement follows because \(U''\) is identically zero.

(b) The inviscid linear analysis excludes exponentially growing small disturbances, yet the flow becomes turbulent in practice at a finite Reynolds number. (The definition of the Reynolds number used in that source for plane Couette flow is not stated on the page cited, so the value 350 is quoted only as an order of magnitude.) A linear stability result answers whether infinitesimal disturbances grow. Transition is decided by finite disturbances and by nonlinearity, as in pipe flow (section "Turbulence begins as an instability of a laminar flow"). Linear stability is therefore not a sufficient condition for a flow to stay laminar.

Exercise 4 (derive and compute). (a) Derive (4.17) from (4.15) for \(R_L(s)=e^{-\lvert s\rvert/T_L}\), and show that it reduces to \(u^2t^2\) for \(t\ll T_L\) and to \(2u^2T_L(t-T_L)\) for \(t\gg T_L\). (b) The diffusion formula \(\langle X^2\rangle=2u^2T_Lt\) overstates the r.m.s. spread. Find the time after which the overstatement is less than 10 %. (c) Evaluate that time for the room, with \(T_L\sim L/u\).

Solution

(a) The inner integral is \(\int_0^{t'}e^{-s/T_L}ds=T_L(1-e^{-t'/T_L})\). The outer integral is \(T_L\int_0^t(1-e^{-t'/T_L})dt'=T_L[t-T_L(1-e^{-t/T_L})]\). Multiplying by \(2u^2\) gives (4.17). For \(t\ll T_L\), \(1-e^{-t/T_L}=t/T_L-\tfrac12(t/T_L)^2+O((t/T_L)^3)\), so the bracket is \(t^2/(2T_L)\) to leading order and \(\langle X^2\rangle=u^2t^2\). For \(t\gg T_L\), \(e^{-t/T_L}\to0\) and \(\langle X^2\rangle\to2u^2T_L(t-T_L)\).

(b) With \(t\) in units of \(T_L\), the ratio of the r.m.s. spreads is \([t/(t-1+e^{-t})]^{1/2}\). Setting it equal to 1.1 and solving numerically (by bisection) gives \(t=5.74\): after about \(5.7T_L\), the overstatement is below 10 %. For large \(t\) the ratio is close to \([t/(t-1)]^{1/2}\approx1+1/(2t)\), so the overstatement decays only as \(1/(2t)\). The shift \(-T_L\) in \(2u^2T_L(t-T_L)\) is the memory of the initial straight-line motion.

(c) With \(T_L\sim L/u=100\ \mathrm s\), the time is about 570 s, nearly 10 minutes, while the mixing time estimated in section "A weak buoyant stir mixes the room in minutes" is about 2 minutes. During the mixing, the room is not in the diffusive regime. The value \(T_L\sim L/u\) is itself only the estimate implied by \(K\sim uL\).

Exercise 5 (judge a model). A modeller predicts the temperature in the room 10 s after the radiator is switched on by solving (4.12) with \(K=uL=0.25\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). Assume the exponential correlation of (4.17) with \(T_L=100\ \mathrm s\) and \(u=0.05\ \mathrm{m/s}\). (a) Compare the r.m.s. displacement of an air particle after 10 s with the diffusion prediction \((2Kt)^{1/2}\). (b) Does the model spread heat too fast or too slowly at early times, and by what factor? (c) State the conditions under which the modeller's approach would be justified.

Solution

(a) With \(t/T_L=0.1\), (4.17) gives \(\langle X^2\rangle=2[0.1-(1-e^{-0.1})](uT_L)^2=0.00967\times(5\ \mathrm m)^2\), so the r.m.s. displacement is \(0.0984\times5\ \mathrm m=0.49\ \mathrm m\). This is close to the straight-line value \(ut=0.5\ \mathrm m\). The diffusion prediction is \((2\times0.25\times10)^{1/2}=2.2\ \mathrm m\).

(b) The diffusion model spreads the heat too fast at early times, by a factor of 4.5 in r.m.s. distance. The reason is structural: diffusion with a constant \(K\) spreads as \(t^{1/2}\), which at times short compared with \(T_L\) exceeds the actual spread, which grows as \(t\) from zero.

(c) The constant-\(K\) model is justified for times long compared with \(T_L\), by Exercise 4 more than about \(6T_L\) for a 10 % error in spread, and for distances over which the turbulence is statistically homogeneous. In the room both conditions fail during the first minutes, so the model should be used, if at all, only for the order of magnitude of the total mixing time, which is the quantity \(K\) was calibrated to give.

Exercise 6 (design and judge). A design guide states: "pipe flow is laminar below \(\mathrm{Re}_D=2100\) and turbulent above." (a) Using Reynolds (1883) and Meseguer and Trefethen (2003), state which part of the statement the evidence supports and which part it does not. (b) A laboratory needs laminar flow in a pipe at \(\mathrm{Re}_D=8000\). Using Reynolds's descriptions, list the precautions that make this possible, and state what makes it fragile.

Solution

(a) The first part is supported: Reynolds found a lower critical velocity below which eddies present in the entering water die out downstream (p. 946), and Meseguer and Trefethen place the lowest Reynolds number at which transition is observed in pipe flow at about 2000 (pp. 15–16). The second part is not: Reynolds's still-water experiments kept the flow steady up to a velocity \(278/43.79=6.3\) times his lower critical velocity (pp. 943, 948). If the lower value is identified with \(\mathrm{Re}_D\approx2000\), a combination of the two sources that neither of them makes, the upper value corresponds to about \(1.3\times10^{4}\). Above about 2000 the flow can be laminar or turbulent, depending on the disturbances. A statement consistent with the evidence is: "below about 2000, disturbances decay and the flow becomes laminar; above it, the flow becomes turbulent if the disturbances are large enough."

(b) Reynolds's tubes were fitted with "trumpet mouthpieces, so that water might enter without disturbance" (p. 941), drew water from a large tank, and gave consistent results only with "the greatest care as to the uniformity of the temperature of the tank and the stillness of the water" (p. 943). The design therefore needs a smooth, rounded inlet, a settled supply with no circulation and uniform temperature. With the identification made in (a), \(\mathrm{Re}_D=8000\) lies within the range that his still-water experiments reached. The flow is fragile because "the steady motion was unstable for large disturbances long before the critical velocity was reached" (p. 943): one disturbance large enough, for example from a pump or a valve, makes it turbulent, and the turbulence then persists, because \(\mathrm{Re}_D\) is far above the lower critical value below which eddies die out.
