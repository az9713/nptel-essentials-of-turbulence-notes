Modeling approaches and criteria for appraising models

The channel of chapter 9 (half-width \(d=2\ \mathrm{cm}\), kinematic viscosity \(\nu=1.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), friction Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}_\tau=1000.5\)) must be designed, and two questions are asked of it. The first is what pressure gradient drives a bulk velocity of \(1.00\ \mathrm{m/s}\). The mean momentum balance of chapter 9 fixes the gradient as \(\tau_w/d\), which is \(125\ \mathrm{Pa/m}\) for the direct simulation used there, but \(\tau_w\) depends on how the fluctuations carry momentum, so the answer needs a model of the Reynolds stress. The second question is how often the velocity at one point exceeds twice its mean. That depends on the probability density of the velocity, which the mean velocity does not contain. A direct simulation of this flow answers both questions and needs \(2304\times512\times2048=2.4\times10^{9}\) grid points. A model of the mean flow answers only the first and needs one ordinary differential equation. This chapter sets out the three families of method between those extremes, the five criteria on which the lecture appraises them, and the two extreme cases, a mixing-length model and a direct simulation, on which it illustrates each criterion.

Grounded in three recordings of the NPTEL course Essentials of Turbulence (Prof. Abhilash J. Chandy, IIT Bombay): Lecture 43, Lecture 44 and Lecture 45. Lecture material: the difficulties of the turbulence problem; the division of methods into direct numerical simulation (DNS), large-eddy simulation (LES) and Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) calculations; the growth of DNS cost with the Reynolds number; the five criteria (level of description, completeness, cost and ease of use, range of applicability, accuracy); the mixing-length model; the table of flows by statistical dimensionality; the growth of computer speed; the sources of discrepancy between calculation and experiment. The objects of the chapter and the running case

A realization is one repetition of the experiment, as in chapters 7 and 8. The velocity \(\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)\) is a random field: each realization gives a different function. Its mean is \(\langle\mathbf U\rangle\), the fluctuation is \(\mathbf u=\mathbf U-\langle\mathbf U\rangle\), and the Reynolds stresses are \(\langle u_iu_j\rangle\) (chapter 9). The turbulent kinetic energy is \(k=\tfrac12\langle u_iu_i\rangle\). The dissipation rate \(\varepsilon=2\nu\langle s_{ij}s_{ij}\rangle\), with \(s_{ij}\) the strain rate of the fluctuation, is itself an average over the ensemble.

The lecture separates two words that it says are often confused. A simulation solves equations for a velocity field that represents one realization. A model solves equations for mean quantities and approximates what the averaging removes. This chapter keeps that usage. It is a convention of the lecture, not a sharp boundary: Pope (2004, pp. 2–3) describes LES as a calculation of a resolved velocity field, and p. 6 records the opinion of one group that LES is a physical model because the effect of the unresolved motions is modeled explicitly. LES computes a resolved field for one realization from equations that contain a model for the unresolved motions, so it belongs to both words; the section on LES below reports why that resolved field is not the filtered velocity of the lecture's description.

The running case is the channel of chapter 9: flow between two parallel plane walls, statistically stationary, with statistics that depend only on the wall distance \(y\) (the slide writes \(x\) and \(y\) for the streamwise and wall-normal coordinates; chapter 9 writes \(x_1\) and \(x_2\), with \(y\) measured from the lower wall). The DNS of Lee and Moser (2015) at \(\mathrm{Re}_\tau=u_\tau d/\nu=1000.512\) supplies the reference values. With the round values \(d=2\ \mathrm{cm}\), \(\nu=1.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) and \(\rho=1000\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\), which chapter 9 chose as inputs, the friction velocity is \(u_\tau=0.0500\ \mathrm{m/s}\), the wall shear stress is \(\tau_w=\rho u_\tau^2=2.50\ \mathrm{Pa}\), the pressure gradient is \(\tau_w/d=125\ \mathrm{Pa/m}\), the bulk velocity is \(1.000\ \mathrm{m/s}\) (the header value \(U_b/u_\tau=19.99\)), and the viscous length \(\nu/u_\tau\) is \(20\ \mu\mathrm m\). The wall-unit symbols \(y^+=yu_\tau/\nu\) and \(U^+=\langle U\rangle/u_\tau\) are those of chapter 9.

The turbulence problem and three ways to compute around it L43 2:21

The slide states the aim as a tractable quantitative theory or model that calculates quantities of interest and practical relevance, and states the obstacle as the “turbulence problem”: no prospect of a simple analytic theory, so digital computers are used. For the channel, the quantities of interest in the opening paragraph are the pressure gradient and a velocity probability. The lecture lists four reasons for the difficulty (slide, L43 6:31–10:40), and each can be attached to a number or to an earlier chapter.

  1. The velocity field \(\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)\) is three-dimensional, time-dependent and random. One computed field is one realization; a statistic requires an average over realizations or a time average whose validity chapter 8 states.
  2. The largest motions are almost as large as the width of the flow and are set by the boundary geometry, so they are not universal. In the channel the large scale is the half-width \(d=2\ \mathrm{cm}\). A relation calibrated on one geometry has no reason to hold on another (this is the point of the completeness and applicability criteria below).
  3. The range of length scales is wide, and each length scale has its own time scale, so the range of time scales is wide too. The ratio of the half-width to the viscous length of the channel is \(\mathrm{Re}_\tau=1000.5\). The ratios of the Kolmogorov scales to the large-eddy scales are given by (5.18) of chapter 5 and (10.15) of chapter 10.
  4. The nonlinear convection term and the pressure gradient term. The viscous term is a second derivative and linear. The pressure is fixed by the constraint of zero divergence, with the convective term as the source (chapter 6, section “Pressure is fixed by the constraint of zero divergence”). The convective term couples Fourier modes in pairs (chapter 12, section “The nonlinear coefficient is a sum over pairs of modes”).

The lecture says that the nonlinear term makes an analytical solution “impossible” (L43 4:50–5:30). The precise statement that the book has is the count of chapter 9, section “The closure problem”: four equations for ten unknown mean fields. The lecture also calls the effect of the nonlinear term “enhanced diffusion” and adds that technically it is not diffusion, because the mechanism differs from molecular diffusion (L43 9:30–10:00); chapter 4 treats the turbulent diffusivity as a model for that effect.

The slide then contrasts what is solved for. In a simulation, equations are solved for a time-dependent velocity field that represents the velocity field for one realization of the flow. In a turbulence model, equations are solved for mean quantities, for example \(\langle\mathbf U\rangle\), \(\langle u_iu_j\rangle\) and \(\varepsilon\). The lecture adds that \(\varepsilon\) is a single quantity to which no mean applies (L43 13:55–14:00). That is not accurate: \(\varepsilon=2\nu\langle s_{ij}s_{ij}\rangle\) is already an ensemble average, which is why the slide lists it among the mean quantities. The lecture's ordering of cost and accuracy. The lecture states twice (L43 23:10–24:40; L44 1:18–1:40) that both cost and accuracy increase from RANS to LES to DNS, and attributes the accuracy to the grid: the RANS grid is “extremely coarse”, the LES grid resolves the large eddies, and the DNS grid resolves all scales. The ordering of cost is supported (below). The ordering of accuracy needs two qualifications. First, the accuracy gain does not come from the grid alone. A RANS calculation on a finer grid still solves for mean quantities with a model for all turbulent motions; its error from the model does not shrink with the grid. What increases from RANS to DNS is the fraction of the turbulent motion that is computed rather than modeled. Pope (2004, p. 8) defines a measure of that fraction, the ratio of the residual to the total turbulent kinetic energy:

\[ M\equiv\frac{k_r}{k_{\mathrm{res}}+k_r}, \tag{16.1} \]

where \(k_r\) is the kinetic energy of the residual (modeled) motions and \(k_{\mathrm{res}}\) that of the resolved fluctuations (the source writes \(K\) and \(k_r\)). The value \(M=0\) corresponds to DNS and \(M=1\) to RANS; smaller values resolve more of the turbulent motion. Pope uses \(M\) to define an adaptive LES in which the grid is refined where \(M\) exceeds a tolerance (for example \(M=0.2\) resolves 80 % of the kinetic energy). With (16.1) the three approaches lie on one axis, and the lecture's ordering becomes a statement about that axis. Second, the ordering does not hold for every flow. Pope (2004, pp. 3–4) states the case for LES as strong when the rate-controlling processes lie in the resolved large scales, as in free shear flows at high Reynolds number, and weaker when they lie below the resolved scales, as in turbulent combustion, or in the viscous near-wall region, where the near-wall motions cannot be resolved in high-Reynolds-number LES and must be modeled. The same pages state that RANS models will be prevalent for some time. The lecture's ordering is therefore a trend along \(M\) for a fixed flow and a fixed numerical method. It is not a ranking that applies to every pair of calculations.

A third qualification comes from the slide on accuracy (L45, below): DNS removes the error of the model, but the numerical error, the measurement error and the error in the boundary conditions remain. “Resolving the flow exactly” (L43 1:45) means that no model is used for the equations; it does not mean that the computed field equals the field of the experiment.

How the cost of a direct simulation grows with the Reynolds number L43 16:07

The slide states that the cost of DNS increases as \(\mathrm{Re}^3\), so that the approach is restricted to low-to-moderate Reynolds number. The lecture illustrates it with a change from \(\mathrm{Re}=100\) to \(1000\), which multiplies the cost by \((1000/100)^3=10^3\) (L43 16:15–16:40). The slide writes bare \(\mathrm{Re}\). From chapter 10 on this book writes \(\mathrm{Re}_0=u_0\ell_0/\nu\) for the Reynolds number of the energy-containing eddies, and the scaling is a statement about that number.

Chapter 5, section “A resolution budget for a direct simulation”, derives the scaling and states its assumptions. The grid spacing must be proportional to the Kolmogorov length \(\eta\), so the number of points per direction is \(N\propto\ell_0/\eta\propto\mathrm{Re}_0^{3/4}\); the time step of an explicit method at a fixed Courant number is proportional to the grid spacing, so the number of steps for one large-eddy turnover time is proportional to \(N\); the work is the product,

\[ \text{work}\propto N^{3}\times N=N^{4}\propto\mathrm{Re}_0^{3}. \tag{16.2} \]

The exponent 3 is a scaling, not a prediction of a number of operations: the constant depends on the numerical method. The exponent \(9/4\) of the number of points can be compared with the three DNS of the channel whose data files Lee and Moser (2015) provide. Their headers list the grids \(1536\times384\times1024\) at \(\mathrm{Re}_\tau=543.5\), \(2304\times512\times2048\) at \(1000.5\) and \(10240\times1536\times7680\) at \(5185.9\), that is \(6.04\times10^{8}\), \(2.42\times10^{9}\) and \(1.21\times10^{11}\) points. The local exponents \(\ln(\text{points ratio})/\ln(\mathrm{Re}_\tau\text{ ratio})\) are \(2.27\) and \(2.38\), against \(9/4=2.25\). This is a consistency check and not a verification of (16.2). The Reynolds number is \(\mathrm{Re}_\tau\), not \(\mathrm{Re}_0\); the grids carry choices of the authors (domain \(8\pi d\times3\pi d\), numerical method); and the exponent of the number of steps is not tested at all, because the headers do not give the step count.

Pope (2004, p. 13) gives the corresponding statement for LES: the cost varies approximately as \((\ell_0/\Delta)^4\), where \(\ell_0\) is the integral length scale of the energy-containing motions (Pope writes \(L\) for it, p. 11) and \(\Delta\) the turbulence-resolution length, and with \(\Delta\approx\eta\) the cost is comparable with that of DNS. So LES is cheaper than DNS only to the extent that \(\Delta\) is chosen larger than \(\eta\). The phrase “low-to-moderate Reynolds number” has no fixed boundary: the largest case above has \(1.2\times10^{11}\) points and a Reynolds number five times that of the middle case. The section on computer speed below connects the boundary to hardware.

The memory of these calculations gives the same scale of difficulty in bytes. One snapshot of the three velocity components in double precision (8 bytes each) occupies \(3\times8\times\) (number of points) bytes: 14 GB, 58 GB and 2899 GB (about 2.9 TB) for the three grids above (decimal gigabytes; program output below). A calculation holds several arrays and usually many snapshots, so these figures are lower bounds on the memory and on the storage of results. The lecture's questions about resources (do you have the computing resources, and are they enough for the memory-intensive codes; L45 13:00–13:50, L44 26:25–26:45) begin with numbers of this order.

Five criteria and two extreme cases L43 24:46

The slide lists the principal criteria that can be used to assess different models: (1) level of description, (2) completeness, (3) cost and ease of use, (4) range of applicability, (5) accuracy. It adds that two extreme approaches are considered, the mixing-length model and DNS, and that DNS consists of solving the Navier–Stokes equations to determine \(\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)\) for one realization of the flow. Pope (2004, p. 15) lists the same five criteria, in the same order, and attributes them to Pope (2000). The lecture's list is therefore Pope's list; the page checked is in Pope (2004), not in the book. Pope (2004) adds a sixth condition for LES alone, convergence to DNS as \(\Delta/\eta\to0\); the lecture does not mention it, and this chapter does not add it to the list.

The two extremes are chosen because they sit at opposite ends on every criterion: the mixing-length model is an algebraic RANS closure (one expression for the turbulent viscosity, no transport equation), which the lecture calls one of the first, and DNS involves no closure. The lecture states (L43 26:06–26:59) that it will mostly look at the two extremes “to get an idea” of the criteria; the lecture returns to the question “how complete are LES and RANS?” (L43 25:40) only through the mixing-length case. Pope (2004, pp. 7–8) answers it for LES, and the completeness section below reports the answer.

The mixing-length model: one algebraic relation closes the mean equations L44 2:36

The model is applied to statistically stationary boundary-layer flows. The slide gives the boundary-layer form of the mean equations for \(\langle U(x,y)\rangle\) and \(\langle V(x,y)\rangle\), with the Reynolds shear stress and the turbulent viscosity obtained from two model equations:

\[ \langle uv\rangle=-\nu_T\,\frac{\partial\langle U\rangle}{\partial y}, \tag{16.3} \] \[ \nu_T=\ell_m^{2}\,\Bigl|\frac{\partial\langle U\rangle}{\partial y}\Bigr|, \tag{16.4} \]

where the mixing length \(\ell_m(x,y)\) is “specified as a function of position”. The slide writes \(l_m\); this book writes \(\ell_m\) because \(L_m\) denotes the laminar depth in chapter 5. The slide's \(\langle uv\rangle\) is the \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle\) of chapter 9. Both sides of (16.4) have units \(\mathrm{m^2/s}\), and \(\langle uv\rangle\) in (16.3) has units \(\mathrm{m^2/s^2}\). Substituting (16.4) in (16.3),

\[ \langle uv\rangle=-\,\ell_m^{2}\,\Bigl|\frac{\partial\langle U\rangle}{\partial y}\Bigr|\,\frac{\partial\langle U\rangle}{\partial y}. \tag{16.5} \]

The stress has the sign opposite to the mean gradient, so momentum is transported down the gradient, and \(\nu_T\ge0\) follows from (16.4); in chapter 9 positivity of \(\nu_T\) is a postulate of the hypothesis. Equation (16.3) is the one shear component of the turbulent-viscosity hypothesis (9.27): in simple shear \(a_{12}=\langle u_1u_2\rangle=-\nu_T\,d\langle U_1\rangle/dy\). The model specifies this one component and nothing else. It gives no value for the normal stresses and no equation for \(k\) or \(\varepsilon\). Its role in the sequence of chapters is to reduce the unknown stress of the closure problem (chapter 9, section “The closure problem”) to an unknown length. The lecture says that the model equations close the Reynolds equations (L44 7:34–8:00); what is left unclosed is \(\ell_m\), and the completeness criterion below concerns exactly that.

The lecture makes three remarks on the model. (i) The relation of a Reynolds stress to the mean gradient with a viscosity as the coefficient is the Boussinesq hypothesis, discussed in chapter 9, section “The turbulent-viscosity hypothesis”, which attributes it to Boussinesq (1877) with the support of Schmitt (2007). (ii) The turbulent viscosity violates the feature that turbulence is a property of the flow and not of the fluid, because \(\nu_T\) in (16.4) depends on the flow through \(\ell_m\) and the mean gradient, unlike \(\nu\) (L44 4:12–4:30). The lecture adds that Boussinesq warned of this when introducing the hypothesis (L44 4:30–4:42); this chapter has not checked that statement. (iii) The lecture explains the origin of the model by analogy with the kinetic theory of gases, a molecule travelling a mean free path before it passes energy to another molecule, so that a fluid particle travels the mixing length before it passes momentum to a neighbour (L44 6:01–7:15), and it says that this is how energy is cascaded from the large to the small scales. The analogy concerns the transport of mean momentum across the mean shear, which is what (16.3) states. The energy cascade of chapter 10 is a transfer of energy between scales, and the model contains no energy equation and no scales, so the two should not be identified.

The model in the channel (teaching construction). The running case reduces the slide's boundary-layer form exactly, with no boundary-layer approximation: the statistics depend on \(y\) only, and chapter 9 derives the total stress balance (9.13), \(\nu\,dU/dy-\langle u_1u_2\rangle=u_\tau^2(1-y/d)\), which contains no model. Insert (16.5) in the lower half of the channel, where \(d\langle U\rangle/dy\ge0\), and divide by \(u_\tau^2\). With \(g\equiv dU^+/dy^+\), \(\ell_m^+\equiv\ell_mu_\tau/\nu\) and \(y/d=y^+/\mathrm{Re}_\tau\),

\[ g+\bigl(\ell_m^+\bigr)^2g^{2}=1-\frac{y^+}{\mathrm{Re}_\tau}. \tag{16.6} \]

The positive root, the only one with \(g\ge0\), is

\[ g=\frac{-1+\sqrt{1+4\bigl(\ell_m^+\bigr)^2\bigl(1-y^+/\mathrm{Re}_\tau\bigr)}}{2\bigl(\ell_m^+\bigr)^2},\qquad g\to1-\frac{y^+}{\mathrm{Re}_\tau}\ \text{ as }\ \ell_m^+\to0. \tag{16.7} \]

The limit is the profile for which the viscous stress alone carries the total stress. Once \(\ell_m^+(y^+)\) is chosen, integrating (16.7) from \(U^+=0\) at \(y^+=0\) gives the whole mean profile and the bulk velocity. The completeness section uses this to show what the choice of \(\ell_m\) does.

Level of description L44 8:13

The level of description is the set of quantities that a method represents directly. In DNS the flow is described by \(\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)\), from which all other information follows: flow visualizations can examine turbulent structures, and multi-point, multi-time statistics can be extracted (slide; L44 8:13–9:10). Nothing more needs to be specified. In the mixing-length model the description is at the mean-flow level: apart from the specified mixing length, the only quantities represented directly are \(\langle U\rangle\) and \(\langle V\rangle\) (for the two-dimensional boundary layer of the slide). No information is provided about the probability density of the velocity, two-point correlations or turbulent structures. Equations (16.3)–(16.5) show why: they contain only mean quantities and a length.

The statistics of the earlier chapters can be placed on a ladder, ordered by what a method must supply. The mean \(\langle\mathbf U\rangle\) and the Reynolds stresses \(\langle u_iu_j\rangle\) (chapter 9) are moments of the one-point probability density \(f(V)\) of chapter 7. That density is a marginal of the joint density at two points, which determines the two-point correlation \(R_{ij}(\mathbf r)\) of chapter 8. Statistics at several times and several points, which the slide lists for DNS, lie above both. A method that supplies a statistic on a higher rung supplies the lower ones, since the lower are averages of the higher; the converse is false. Equation (16.5) uses the mean and one component of the second moments, and nothing above them.

The slide adds that a limited description by a mean-flow closure is adequate in many applications, but that higher levels of description exist that can provide better characterization. The lecture's argument is that for design the mean may be all that is needed (L44 10:15–10:45). For the running case, the pressure gradient needed for a given bulk velocity is a mean-flow quantity, and (16.6) can supply it once \(\ell_m^+\) is fixed. The probability that the velocity at one point exceeds twice its mean is not: it needs the density \(f(V)\) of chapter 7, which the mean-flow description does not contain, and (16.5) cannot supply it, since it fixes only the mean of the product \(uv\). A higher level of description costs more (the cost sections below). Level of description is therefore the first criterion to check against the question asked, before the others.

The lecture also grades the mixing-length model: it is an algebraic model, one of the first turbulence models, “probably the least accurate by all standards”, with two-equation models and Reynolds-stress models expected to be more accurate; it has been used for decades in aerodynamic design, and “hardly used” today (L44 10:56–11:50). Those are the speaker's judgments. This chapter has not checked them, and it does not rely on them.

The lecture names the \(k\)–\(\varepsilon\) and \(k\)–\(\omega\) models. A two-equation model adds to the mean-flow equations two transport equations; in the \(k\)–\(\varepsilon\) model they are equations for the turbulent kinetic energy and the dissipation rate (the usual description of the model); their solutions \(k_m\) and \(\varepsilon_m\) are defined by those model equations, not by the definitions of \(k\) and \(\varepsilon\) in chapter 9 (Pope 2004, p. 9). The chapter titled “Turbulent viscosity models” gives the equations; this chapter makes no statement about the model's coefficients.

Completeness L44 11:57

The lecture's definition. The slide reads: a model is deemed complete if its constituent equations are free from flow-dependent specifications. In theory one flow is distinguished from another solely by the specification of material properties (like \(\rho\) and \(\nu\)) and of initial and boundary conditions. DNS is complete, whereas the mixing-length model is incomplete, because the mixing length \(\ell_m(x,y)\) has to be specified and the appropriate specification is flow dependent. Incomplete models can be useful for flows within a narrow class (the slide's example is the attached boundary layer on airfoils) for which there is a body of semi-empirical knowledge on the appropriate flow-dependent specifications. In general completeness is clearly desirable.

The statement in the source. Pope (2004, p. 7) gives the same definition, citing Pope (2000): a model for turbulent flows is deemed complete if its constituent equations are free from flow-dependent specifications, and one flow is distinguished from another solely by the specification of material properties and of initial and boundary conditions. His example is the same pair: the \(k\)–\(\varepsilon\) model is complete and the mixing-length model is incomplete, because the mixing length must be specified, as a function of position and time. He adds a consequence: if two competent practitioners make two independent mixing-length calculations of the same complex flow, the results are bound to differ, because different choices are made for the mixing length.

Two distinctions keep the criterion from being misread. First, the definition separates a flow-dependent specification inside the model equations from the specification of the flow itself. The initial and boundary conditions of the channel (the geometry, the pressure gradient or the bulk velocity) belong to the flow, and every method needs them. DNS is complete because no term in its equations needs a choice that depends on the flow. The lecture's later remark (L45 21:15–26:15, below) that the inlet conditions of a calculation may have to be taken from experiments concerns the accuracy of the boundary conditions. It does not make DNS incomplete. Second, the lecture says on L45 (3:19–3:37) that the mixing-length model is incomplete because certain constants in it are obtained from empirical relations for a particular class of flows. Under the definition above, a constant that is fixed once for all flows is not a flow-dependent specification, so the origin of a constant, empirical or not, does not decide completeness. What decides it is whether a choice has to be made for each flow, and Pope (2004, p. 7) calls the \(k\)–\(\varepsilon\) model complete. What makes the mixing-length model incomplete is a function \(\ell_m(x,y)\) that has to be re-specified for each flow. A model that is complete but calibrated on a limited set of flows is a case for the criteria of applicability and accuracy.

LES and RANS. The lecture poses the question of how complete LES and RANS are (L43 25:40) and does not answer it for LES. Pope (2004, pp. 7–8) does: as generally practised, LES is incomplete. The grid spacing \(h(\mathbf x)\) is generated first, and the turbulence-resolution length \(\Delta(\mathbf x)\) is then specified proportional to it, “in a flow-dependent, subjective manner”, although \(\Delta\) is a significant parameter of the equations. He proposes an adaptive LES in which a tolerance on \(M\) from (16.1) is specified and the grid is refined where \(M\) exceeds it; with the tolerance as part of the model specification, the subjectivity is removed. For RANS the answer depends on the closure: the mixing-length model is incomplete and \(k\)–\(\varepsilon\) is complete (Pope 2004, p. 7). This chapter has checked those two cases only.

Range of applicability L45 17:25

The slide gives three statements. Not all models are applicable to all flows: many models based on velocity spectra or two-point correlations are applicable to homogeneous turbulence, and particular mixing-length models make assumptions about the flow geometry in the specification of the mixing length, so that their applicability is confined to flows of that geometry. Computational requirements place other limitations: for DNS the requirements rise so sharply with \(\mathrm{Re}\) that it is applicable only to low-to-moderate \(\mathrm{Re}\). And applicability is different from accuracy: applicability means that the model equations are well posed and can be solved, irrespective of how accurate they are.

The first example, models of the spectrum or of two-point correlations that hold for homogeneous turbulence, is the lecture's; this chapter has not checked it against a source. The lecture explains that the mixing-length model has been applied to a wide range of flows (pipe flows, jets, aerodynamic flows) although its constants come from empirical data on one kind of flow, boundary layers, and that being applied to a flow is not the same as being applicable in the sense of the slide (L45 18:35–19:40). Accuracy L45 20:52

The slide defines accuracy as a desirable attribute of any model that can be determined by comparing model calculations with experimental measurements. It continues that the boundary conditions in the calculations need not correspond exactly to those of the measured flow (a flow that is approximately two-dimensional may be assumed exactly so in the calculation), and that boundary conditions on some properties are not necessarily known and have to be estimated or taken from experimental data. Then it lists the sources of the discrepancy between measured and calculated flow properties: (1) inaccuracies of the model, (2) numerical error, for example the truncation error \((\Delta x)^n\), (3) measurement error and (4) discrepancies in the boundary conditions. It notes that while the truncation error scales as a positive power of \(\Delta x\), the computational cost increases with \(1/\Delta x\), possibly as a positive power. The lecture discusses only the boundary conditions in this recording and defers the rest (L45 26:20).

The jet example (L45 21:15–26:18). The lecture describes a turbulent jet measured downstream of a long pipe and a nozzle, so that the flow is fully developed and turbulent when it leaves the nozzle. A calculation of the jet may not afford the long pipe, and now usually includes the nozzle because the literature has shown that it matters for accuracy; but a short nozzle gives the fluid no time to become turbulent. The calculation then takes a fully developed turbulent profile from the experiment and attaches it at the inlet. The experiment supplies a mean profile, which is smooth, and the calculation needs fluctuations on top of it. The lecture asks how many orders of statistics to supply: second order only, or third and fourth. Whatever is chosen is an approximation, so the calculation starts from conditions that differ from those of the experiment, and a match is not to be expected. This is the fourth source in the slide.

The example also shows how the fourth source depends on the level of description. A mean-flow calculation needs the mean profile and the few turbulence quantities that its model carries. A DNS or an LES needs a velocity field at every instant at the inlet, so the inlet specification must contain the fluctuations; this is the inference of this chapter, and the lecture draws it only through the second, third and fourth statistics.

DNS and the four sources. The lecture calls DNS the most accurate approach, since it determines \(\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)\) for one realization without a model. In the list of the slide, DNS removes only the first source. The truncation error of the second source, the measurement error and the boundary-condition discrepancy remain. The accurate statement is that DNS has no error from a model for the turbulence, and that its remaining errors can be reduced at a computational price, which brings the second statement of the slide to a quantitative form. Let the truncation error scale as \((\Delta x)^n\) and the cost of a three-dimensional time-dependent calculation as \((\Delta x)^{-4}\), the exponent of (16.2) and of Pope (2004, pp. 5–6, 15), which states that halving the grid spacing increases the cost by about \(2^4=16\) with the numerical methods usually employed. Then

\[ e_{\mathrm{tr}}\propto(\Delta x)^{n},\qquad\text{cost}\propto(\Delta x)^{-4}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{cost}\propto e_{\mathrm{tr}}^{-4/n}. \tag{16.9} \]

To lower the truncation error by a factor 10, the cost grows by a factor \(10^{4/n}\): 100 for \(n=2\) and 10 for \(n=4\) (program output below). The relation assumes that the error is in the asymptotic range, that the leading truncation term dominates and that the time step is refined with the grid. It also says nothing about sources 1, 3 and 4: they set a floor under the discrepancy that no refinement removes. A better numerical method lowers the cost of a given error, which is the content of the lecture's remark that the cost also depends on the numerical method.

Where large-eddy simulation sits on the five criteria

The lecture applies the criteria to the two extremes and defers LES to later lectures; the chapters titled “Large-eddy simulation: conceptual steps and filtering” and “Filtered equations, the Smagorinsky model, and LES in wavenumber space” treat it. Pope (2004) applies the same five criteria to LES, and the following statements are checked on its pages. This section is a research extension to the lecture.

Level of description. The lecture describes LES as the solution for the filtered velocity of one realization (L43 21:00–21:30). Pope (2004, p. 9) calls the view that the resolved field \(W(\mathbf x,t)\) is the spatially filtered value of \(\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)\) “not sustainable”. The resolved field is defined as the solution of the LES equations. A perfect LES cannot make it equal to the filtered velocity, because \(\mathbf U\) is a random field whose future is not determined by its present state: the two can be made equal at \(t=0\), but for \(t\gt0\) the filtered field has a statistical distribution and no single value of \(W\) equals it. The relationship is therefore statistical (pp. 9–10). A statistic \(Q\) of the velocity is estimated by \(Q_m=Q_w+Q_r\), where \(Q_w\) is determined by the resolved field alone and \(Q_r\) is a model for the residual contribution (p. 10). The level of description of LES is thus a resolved field together with a model that estimates the statistics of the unresolved motion.

Accuracy. The estimate depends on the turbulence-resolution length \(\Delta\), a parameter that does not enter the flow itself: \(Q_m=Q_m(\Delta)\) (p. 11). Pope proposes, as a hypothesis in need of testing, that at a very high Reynolds number \(Q_m(\Delta)\) approaches an intermediate asymptote \(Q_{mI}\) for \(\Delta\) in the inertial subrange, between the demarcation lengths \(\ell_{DI}\) and \(\ell_{EI}\) of chapter 10 (pp. 11–12); at moderate Reynolds numbers the asymptote may not be discernible (p. 13). For a statistic that depends only on the energy-containing range, \(Q_m\) converges to \(Q\) as \(\Delta/\ell_{EI}\) decreases (p. 13). Accuracy is then a property of the function \(Q_m(\Delta)\), not of one calculation: two models compared at a single \(\Delta\) can be ranked in the wrong order (p. 15).

The other three criteria. LES as generally practised is incomplete, because \(\Delta\) is specified subjectively (pp. 7–8). Its cost varies approximately as \((\ell_0/\Delta)^4\) and equals about that of DNS for \(\Delta\approx\eta\) (p. 13). The case for its range is strong for free shear flows at high Reynolds number and weaker where the rate-controlling processes lie below the resolved scales (pp. 3–4). For LES alone Pope adds the requirement that the model converge to DNS as \(\Delta/\eta\to0\) (p. 15).

Table 16.1 therefore needs a qualification for LES. The lecture's “simulation” of one realization is a field that solves equations containing a model, and the numbers that LES returns for a statistic are estimates that depend on \(\Delta\).

# viscous stress and the modelled Reynolds stress against the exact total

def dudy(yp, lp):
    """Positive root of g + lp^2 g^2 = 1 - y+/Re_tau (lp = 0 gives g = s)."""
    s = 1.0 - yp/RE_TAU
    lp2 = lp**2
    return np.where(lp2 > 0, (-1 + np.sqrt(1 + 4*lp2*s))/(2*np.maximum(lp2, 1e-300)), s)

What the criteria establish and what they do not

The five criteria are a checklist for what to ask of a method. They do not rank methods, and the lecture applies them to two extremes, so they do not decide between LES and a two-equation RANS model. Several of the statements in this chapter have stated limits.

  • The ordering of cost and accuracy from RANS to LES to DNS is a trend along the resolution measure (16.1) for a fixed flow and a fixed numerical method. Pope (2004, pp. 3–4, 13) describes flows in which LES offers little advantage (rate-controlling processes below the resolved scales, near-wall regions) and a resolution \(\Delta\approx\eta\) at which LES costs about as much as DNS.
  • The scaling (16.2) rests on the grid being proportional to \(\eta\), on a Courant-limited time step and on \(\ell_0/\eta\propto\mathrm{Re}_0^{3/4}\) (chapter 5). The headers of the three DNS data files are consistent with the exponent \(9/4\) for the grid in terms of \(\mathrm{Re}_\tau\), within 6 %; they say nothing about the step count.
  • Table 16.2 shows that the answer of a model that lacks a specification depends on the specification. It is not a test of the accuracy of the mixing-length model, because all three specifications lack the damping of \(\ell_m\) near the wall, and it uses a flow, the channel, from which the usual specifications are taken.
  • The statement that a mean-flow description is adequate in many applications, and the grading of the mixing-length model as least accurate and now rarely used, are the speaker's judgments.
  • The statistical-dimensionality table (Table 16.3) is a statement about the difficulty of mean-flow calculations; it does not describe the cost of a DNS.
  • The growth estimate (16.8) holds only if the trend of computer speed continues and if (16.2) holds with a fixed constant. The slide itself says there is no basis for extrapolation.
  • The lecture's list and the definition of completeness are checked against Pope (2004), which cites Pope (2000). The book itself was not available, and no page of it is cited. The criteria may be stated in it with more detail or different examples than in the lecture or in Pope (2004).
  • The criteria are not independent: completeness and range of applicability describe the same limitation for an incomplete model, and cost and accuracy are linked by (16.9).

Exercises

Exercise 1 (derive and compute). In the channel at \(y^+\gg1\) and \(y/d\ll1\), (16.6) is dominated by the Reynolds stress. Show that \(\ell_m=\kappa y\) then gives \(dU^+/dy^+=1/(\kappa y^+)\), and compute \(\nu_T/\nu\) and \(d\langle U\rangle/dy\) at \(y=1\ \mathrm{mm}\) in the running case, taking \(\kappa=0.41\). Check how good the approximation is at that height by comparing it with the root (16.7).

Solution

If the viscous term \(g\) and the correction \(y^+/\mathrm{Re}_\tau\) are negligible, (16.6) reads \((\ell_m^+g)^2=1\), so \(g=1/\ell_m^+=1/(\kappa y^+)\) for \(\ell_m=\kappa y\). In dimensional form \(d\langle U\rangle/dy=u_\tau/(\kappa y)\), which integrates to a logarithmic profile. At \(y=1\ \mathrm{mm}\): \(y^+=yu_\tau/\nu=10^{-3}\times0.0500/10^{-6}=50\), \(d\langle U\rangle/dy=0.0500/(0.41\times10^{-3})=122\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), and \(\nu_T=\ell_m^2|d\langle U\rangle/dy|=\kappa u_\tau y=0.41\times0.0500\times10^{-3}=2.05\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), so \(\nu_T/\nu=\kappa y^+=20.5\). The exact root of (16.7) at \(y^+=50\) is \(g=0.0464\), against \(1/(\kappa y^+)=0.0488\), a ratio of \(0.951\). Two corrections of the same size produce it. Dropping the viscous term \(g\) from (16.6) but keeping the fall of the total stress gives \(g=\sqrt{1-y^+/\mathrm{Re}_\tau}\,/(\kappa y^+)=0.0475\), a ratio of \(0.975\); the viscous term lowers it by a further factor \(0.975\). For \(\ell_m^+\gg1\) with \(a=\ell_m^+=\kappa y^+\) and \(s=1-y^+/\mathrm{Re}_\tau\), \(\sqrt{1+4a^2s}=2a\sqrt s\,\bigl(1+1/(8a^2s)+\dots\bigr)\) in (16.7) gives \(g\approx\sqrt s/a-1/(2a^2)\), and \(\sqrt s\approx1-y^+/(2\mathrm{Re}_\tau)\); hence the root expands as \(g\approx\dfrac{1}{\kappa y^+}\Bigl(1-\dfrac{y^+}{2\mathrm{Re}_\tau}-\dfrac{1}{2\kappa y^+}\Bigr)\), so the two first-order corrections are \(y^+/(2\mathrm{Re}_\tau)=0.025\) and \(1/(2\kappa y^+)=0.024\). They are equal where \(\kappa(y^+)^2=\mathrm{Re}_\tau\), at \(y^+=49\); nearer the wall the viscous term is the larger correction, farther out the fall of the total stress is. The approximation \(1/(\kappa y^+)\) is good to 5 % at this height, and the value \(\kappa=0.41\) is an assumed input.

Exercise 2 (judge). A colleague argues that the mixing-length model with \(\ell_m=\kappa y\) is complete, because its only free quantity is the single number \(\kappa\), which is the same for all flows. Decide whether this is correct, and say what happens when the model is applied to a plane jet.

Solution

The argument is not correct. Under the definition of the slide and of Pope (2004, p. 7), a model is complete if its equations are free from flow-dependent specifications. A constant that is the same for all flows would not violate that. But \(\ell_m=\kappa y\) is a function of position that contains \(y\), the distance from a wall. It states what the length is for flows with a wall, which is a specification that depends on the flow. In a plane jet there is no wall, \(y\) from a wall has no meaning, and a different function has to be chosen, for example a fraction of the local jet width, which the lecture mentions (L44 7:15–7:35). The new function is again flow dependent. Two competent practitioners can choose different fractions, and Table 16.2 shows the consequence: specifications A and C give \(U_b^+=12.52\) and \(5.66\) for the same equations and the same exact stress balance, a ratio of 2.2. The colleague is right that a single number would not make a model incomplete; the model is incomplete because of the function.

Exercise 3 (check an assumption). Specification A has no wall damping. Using the DNS values \(dU^+/dy^+=0.8786\) and \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle^+=-0.1164\) at \(y^+=5.029\) from chapter 9, compare the Reynolds shear stress that (16.5) gives with the DNS slope to the DNS stress, and state what the result implies for the accuracy of Table 16.2.

Solution

With \(\ell_m^+=\kappa y^+=0.41\times5.029=2.062\), (16.5) in wall units gives \(|\langle uv\rangle|^+=(\ell_m^+)^2g^2=4.251\times0.8786^2=3.28\). The DNS stress is \(0.1164\), which satisfies the exact balance \(0.8786+0.1164=0.995=1-y^+/\mathrm{Re}_\tau\). The model stress is 28 times the DNS stress at this height. A model with this stress cannot have the DNS slope: (16.7) gives \(g=0.380\), a ratio of 0.43 to the DNS slope. The near-wall region, where the molecular term should carry most of the stress, is where the undamped \(\ell_m\) gives an excessive Reynolds stress. Table 16.2 therefore says how three specifications fail to reproduce the bulk velocity of a channel; the \(-37\ \%\) of specification A includes this near-wall error, and the table must not be read as the accuracy of the mixing-length model with a damped \(\ell_m\).

Exercise 4 (design). For the running case, three clients ask: (a) the pressure gradient for a bulk velocity of \(1.00\ \mathrm{m/s}\) to within \(\pm10\ \%\); (b) the probability density of \(u\) at \(y^+=15\); (c) the two-point correlation of the streamwise fluctuation at two points \(20\ \mathrm{mm}\) apart. Using the five criteria, choose between a mixing-length calculation and a DNS for each, and state what would change your choice.

Solution

(b) and (c) need a level of description above the mean: the probability density and the two-point correlation are not represented in the mixing-length model (slide, L44 8:13–9:10). They need \(\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)\), so DNS (or a resolved LES) is the choice, at a cost that for \(\mathrm{Re}_\tau=1000.5\) is \(2.4\times10^{9}\) grid points in the reference simulation. For (a), the mean-flow description is sufficient. The mixing-length model costs one ordinary differential equation, but it is incomplete: the three specifications of Table 16.2 give \(275\), \(227\) and \(1539\ \mathrm{Pa/m}\) for \(U_b=1.00\ \mathrm{m/s}\) against the DNS value of \(125\ \mathrm{Pa/m}\) (completeness section), so an undamped specification cannot meet \(\pm10\ \%\). It becomes usable only with a specification calibrated on channel flows, as the lecture says of incomplete models for a narrow class with a body of semi-empirical knowledge (slide, L44 12:30–17:32), and its accuracy must then be assessed against data for this class. A DNS needs no specification, but the question is then answered at a cost out of proportion with a mean-flow question. The choice would change if client (a) needed several Reynolds numbers or geometries for which no calibration exists, or if a complete RANS closure were available (Pope 2004, p. 7 calls \(k\)–\(\varepsilon\) complete; the chapter titled “Turbulent viscosity models” treats them).

Exercise 5 (derive and compute). Using (16.2) with \(N\propto\mathrm{Re}_0^{3/4}\), find the factors by which the number of grid points, the number of time steps and the total work increase when the Reynolds number is doubled. Compare the ratio of grid points between the DNS of the channel at \(\mathrm{Re}_\tau=543.5\) and \(1000.5\) with the corresponding prediction, and state what the comparison does not test.

Solution

Doubling \(\mathrm{Re}_0\) multiplies \(N\) by \(2^{3/4}=1.682\), the number of grid points \(N^3\) by \(2^{9/4}=4.757\), the number of steps (proportional to \(N\)) by \(1.682\), and the work by \(1.682\times4.757=8=2^3\). If the memory is proportional to the number of points, it grows by \(4.76\). For the channel, the ratio of the Reynolds numbers is \(1000.512/543.496=1.841\), and \(1.841^{9/4}=3.95\), against a ratio of grid points of \(2416/604=4.00\) from the file headers. The comparison does not test the step count, which the headers do not give; it uses \(\mathrm{Re}_\tau\) as a stand-in for \(\mathrm{Re}_0\); and the grid sizes include the authors' choices of resolution.

Exercise 6 (derive). A calculation has truncation error \(5\ \%\) and cost \(T_0\). Using (16.9), find the cost needed to reduce the truncation error to \(0.5\ \%\) for a second-order (\(n=2\)) and a fourth-order (\(n=4\)) method. Then assume that the other three sources of the slide contribute a fixed \(2\ \%\) discrepancy and that all errors add in magnitude, and find the reduction of the total discrepancy that the cost buys.

Solution

From (16.9) the cost scales as \(e_{\mathrm{tr}}^{-4/n}\). A factor 10 in the error gives \(10^{4/2}=100\) for \(n=2\) and \(10^{4/4}=10\) for \(n=4\), so the cost is \(100\,T_0\) and \(10\,T_0\) (the same numbers as the program prints). With a fixed \(2\ \%\) from the other sources, added in magnitude (a worst case, assumed here), the total falls from \(7\ \%\) to \(2.5\ \%\), a factor of \(2.8\), for a factor 100 in cost with \(n=2\). Further refinement cannot take the total below \(2\ \%\). The assumptions are the asymptotic range, the dominance of the leading truncation term, a time step refined with the grid and errors that add; real errors may partly cancel or combine in quadrature.
