Spectral energy: Fourier modes, the velocity spectrum tensor, and one-dimensional spectra

A hot-wire probe in a stream records one velocity component along one line, so the spectrum it yields is a one-dimensional spectrum \(E_{11}(\kappa_1)\), not the three-dimensional energy spectrum \(E(\kappa)\) to which Kolmogorov's inertial-range law \(E=C\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3}\) refers. The two functions have the same \(-5/3\) slope in an isotropic inertial range, and their constants differ by the factor \(\tfrac{55}{18}\). A dissipation rate read from a measured \(E_{11}\) with the three-dimensional constant \(C=1.5\) is therefore too small by \((55/18)^{3/2}=5.34\). For the air-jet station of the preceding chapters, with \(\varepsilon=80\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\) and an isotropic test spectrum, the one-dimensional constant returns \(73.6\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\) and the three-dimensional constant returns \(13.8\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\). The two spectra also differ outside the inertial range: \(E(\kappa)\) vanishes at zero wavenumber, whereas \(E_{11}(\kappa_1)\) is largest there, because the line measurement at \(\kappa_1\) collects energy from every wavenumber vector whose magnitude exceeds \(\kappa_1\). This chapter derives these relations. It starts from the Fourier coefficients of a velocity field in a periodic box, shows that homogeneity makes the coefficients of different wavenumbers uncorrelated, connects the covariance of the coefficients to the velocity-spectrum tensor \(\Phi_{ij}\) as the box grows, reduces \(\Phi_{ij}\) to \(E(\kappa)\) for isotropic turbulence, and derives the one-dimensional spectra, their inversion and the constants \(\tfrac{55}{18}\) and \(\tfrac43\).

The objects of the chapter and the running case

The formal model has two levels. The first is the one of the chapter "Fourier modes and their evolution": a real velocity field \(\mathbf u(\mathbf x,t)\) of an incompressible fluid with constant kinematic viscosity \(\nu\), periodic with period \(\mathcal L\) in each Cartesian direction, divergence-free, with zero average over the cube \(0\le x_i\le\mathcal L\). It has the Fourier series \(\mathbf u=\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa}e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}\hat{\mathbf u}(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)\), in which the wavenumber vectors lie on the lattice \(\boldsymbol\kappa=\kappa_0\mathbf n\), with \(\kappa_0=2\pi/\mathcal L\) and \(\mathbf n\) a vector of integers. The coefficients satisfy the conjugate symmetry \(\hat{\mathbf u}(-\boldsymbol\kappa,t)=\hat{\mathbf u}^*(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)\), the asterisk denoting the complex conjugate, and the continuity equation \(\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\hat{\mathbf u}=0\). As in that chapter, \(\mathbf u\) is the whole velocity, because its mean is zero; \(\langle\cdot\rangle_V\) is the average over the box and \(\langle\cdot\rangle\) the expectation, the ensemble average of chapter 7. The second level is the statistically homogeneous field of chapter 8 in unbounded space, with two-point correlation \(R_{ij}(\mathbf r,t)=\langle u_i(\mathbf x,t)u_j(\mathbf x+\mathbf r,t)\rangle\) (8.26), velocity-spectrum tensor \(\Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)\) (8.29)–(8.30) and energy-spectrum function \(E(\kappa,t)\) (8.32). Isotropy, the invariance of all statistics under rotations and reflections of the axes (chapter 8, section "Isotropy"), is assumed only where a statement says so. The argument of \(E\) is always written, because \(E(\omega)\) of chapter 8 is a frequency spectrum.

The running case is the air-jet station of chapters 6 to 11 and of the chapter "Fourier modes and their evolution", with the values chosen in chapter 6: kinematic viscosity \(\nu=1.4607\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), energy-containing eddies of velocity \(u_0=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\) and size \(\ell_0=0.1\ \mathrm m\), so \(\mathrm{Re}_0=u_0\ell_0/\nu=1.37\times10^{4}\). With the unit constant in \(\varepsilon\sim u_0^3/\ell_0\) of chapter 5, (5.16), the dissipation rate is \(\varepsilon=80\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\), and the Kolmogorov length is \(\eta=(\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}=79.0\ \mu\mathrm m\) (5.14). The turbulent kinetic energy is taken as \(k=\tfrac32u_0^2=6\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\), as in chapter 11, which gives \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=453\) for these values. The station of a real jet is neither homogeneous nor known to be isotropic; the chapter treats it as homogeneous isotropic turbulence with these scales, so the numbers illustrate the relations and are not predictions for a particular jet. For the box level, the side is \(\mathcal L=1\ \mathrm m\), ten times \(\ell_0\), as in the chapter "Fourier modes and their evolution", so \(\kappa_0=6.28\ \mathrm{rad/m}\).

Several relations of the chapter need a spectrum with a definite shape. The program of the section "Executable check" uses an assumed test spectrum,

\[ E(\kappa)=C\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3}\Bigl[\frac{(\kappa\ell_L)^2}{1+(\kappa\ell_L)^2}\Bigr]^{11/6}e^{-\beta\kappa\eta}, \]

with \(C=1.5\) and two lengths fixed so that \(\int_0^\infty E\,d\kappa=k\) and \(2\nu\int_0^\infty\kappa^2E\,d\kappa=\varepsilon\): the program finds \(\ell_L=75.86\ \mathrm{mm}\) and \(\beta=2.093\). The form has the three properties that the slide of Lecture 36 lists for the spectra it compares: \(E\propto\kappa^2\) at low wavenumbers, a \(-5/3\) power in the middle and a decay faster than any power at high wavenumbers. It is a test function for the relations of this chapter, not a model of measured spectra; the model spectrum of the course is the subject of the chapter "Kolmogorov, model, and dissipation spectra". Its peak lies at \(\kappa_p\ell_L=(6/5)^{1/2}\), that is \(\kappa_p=14.4\ \mathrm{rad/m}\), a wavelength of \(0.44\ \mathrm m\).

The evolution equation with the nonlinear term written out L34 0:05

The chapter "Fourier modes and their evolution" ends with the equation of each coefficient with the pressure eliminated by the projection tensor \(P_{jk}(\boldsymbol\kappa)=\delta_{jk}-\kappa_j\kappa_k/\kappa^2\), \(d\hat u_j/dt+\nu\kappa^2\hat u_j=-P_{jk}(\boldsymbol\kappa)\hat G_k\) for \(\kappa\ne0\), in which \(\hat G_k(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)\) is the transform of the nonlinear term, and it writes \(\hat G_k\) as a sum over pairs of modes. Lecture 34 recalls the first result and derives the second (L34 10:32). The transform of the convection term is \(\hat G_j=\mathscr F_{\boldsymbol\kappa}\{\partial(u_ju_k)/\partial x_k\}=i\kappa_k\mathscr F_{\boldsymbol\kappa}\{u_ju_k\}\), by the derivative rule of that chapter, with \(\mathscr F_{\boldsymbol\kappa}\{g\}=\langle g\,e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}\rangle_V\) the coefficient of \(g\) at \(\boldsymbol\kappa\). Each factor of the product is replaced by its series, \(u_j=\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa'}\hat u_j(\boldsymbol\kappa')e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa'\cdot\mathbf x}\) and \(u_k=\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa''}\hat u_k(\boldsymbol\kappa'')e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa''\cdot\mathbf x}\). The coefficients do not depend on \(\mathbf x\) and leave the box average, which keeps only \(\langle e^{i(\boldsymbol\kappa'+\boldsymbol\kappa'')\cdot\mathbf x}e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}\rangle_V=\delta_{\boldsymbol\kappa,\boldsymbol\kappa'+\boldsymbol\kappa''}\), by the orthonormality of the modes (the slide writes the box average as \(\langle\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal L}\)). The Kronecker delta removes the sum over \(\boldsymbol\kappa''\) and sets \(\boldsymbol\kappa''=\boldsymbol\kappa-\boldsymbol\kappa'\), so that \(\hat G_j(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)=i\kappa_k\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa'}\hat u_j(\boldsymbol\kappa',t)\,\hat u_k(\boldsymbol\kappa-\boldsymbol\kappa',t)\). The lecture then substitutes this sum into the equation of the coefficient (L34 17:56). The result is

\[ \frac{d\hat u_j}{dt}+\nu\kappa^2\hat u_j=-i\kappa_l\,P_{jk}(\boldsymbol\kappa)\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa'}\hat u_k(\boldsymbol\kappa',t)\,\hat u_l(\boldsymbol\kappa-\boldsymbol\kappa',t)\qquad(\kappa\ne0). \tag{13.1} \]

Here \(j\) is the free index and \(k\), \(l\) are summed. The slide prints \(\hat u_j(\boldsymbol\kappa')\) inside the sum, so that \(j\) appears three times and \(k\) only once; the contraction with \(P_{jk}\) requires \(\hat u_k(\boldsymbol\kappa')\), as in (13.1). The lecture changes the dummy index of the nonlinear term to \(l\) because \(P_{jk}\) already uses \(k\).

The left side involves the coefficient at \(\boldsymbol\kappa\) only. The right side involves the coefficients at all pairs \(\boldsymbol\kappa'\), \(\boldsymbol\kappa''=\boldsymbol\kappa-\boldsymbol\kappa'\) whose vectors add to \(\boldsymbol\kappa\); the three vectors form a wavenumber triad. The slide states that the pairs with \(\boldsymbol\kappa'=\boldsymbol\kappa\) or \(\boldsymbol\kappa''=\boldsymbol\kappa\) contribute nothing, and the lecture ascribes this to the Kronecker delta of the derivation. The delta only fixes \(\boldsymbol\kappa''=\boldsymbol\kappa-\boldsymbol\kappa'\); it does not remove these pairs. The reason they vanish is the zero box average: \(\boldsymbol\kappa'=\boldsymbol\kappa\) makes \(\boldsymbol\kappa''=0\), and \(\hat{\mathbf u}(0,t)=\langle\mathbf u\rangle_V=0\); the case \(\boldsymbol\kappa''=\boldsymbol\kappa\) is the same with the roles exchanged. The slide calls the convection term "nonlinear and non-local" in wavenumber space: non-local means that the rate of change at \(\boldsymbol\kappa\) depends on coefficients at wavevectors far from \(\boldsymbol\kappa\), as opposed to the viscous term, which is local, because it involves \(\boldsymbol\kappa\) only. Equation (13.1) is, in the slide's words, a deterministic set of ordinary differential equations for the coefficients (L34 20:36); the description of turbulence by statistics of the coefficients begins in the section "The covariance of the coefficients".

The final period of decay L34 6:33

The slide considers the final period of decay of isotropic turbulence, in which the Reynolds number is so low that convection is negligible relative to the effects of viscosity. Dropping the right side of (13.1), each coefficient obeys \(d\hat u_j/dt=-\nu\kappa^2\hat u_j\), with the solution

\[ \hat{\mathbf u}(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)=\hat{\mathbf u}(\boldsymbol\kappa,0)\,e^{-\nu\kappa^2t}. \tag{13.2} \]

Each coefficient then evolves independently of all others, and high-wavenumber modes decay faster than low-wavenumber ones. The rate \(\nu\kappa^2\) is the decay rate of the coefficient; the energy of the mode, \(\tfrac12|\hat{\mathbf u}|^2\), decays at \(2\nu\kappa^2\). The neglect of the right side is an assumption about the size of the motion: the nonlinear term at \(\boldsymbol\kappa\) is of order \(\kappa\) times the product of two velocities, so its ratio to the viscous term \(\nu\kappa^2\hat u\) is a Reynolds number of the motion at that scale, and (13.2) holds when that number is small for all modes that carry energy. It does not hold for the jet station: there \(\mathrm{Re}_0=1.37\times10^{4}\), and the viscous decay time of the mode at the spectral peak, \(1/(2\nu\kappa_p^2)=164\ \mathrm s\), is about \(3\times10^{3}\) times the eddy turnover time \(\ell_0/u_0=0.05\ \mathrm s\).

The lecture states the decay of each mode. The decay of the kinetic energy follows from it and from the energy spectrum. Under (13.2) the spectrum at time \(t\) is the initial one multiplied by \(e^{-2\nu\kappa^2t}\), because every mode on the shell \(|\boldsymbol\kappa|=\kappa\) decays at the same rate, so that \(\Phi_{ii}(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)=\Phi_{ii}(\boldsymbol\kappa,0)e^{-2\nu\kappa^2t}\) and (8.32) carries the factor to \(E\); the section "The energy-spectrum function as an integral over spherical shells" gives the shell picture in the box. So

\[ k(t)=\int_0^\infty E(\kappa,0)\,e^{-2\nu\kappa^2t}\,d\kappa. \tag{13.3} \]

At large \(t\) the factor \(e^{-2\nu\kappa^2t}\) leaves only wavenumbers below about \((2\nu t)^{-1/2}\), where the initial spectrum has its low-wavenumber form. If \(E(\kappa,0)\approx A_s\kappa^s\) there, with a constant \(A_s\) and an exponent \(s\) (both local to this section; \(s\) is not the time lag of chapter 8), the substitution \(x=\kappa(2\nu t)^{1/2}\) gives

\[ k(t)\approx\tfrac12A_s\,\Gamma\Bigl(\frac{s+1}2\Bigr)(2\nu t)^{-(s+1)/2}, \tag{13.4} \]

with \(\Gamma(\cdot)\) the gamma function. The decay exponent of the final period is set by the low-wavenumber exponent of the spectrum alone: \(k\propto t^{-3/2}\) for \(s=2\) and \(k\propto t^{-5/2}\) for \(s=4\). The program evaluates (13.3) for the test spectrum, which has \(s=2\), and finds the logarithmic slope \(-1.500\) at \(t=10^7\ \mathrm s\). The section "Comparison of the spectra" shows what fixes \(s\).

The covariance of the coefficients, the kinetic energy and the dissipation L34 21:42

The coefficients \(\hat{\mathbf u}(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)\) of a turbulent field are random: they differ from one realization of the flow to the next. The slide takes the mean velocity to be zero, so that the expectations of the coefficients are zero, and turns to the next statistic, the covariance \(\langle\hat u_i(\boldsymbol\kappa',t)\hat u_j(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)\rangle\) of two coefficients. It states that these coefficients are uncorrelated unless \(\boldsymbol\kappa'+\boldsymbol\kappa=0\). The lecture gives conjugate symmetry as the reason. Conjugate symmetry is a property of each realization and says only that the coefficient at \(-\boldsymbol\kappa\) is the conjugate of the one at \(\boldsymbol\kappa\); it says nothing about the correlation of coefficients at, say, \(\boldsymbol\kappa\) and \(2\boldsymbol\kappa\). The statement needs a statistical assumption, and the assumption is homogeneity.

Claim. If the field is statistically homogeneous, then \(\langle\hat{\mathbf u}(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)\rangle=0\) for \(\boldsymbol\kappa\ne0\), and \(\langle\hat u_i(\boldsymbol\kappa',t)\hat u_j(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)\rangle=0\) unless \(\boldsymbol\kappa'=-\boldsymbol\kappa\).

Proof. Insert the series twice in the two-point correlation:

\[ \langle u_i(\mathbf x)u_j(\mathbf x+\mathbf r)\rangle=\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa'}\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa}\langle\hat u_i(\boldsymbol\kappa')\hat u_j(\boldsymbol\kappa)\rangle\,e^{i(\boldsymbol\kappa'+\boldsymbol\kappa)\cdot\mathbf x}\,e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}. \tag{13.5} \]

Homogeneity states that the left side does not depend on \(\mathbf x\). For fixed \(\mathbf r\), the right side is a Fourier series in \(\mathbf x\); the orthonormality of the modes (average over the box against \(e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x}\)) extracts its coefficient at any \(\mathbf q\ne0\), which must vanish: \(\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa}\langle\hat u_i(\mathbf q-\boldsymbol\kappa)\hat u_j(\boldsymbol\kappa)\rangle e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}=0\) for every \(\mathbf r\). This is again a Fourier series, now in \(\mathbf r\), and a series that vanishes for every \(\mathbf r\) has all coefficients zero. So \(\langle\hat u_i(\boldsymbol\kappa')\hat u_j(\boldsymbol\kappa)\rangle=0\) whenever \(\boldsymbol\kappa'+\boldsymbol\kappa=\mathbf q\ne0\). The same argument applied to the one-point mean \(\langle\mathbf u(\mathbf x)\rangle=\sum\langle\hat{\mathbf u}(\boldsymbol\kappa)\rangle e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}\), which is independent of \(\mathbf x\), gives \(\langle\hat{\mathbf u}(\boldsymbol\kappa)\rangle=0\) for \(\boldsymbol\kappa\ne0\). \(\blacksquare\)

The hinge is that a translation of the field by \(\mathbf a\) multiplies the coefficient at \(\boldsymbol\kappa\) by \(e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf a}\), and a product of two coefficients by \(e^{i(\boldsymbol\kappa'+\boldsymbol\kappa)\cdot\mathbf a}\); a statistic that is unchanged by every translation can be nonzero only where that factor is 1. The zero mean of the coefficients at \(\boldsymbol\kappa\ne0\) needs homogeneity, not a zero mean velocity; the mean velocity enters only the coefficient at \(\boldsymbol\kappa=0\), which is zero in this model.

All the second-order information therefore lies in the pairs \(\boldsymbol\kappa'=-\boldsymbol\kappa\). With conjugate symmetry, the slide defines the covariance of the Fourier coefficients

\[ \hat R_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)\equiv\langle\hat u_i(-\boldsymbol\kappa,t)\,\hat u_j(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)\rangle=\langle\hat u_i^*(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)\,\hat u_j(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)\rangle . \tag{13.6} \]

Keeping only the terms \(\boldsymbol\kappa'=-\boldsymbol\kappa\) in (13.5) gives the two-point correlation of the box as a Fourier series with the covariances as coefficients:

\[ R_{ij}(\mathbf r,t)=\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa}\hat R_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)\,e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}. \tag{13.7} \]

Exercise 7 obtains (13.7) by a second route, the one of the course's practice problem, which averages over the box instead of invoking homogeneity for every \(\mathbf x\). Exercise 8 derives three properties of \(\hat R_{ij}\): \(\hat R_{ii}\ge0\) for each \(i\) (no summation), \(\hat R_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)=\hat R_{ji}(-\boldsymbol\kappa)=\hat R_{ji}^*(\boldsymbol\kappa)\), and \(\kappa_i\hat R_{ij}=\kappa_j\hat R_{ij}=0\). The last one follows from the continuity equation and is used below.

The first property is the diagonal case of a stronger one. For a fixed complex vector \(\mathbf a\), the combination \(a_j\hat u_j(\boldsymbol\kappa)\) is a complex random variable, and \(\langle|a_j\hat u_j|^2\rangle=a_i^*\hat R_{ij}a_j\ge0\). So the matrix \(\hat R_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)\), which is Hermitian by the second property, is positive semidefinite at every \(\boldsymbol\kappa\). Its \(2\times2\) principal minors are therefore non-negative, which bounds the off-diagonal entries by the diagonal ones: \(|\hat R_{12}|^2\le\hat R_{11}\hat R_{22}\), and likewise for the other pairs of indices. The ratio \(|\hat R_{12}|^2/(\hat R_{11}\hat R_{22})\) is the squared coherence of \(u_1\) and \(u_2\) at that wavenumber and lies between 0 and 1. The bound passes to \(\Phi_{ij}\) through the limit of the next section.

Kinetic energy and dissipation. Setting \(\mathbf r=0\) in (13.7) and taking half the trace gives the turbulent kinetic energy as a sum over modes. The slide (L34 23:22) names the term of each mode \(\hat E\):

\[ k(t)=\tfrac12\langle u_iu_i\rangle=\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa}\hat E(\boldsymbol\kappa,t),\qquad\hat E(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)\equiv\tfrac12\hat R_{ii}(\boldsymbol\kappa,t),\qquad\varepsilon(t)=\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa}2\nu\kappa^2\hat E(\boldsymbol\kappa,t). \tag{13.8} \]

The dissipation sum follows from chapter 11, (11.4), \(\varepsilon=\nu\langle\partial_ju_i\,\partial_ju_i\rangle\), which holds for a homogeneous incompressible field: differentiating (13.7) twice with respect to \(\mathbf r\) at \(\mathbf r=0\) gives \(\langle\partial_ju_i\,\partial_ju_i\rangle=-\nabla_r^2R_{ii}(0)=\sum\kappa^2\hat R_{ii}\), so \(\varepsilon=\nu\sum\kappa^2\hat R_{ii}=\sum2\nu\kappa^2\hat E\). The factor \(\tfrac12\) in \(\hat E\) is the one on the slide of Lecture 34. The slide of Lecture 31 wrote the modal energy without it, \(\langle\hat{\mathbf u}^*\cdot\hat{\mathbf u}\rangle\), and the chapter "Fourier modes and their evolution" kept that symbol for the slide's quantity while defining the energy per unit mass of a mode in one realization as \(\hat e=\tfrac12\hat{\mathbf u}^*\cdot\hat{\mathbf u}\). The \(\hat E\) of (13.8) is the expectation of that \(\hat e\), half of the Lecture 31 quantity, with units \(\mathrm{m^2/s^2}\). Each chapter states its convention at first use.

From the box to the velocity-spectrum tensor L35 3:17

Lecture 35 turns to unbounded homogeneous turbulence and recalls the Fourier pair of chapter 8: \(\Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)=(2\pi)^{-3}\iiint R_{ij}(\mathbf r)e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}d\mathbf r\) (8.29) and \(R_{ij}(\mathbf r)=\iiint\Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}d\boldsymbol\kappa\) (8.30), with \(\boldsymbol\kappa\) now a continuous vector. The slide of Module 08 prints the prefactor as "\(1/2\pi^3\)" on both transforms (L35 4:00 and L36 1:00), and it omits the volume elements \(d\mathbf r\) and \(d\boldsymbol\kappa\). Both prefactors cannot hold: applying the two transforms in turn would then multiply \(R_{ij}\) by \((2\pi)^{-3}\). The next slide's \(R_{ij}(0)=\iiint\Phi_{ij}\,d\boldsymbol\kappa\), which the lecture uses, is consistent only with chapter 8's placement of the whole factor \((2\pi)^{-3}\) on the forward transform. This chapter uses chapter 8's pair. The slide's description of \(\Phi_{ij}\) is that it is the Reynolds-stress density in wavenumber space: the contribution, per unit volume of wavenumber space, of the mode \(e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}\) to the Reynolds stress \(\langle u_iu_j\rangle\). The box makes this description exact in a limit.

The limit. By orthonormality, (13.7) can be inverted over one period of the separation:

\[ \hat R_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)=\bigl\langle R_{ij}(\mathbf r)\,e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}\bigr\rangle_V=\frac1{\mathcal L^3}\iiint_{\text{box}}R_{ij}(\mathbf r)\,e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}\,d\mathbf r . \tag{13.9} \]

The correlation of the periodic field is itself periodic in \(\mathbf r\) (the chapter "Fourier modes and their evolution" shows \(R_{ij}(\mathcal L\mathbf e_1)=R_{ij}(0)\)), so the integral may be taken over the box centred at \(\mathbf r=0\). If \(\mathcal L\) is large compared with the integral scale \(L_{11}\) of (8.28), the periodic correlation over that box is close to the correlation of unbounded turbulence, which has decayed before the edge of the box. The integral is then close to the full integral in (8.29), and since \(\mathcal L^{-3}=\kappa_0^3/(2\pi)^3\),

\[ \hat R_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)\approx\kappa_0^3\,\Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)\qquad(\mathcal L\gg L_{11}). \tag{13.10} \]

The factor \(\kappa_0^3\) is the volume of wavenumber space per lattice point. Equation (13.10) is the precise form of the slide's "per unit volume in wavenumber space": \(\Phi_{ij}\) is the covariance of one coefficient divided by the volume that the coefficient occupies. The same relation read in the other direction turns the sum (13.7) into a Riemann sum for the integral (8.30), \(\sum\hat R_{ij}e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}\approx\sum\Phi_{ij}e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}\kappa_0^3\to\iiint\Phi_{ij}e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}d\boldsymbol\kappa\), and the sums (13.8) into \(k=\iiint\tfrac12\Phi_{ii}\,d\boldsymbol\kappa\) and \(\varepsilon=\iiint2\nu\kappa^2\,\tfrac12\Phi_{ii}\,d\boldsymbol\kappa=\nu\iiint\kappa^2\Phi_{ii}\,d\boldsymbol\kappa\). The second is chapter 11's \(\varepsilon=\nu\langle\partial_ju_i\,\partial_ju_i\rangle\) (11.4) with \(\langle\partial_ju_i\,\partial_ju_i\rangle=-[\nabla_r^2R_{ii}]_{\mathbf r=0}=\iiint\kappa^2\Phi_{ii}\,d\boldsymbol\kappa\) from (8.30). The slide's dissipation integral (L35 11:13) prints \(\varepsilon=\iiint2\nu\kappa^2\Phi_{ii}\,d\boldsymbol\kappa\), without the factor \(\tfrac12\) of the half trace; that expression is \(2\varepsilon\), and it is not consistent with the slide's own \(E(\kappa)=\oint\tfrac12\Phi_{ii}\,d\mathcal S\) and \(\varepsilon=\int_0^\infty2\nu\kappa^2E\,d\kappa\) of the next section. This chapter keeps the \(\tfrac12\). The positivity \(\hat R_{ii}\ge0\) of Exercise 8 passes through the limit to \(\Phi_{ii}(\boldsymbol\kappa)\ge0\) for each \(i\); this is the route by which this chapter obtains it.

How large the box must be. The approximation (13.10) has two errors: the lattice samples \(\Phi_{ij}\) at spacing \(\kappa_0\), and no mode exists below \(\kappa_0\). Both matter where the spectrum varies on the scale \(\kappa_0\), that is near the energy-containing peak. The program evaluates the lattice sum \(\sum\tfrac12\hat R_{ii}=\sum\kappa_0^3E(\kappa)/(4\pi\kappa^2)\) for the test spectrum (the isotropic form of \(\tfrac12\Phi_{ii}\), from (13.12) below), up to \(|\mathbf n|=320\), plus the integral of \(E\) above that cut. Against \(k=6\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\) it finds \(5.9993\) for \(\mathcal L=4\ \mathrm m\), \(5.9571\) for \(\mathcal L=1\ \mathrm m\) (0.7 % low) and \(4.7002\) for \(\mathcal L=0.25\ \mathrm m\) (22 % low). The last box has \(\kappa_0=25.1\ \mathrm{rad/m}\), above the spectral peak \(\kappa_p=14.4\ \mathrm{rad/m}\): no lattice vector lies below the peak, so the box cannot contain the energy-containing motions. The box of side \(1\ \mathrm m\) has 56 lattice vectors with \(0\lt|\boldsymbol\kappa|\le\kappa_p\). The condition \(\mathcal L\gg L_{11}\) is therefore a condition that \(\kappa_0\) lie well below the peak of the spectrum; for the test spectrum \(L_{11}=88.8\ \mathrm{mm}\), and the three boxes have \(\mathcal L/L_{11}=2.8\), 11 and 45.

Three kinds of information. The slide (L35 8:51) separates what \(\Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)\) contains: the indices \(i\), \(j\) give the directions of the two velocity components; the direction \(\boldsymbol\kappa/|\boldsymbol\kappa|\) gives the direction in physical space along which the mode varies; and the magnitude \(|\boldsymbol\kappa|\) gives the length of the mode, the wavelength \(\ell=2\pi/|\boldsymbol\kappa|\). The energy-spectrum function as an integral over spherical shells L35 11:44

Integration over all wavenumber vectors of one magnitude removes the direction of the mode. With \(\mathcal S(\kappa)\) the sphere in wavenumber space centred at the origin with radius \(\kappa\), and \(\oint(\ )\,d\mathcal S(\kappa)\) the integral over its surface,

\[ E(\kappa,t)=\oint\tfrac12\Phi_{ii}(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)\,d\mathcal S(\kappa). \tag{13.11} \]

The slide prints \(\mathcal S(\kappa)\) in place of \(d\mathcal S(\kappa)\) in the integrand. How many modes a shell holds. In the box, the shell \(n-\tfrac12\le|\mathbf n|\lt n+\tfrac12\), one lattice spacing thick, contains about \(4\pi n^2\) lattice vectors when \(n\) is large, and few when \(n\) is small: the program counts 18 vectors for \(n=1\), 62 for \(n=2\) and 350 for \(n=5\). Assume, for this estimate only, that the coefficients are complex Gaussian random variables, independent from one pair \(\pm\boldsymbol\kappa\) to another (homogeneity makes them uncorrelated, by the claim of the section "The covariance of the coefficients"; independence is the added assumption), and that the two components perpendicular to \(\boldsymbol\kappa\) have equal variance, as isotropy requires, and are uncorrelated, which (13.12) gives because \(\Phi_{ij}=\mathcal AP_{ij}\) is diagonal in any pair of axes perpendicular to \(\boldsymbol\kappa\); for Gaussian variables uncorrelated means independent. Then the squared modulus of each perpendicular component is exponentially distributed, with standard deviation equal to its mean. A shell of \(N\) lattice vectors holds \(N/2\) independent pairs with two components each, so its energy in one realization is a sum of \(N\) independent exponential variables. In isotropic turbulence the tensor is fixed by the energy spectrum L35 18:20

The slide states that \(\Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)\) in general contains more information than \(E(\kappa)\), and that in isotropic turbulence \(\Phi_{ij}\) is completely determined by \(E\). The statement needs incompressibility as well as isotropy, and its proof gives the formula that the one-dimensional spectra need.

Claim. For isotropic turbulence of an incompressible fluid,

\[ \Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)=\frac{E(\kappa)}{4\pi\kappa^2}\Bigl(\delta_{ij}-\frac{\kappa_i\kappa_j}{\kappa^2}\Bigr)=\frac{E(\kappa)}{4\pi\kappa^2}\,P_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa). \tag{13.12} \]

Proof. Fix \(\boldsymbol\kappa\ne0\) and choose axes with \(\mathbf e_1\) along \(\boldsymbol\kappa\). Isotropy requires \(\Phi_{ij}\) to be unchanged by every rotation and reflection of the axes that leaves \(\boldsymbol\kappa\) unchanged. The reflection \(x_2\to-x_2\) changes the sign of every component with exactly one index 2, so \(\Phi_{12}=\Phi_{21}=\Phi_{23}=\Phi_{32}=0\); the reflection \(x_3\to-x_3\) gives \(\Phi_{13}=\Phi_{31}=0\). The rotation by \(90^\circ\) about \(\mathbf e_1\) exchanges the 2 and 3 axes, so \(\Phi_{22}=\Phi_{33}\). Hence \(\Phi=\mathrm{diag}(a,b,b)\) in these axes, which in any axes is \(\Phi_{ij}=\mathcal A\delta_{ij}+\mathcal B\kappa_i\kappa_j\) with \(\mathcal A=b\) and \(\mathcal B=(a-b)/\kappa^2\); rotations of \(\boldsymbol\kappa\) itself make \(\mathcal A\) and \(\mathcal B\) functions of \(\kappa\) only. Incompressibility gives \(\kappa_i\Phi_{ij}=0\), the limit of \(\kappa_i\hat R_{ij}=0\) (Exercise 8), so \(a\kappa=0\), \(a=0\) and \(\mathcal B=-\mathcal A/\kappa^2\). Then \(\Phi_{ij}=\mathcal AP_{ij}\) and \(\Phi_{ii}=2\mathcal A\), because the trace of the projection tensor is 2. Chapter 8, (8.34), gives \(E=2\pi\kappa^2\Phi_{ii}=4\pi\kappa^2\mathcal A\) for an isotropic \(\Phi_{ii}\), so \(\mathcal A=E/(4\pi\kappa^2)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Both assumptions are used. Without incompressibility a second scalar function remains, the longitudinal part \(a\). Without reflection invariance, rotations alone allow a third term proportional to \(\epsilon_{ijl}\kappa_l\), with \(\epsilon_{ijl}\) the alternating symbol; it is antisymmetric, so it contributes nothing to \(\Phi_{ii}\) and to the spectra \(E_{11}\), \(E_{22}\) of the next sections, but \(\Phi_{ij}\) is then not fixed by \(E\) alone. The lecture's description of isotropy as invariance under reflections and rotations (L36 6:21), like the definition of chapter 8, excludes this term. The projection tensor is the one that removed the pressure in (13.1): in isotropic turbulence the spectrum tensor at each \(\boldsymbol\kappa\) is energy spread equally over the two directions perpendicular to \(\boldsymbol\kappa\).

One-dimensional spectra and their relation to the energy spectrum L36 6:05

A probe that records \(u_i\) and \(u_j\) along a line parallel to \(x_1\) gives the correlation \(R_{ij}(r_1\mathbf e_1)\) as a function of the scalar separation \(r_1\), and its Fourier transform is a one-dimensional spectrum. The slide relates \(E_{11}(\kappa_1)\) to the longitudinal autocorrelation function without writing its definition. This chapter defines

\[ E_{ij}(\kappa_1)\equiv\frac1\pi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}R_{ij}(r_1\mathbf e_1)\,e^{-i\kappa_1r_1}\,dr_1=2\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)\,d\kappa_2\,d\kappa_3 . \tag{13.13} \]

The second form follows from (8.30) at \(\mathbf r=r_1\mathbf e_1\): \(R_{ij}(r_1\mathbf e_1)=\int e^{i\kappa_1r_1}\bigl[\iint\Phi_{ij}\,d\kappa_2d\kappa_3\bigr]d\kappa_1\), so the bracket is the one-dimensional Fourier transform of the line correlation with the factor \(1/(2\pi)\), and (13.13) has the factor \(1/\pi\). The factor 2 makes the spectrum one-sided: \(R_{11}\) is real and even, \(E_{11}\) is real and even in \(\kappa_1\), and \(E_{11}(\kappa_1)=(2/\pi)\int_0^\infty R_{11}(r\mathbf e_1)\cos(\kappa_1r)\,dr\), with the inverse \(R_{11}(r\mathbf e_1)=\int_0^\infty E_{11}(\kappa_1)\cos(\kappa_1r)\,d\kappa_1\). At \(r=0\) this gives \(\int_0^\infty E_{11}\,d\kappa_1=\langle u_1^2\rangle\), the normalization used in chapter 11. Here \(\kappa_1\) is the component of the wavenumber vector along the line, not a magnitude.

The longitudinal spectrum of isotropic turbulence. Insert (13.12) with \(i=j=1\) in the second form of (13.13). The integrand depends on \(\kappa_2\), \(\kappa_3\) only through \(\kappa_\perp^2=\kappa_2^2+\kappa_3^2\), with \(\kappa_\perp\) the distance from the \(\kappa_1\) axis. With polar coordinates \(\kappa_\perp\), \(\varphi\) in the \((\kappa_2,\kappa_3)\) plane (both local to this derivation), \(d\kappa_2\,d\kappa_3=\kappa_\perp\,d\kappa_\perp\,d\varphi\), and the substitution \(\kappa^2=\kappa_1^2+\kappa_\perp^2\), \(\kappa_\perp\,d\kappa_\perp=\kappa\,d\kappa\), maps \(\kappa_\perp\) from 0 to \(\infty\) to \(\kappa\) from \(\kappa_1\) to \(\infty\) (for \(\kappa_1\ge0\)):

\[ E_{11}(\kappa_1)=2\cdot2\pi\int_{\kappa_1}^\infty\frac{E(\kappa)}{4\pi\kappa^2}\Bigl(1-\frac{\kappa_1^2}{\kappa^2}\Bigr)\kappa\,d\kappa=\int_{\kappa_1}^\infty\frac{E(\kappa)}{\kappa}\Bigl(1-\frac{\kappa_1^2}{\kappa^2}\Bigr)d\kappa . \tag{13.14} \]

This is the slide's formula, which the lecture states without derivation ("I am not going to be dealing with how to derive it"). Its structure states the observation of the opening paragraph: \(E_{11}\) at \(\kappa_1\) is an integral of \(E\) over all magnitudes \(\kappa\ge\kappa_1\), because a line along \(x_1\) sees a mode of magnitude \(\kappa\) whose direction is oblique to the line as a variation with the smaller wavenumber \(\kappa_1=\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf e_1\lt\kappa\). Energy that belongs to small scales in three dimensions appears at low wavenumbers in one dimension. The weight \(1-\kappa_1^2/\kappa^2\) is proportional to the share of the mode's energy that lies in the component \(u_1\): by (13.12), \(\Phi_{11}/\Phi_{ii}=\tfrac12(1-\kappa_1^2/\kappa^2)\), and the factor 2 of the one-sided definition (13.13) doubles it. A mode with \(\boldsymbol\kappa\) along \(\mathbf e_1\) has no \(u_1\) (its velocity is perpendicular to \(\boldsymbol\kappa\)).

The share of \(E_{11}(\kappa_1)\) that comes from larger magnitudes can be computed for the inertial-range form \(E=C\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3}\) taken as exact for all \(\kappa\ge\kappa_1\). With \(x=\kappa/\kappa_1\), (13.14) gives \(E_{11}=C\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa_1^{-5/3}\int_1^\infty(x^{-8/3}-x^{-14/3})\,dx=\tfrac{18}{55}C\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa_1^{-5/3}\), and the part from \(\kappa\gt a\kappa_1\) is the fraction \(\tfrac{55}{18}\bigl(\tfrac35a^{-5/3}-\tfrac3{11}a^{-11/3}\bigr)\) of it: 51 % for \(a=2\) and 3.9 % for \(a=10\). About half of the one-dimensional spectrum at \(\kappa_1\) belongs to three-dimensional wavenumbers more than twice as large. Exercise 6 uses the same integral for a power law that ends.

Three consequences can be read from (13.14).

Normalization. Exchanging the order of integration, \(\int_0^\infty E_{11}\,d\kappa_1=\int_0^\infty(E/\kappa)\int_0^\kappa(1-\kappa_1^2/\kappa^2)\,d\kappa_1\,d\kappa=\tfrac23\int_0^\infty E\,d\kappa=\tfrac23k\), which is \(\langle u_1^2\rangle\) in isotropic turbulence, as (13.13) requires.

Monotonicity. The boundary term of the derivative vanishes, because the bracket of (13.14) is zero at \(\kappa=\kappa_1\), so

\[ \frac{dE_{11}}{d\kappa_1}=-2\kappa_1\int_{\kappa_1}^\infty\frac{E(\kappa)}{\kappa^3}\,d\kappa\le0, \tag{13.15} \]

since \(E\ge0\). So \(E_{11}\) never increases, and its maximum is at \(\kappa_1=0\), where \(E_{11}(0)=\int_0^\infty E/\kappa\,d\kappa\). The slide's statement that the one-dimensional spectra are largest at zero wavenumber is a theorem for \(E_{11}\); the next section shows that it fails for \(E_{22}\).

Inversion. Dividing (13.15) by \(\kappa_1\) and differentiating once more gives \(d(\kappa_1^{-1}dE_{11}/d\kappa_1)/d\kappa_1=2E(\kappa_1)/\kappa_1^3\), so

\[ E(\kappa)=\frac12\kappa^3\frac{d}{d\kappa}\Bigl(\frac1\kappa\frac{dE_{11}(\kappa)}{d\kappa}\Bigr), \tag{13.16} \]

the slide's inversion (L36 7:43), in which \(E_{11}\) is evaluated at the argument \(\kappa\). Yeung and Zhou (1997, ICASE Report 97-64, eq. (2), report p. 2) state the same relation as a kinematic constraint between one- and three-dimensional spectra that holds if the turbulence at the scale \(1/\kappa\) is isotropic. The program applies (13.16) by finite differences to the \(E_{11}\) computed from the test spectrum with (13.14) and recovers \(E\) to a relative error of \(1.5\times10^{-5}\) for \(10^{-3}\lt\kappa\eta\lt1\).

The direction of the two relations matters. Equation (13.16) is local: \(E\) at \(\kappa\) needs only \(E_{11}\) and its first two derivatives at \(\kappa\). Equation (13.14) is not: \(E_{11}\) at \(\kappa_1\) needs \(E\) at every \(\kappa\ge\kappa_1\). A measured \(E_{11}\) therefore determines \(E\) wavenumber by wavenumber, through second derivatives of measured data, and only if the turbulence is isotropic at those scales. The lecture remarks, of the functions it considers in isotropic turbulence, that "this applies to other flows as well" (L36 6:16); (13.12) and therefore (13.14) and (13.16) hold under isotropy, and in another flow they hold to the extent that the scales concerned are locally isotropic, which is a hypothesis to be tested (chapter 11 reports Saddoughi's test).

Power laws and the constants of the three spectra L36 10:55

In examining Kolmogorov's hypotheses, the slide takes the one-dimensional spectrum in a power-law form, \(E_{11}(\kappa_1)=C_1A\kappa_1^{-p}\), with \(C_1\) a constant and \(A\) a normalization factor; its example is \(A=\langle u_1^2\rangle L_{11}^{1-p}\), which gives \(E_{11}\) the units \(\mathrm{m^2/s^2}\cdot\mathrm m^{1-p}\cdot\mathrm m^{p}=\mathrm{m^3/s^2}\) for any \(p\). \[ E(\kappa)=CA\kappa^{-p},\quad C=\tfrac12p(2+p)\,C_1;\qquad E_{22}(\kappa_1)=C_1'A\kappa_1^{-p},\quad C_1'=\tfrac12(1+p)\,C_1 . \tag{13.20} \]

On which interval the ratios hold. The ratios \(\tfrac{55}{18}\) and \(\tfrac43\) are exact for an infinite power law and approximate over a finite inertial range.

  4. At low wavenumber \(E_{11}\) and \(E_{22}\) are in the ratio \(2:1\), consistent with the ratio of the integral scales. This is (13.18) at \(\kappa_1=0\) together with (13.17).

What fixes the low-wavenumber exponent. If \(R_{ii}(\mathbf r)\) is absolutely integrable over all separations, \(\Phi_{ii}\) is continuous, and (13.11) gives \(E(\kappa)=2\pi\kappa^2\Phi_{ii}(0)+o(\kappa^2)\) as \(\kappa\to0\), with \(\Phi_{ii}(0)=(2\pi)^{-3}\iiint R_{ii}(\mathbf r)\,d\mathbf r\) by (8.29). The spectrum vanishes as \(\kappa^2\) if that integral is nonzero, and faster if it is zero. In isotropic turbulence the integral can be written with chapter 11's functions: \(R_{ii}=u'^2(f+2g)\) by (11.17), and \(g=f+\tfrac r2f'\) by (11.18), so \(r^2R_{ii}=u'^2\,d(r^3f)/dr\) and

\[ \iiint R_{ii}(\mathbf r)\,d\mathbf r=4\pi\int_0^\infty r^2R_{ii}\,dr=4\pi u'^2\lim_{r\to\infty}r^3f(r). \tag{13.22} \]

A spectrum \(E\propto\kappa^2\) at low wavenumbers therefore requires the longitudinal correlation to decay as slowly as \(r^{-3}\); a correlation that decays faster, for example exponentially, makes the coefficient zero. Then \(E\) vanishes at least as fast as \(\kappa^4\): when \(R_{ii}\) has finite second moments, \(\Phi_{ii}\) has a Taylor expansion at \(\boldsymbol\kappa=0\), and \(R_{ii}(\mathbf r)=R_{ii}(-\mathbf r)\) (8.27) removes the odd powers, so the next term after the constant is of order \(\kappa^2\) and \(E=2\pi\kappa^2\Phi_{ii}\) is of order \(\kappa^4\) (Exercise 3 has a Gaussian correlation and \(E\propto\kappa^4\)). The exponent is set by the largest scales of the flow and its history, and it decides the decay law (13.4) of the final period. The slide's figure of longitudinal correlations measured in grid turbulence at \(x_1/M=42\), 98 and 172 (L36 19:06; the caption attributes the data to Comte-Bellot and Corrsin, 1971), with \(M\) the mesh size of the grid, shows \(f(r)\) decreasing to below 0.05 at \(r/M\approx4\); the plotted range does not show the decay rate at large \(r\) that (13.22) requires, and this chapter makes no claim about it.

Executable check: the test spectrum, the one-dimensional spectra, the constants and the box

The program below implements the relations of the chapter for the test spectrum at the jet's values. Inputs, set in the code: \(\nu\), \(u_0\), \(\ell_0\), \(C=1.5\), and the wavenumber grid \(10^{-3}\le\kappa\le10^{7}\ \mathrm{rad/m}\) with 20001 logarithmically spaced points. It fixes the two lengths of the test spectrum by bisection so that \(k=6\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\) and \(\varepsilon=80\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\). It computes \(E_{11}\) and \(E_{22}\) from (13.14) and (13.18) as \(I_1-\kappa_1^2I_3\) and \(\tfrac12(I_1+\kappa_1^2I_3)\), with \(I_1\), \(I_3\) the tail integrals of \(E/\kappa\) and \(E/\kappa^3\); asserts the monotonicity (13.15); checks the normalizations, the ratio \(E_{22}(0)/E_{11}(0)\), the two forms of (13.17), the inversion (13.16), the local ratios against (13.20), the estimates (13.21), the late-time slope of (13.3), the lattice sums of the section "From the box to the velocity-spectrum tensor", the number of lattice vectors below the spectral peak and the shell populations of the section "The energy-spectrum function as an integral over spherical shells". import numpy as np

# Running case: the air-jet station of chapters 6-11, treated as homogeneous
# isotropic turbulence. SI units.
nu, u0, ell0 = 1.4607e-5, 2.0, 0.1
eps, k = u0**3/ell0, 1.5*u0**2          # 80 m^2/s^3, 6 m^2/s^2
eta = (nu**3/eps)**0.25
C, C1 = 1.5, 18/55*1.5                  # 3D constant; isotropic 1D constant
kap = np.logspace(-3, 7, 20001)         # wavenumber grid, rad/m
lnk = np.log(kap)


def integral(f):
    """Integral over kappa of samples f on the grid (trapezoid in ln kappa)."""
    g = f*kap
    return np.sum(0.5*(g[1:] + g[:-1])*np.diff(lnk))


def tail(f):
    """Integral of f from kap[n] to infinity, for every grid point n."""
    g = f*kap
    seg = 0.5*(g[1:] + g[:-1])*np.diff(lnk)
    return np.append(np.cumsum(seg[::-1])[::-1], 0.0)


def model(q, lL, beta):
    """Assumed spectrum: q^2 at low q, -5/3 in the middle, exp at high q."""
    x = q*lL
    return C*eps**(2/3)*q**(-5/3)*(x*x/(1 + x*x))**(11/6)*np.exp(-beta*q*eta)


def calibrate():
    """Choose lL and beta so that the integrals of E give k and eps."""
    lL, beta = ell0, 5.0
    for _ in range(20):
        lo, hi = 1e-4, 10.0                       # energy fixes lL
        for _ in range(60):
            lL = np.sqrt(lo*hi)
            low = integral(model(kap, lL, beta)) < k
            lo, hi = (lL, hi) if low else (lo, lL)
        lo, hi = 0.01, 50.0                       # dissipation fixes beta
        for _ in range(60):
            beta = 0.5*(lo + hi)
            high = integral(2*nu*kap**2*model(kap, lL, beta)) > eps
            lo, hi = (beta, hi) if high else (lo, beta)
    return lL, beta


lL, beta = calibrate()
E = model(kap, lL, beta)
print(f"eta={eta*1e6:.1f} um  lL={lL*1e3:.2f} mm  beta={beta:.3f}")
print(f"k={integral(E):.4f} m^2/s^2  "
      f"eps={integral(2*nu*kap**2*E):.3f} m^2/s^3")

# One-dimensional spectra of isotropic turbulence from E (kappa_1 = kap).
I1, I3 = tail(E/kap), tail(E/kap**3)
E11 = I1 - kap**2*I3
E22 = 0.5*(I1 + kap**2*I3)
u1sq = 2*k/3
print(f"int E11={integral(E11):.4f}  int E22={integral(E22):.4f}  "
      f"(2k/3={u1sq:.4f})")
assert np.all(np.diff(E11) <= 1e-12*E11[0])     # E11 never increases
n = np.argmax(E22)                               # E22 does, near zero
print(f"E22(0)/E11(0)={E22[0]/E11[0]:.4f}  max E22/E22(0)={E22[n]/E22[0]:.4f}"
      f" at kappa_1={kap[n]:.1f}/m")
L11 = np.pi*E11[0]/(2*u1sq)
print(f"L11=pi E11(0)/(2<u1^2>)={L11*1e3:.1f} mm  "
      f"(3 pi/4k) int E/kappa={3*np.pi/(4*k)*integral(E/kap)*1e3:.1f} mm")

# Inversion E = (1/2) kappa^3 d/dkappa [kappa^-1 dE11/dkappa], by differences.
band = (kap*eta > 1e-3) & (kap*eta < 1)
Einv = 0.5*kap**3*np.gradient(np.gradient(E11, kap)/kap, kap)
err = np.max(np.abs(Einv[band]/E[band] - 1))
print(f"inversion: max relative error {err:.1e} for 1e-3 < kappa eta < 1")

# Ratios at equal wavenumber; an isotropic power law p = 5/3 gives 55/18, 4/3.
slope = np.gradient(np.log(E11[band]), lnk[band])
for ke in (1e-3, 3e-3, 1e-2, 3e-2, 1e-1):
    n = np.argmin(np.abs(kap*eta - ke))
    m = np.argmin(np.abs(kap[band]*eta - ke))
    print(f"kappa eta={ke:.0e}: E/E11={E[n]/E11[n]:.3f}  "
          f"E22/E11={E22[n]/E11[n]:.3f}  slope of E11={slope[m]:+.3f}")
print(f"55/18={55/18:.4f}  4/3={4/3:.4f}")

# Dissipation inferred from the compensated E11 at its maximum.
comp = E11*kap**(5/3)
n = np.argmax(comp)
for name, c in (("C1", C1), ("C ", C)):
    print(f"eps from E11 at {kap[n]:.0f}/m (kappa eta={kap[n]*eta:.4f}) with "
          f"{name}={c:.3f}: {(comp[n]/c)**1.5:.1f} m^2/s^3")
print(f"(55/18)^(3/2)={(55/18)**1.5:.3f}")

# Final period of decay: every mode decays as exp(-2 nu kappa^2 t).
ts = np.logspace(3, 7, 9)
ks = np.array([integral(E*np.exp(-2*nu*kap**2*t)) for t in ts])
print("late-time slope d ln k/d ln t =",
      f"{np.log(ks[-1]/ks[-2])/np.log(ts[-1]/ts[-2]):+.3f}")

# A periodic box of side L: the lattice sum of (1/2) R_hat_ii
# = kappa0^3 E/(4 pi kappa^2), plus the integral above the lattice cut,
# against k. r3[m] counts the integer vectors n with |n|^2 = m.
M = 320
r2 = np.zeros(M*M + 1)
r3 = np.zeros(M*M + 1)
for i in range(-M, M + 1):
    for j in range(-M, M + 1):
        if i*i + j*j <= M*M:
            r2[i*i + j*j] += 1
for i in range(-M, M + 1):
    r3[i*i:] += r2[:M*M + 1 - i*i]
kp = np.sqrt(1.2)/lL                    # peak of E without the exponential
for L in (0.25, 1.0, 4.0):
    k0 = 2*np.pi/L
    q = k0*np.sqrt(np.arange(1, M*M + 1))
    lattice = np.sum(r3[1:]*k0**3*model(q, lL, beta)/(4*np.pi*q**2))
    above = integral(np.where(kap > q[-1], E, 0.0))
    print(f"L={L:4.2f} m  kappa0={k0:6.2f}/m  lattice + tail="
          f"{lattice + above:.4f} m^2/s^2  (k={k:.0f})")
    print(f"           lattice vectors with |kappa| <= kappa_p: "
          f"{int(r3[1:int((kp/k0)**2) + 1].sum())}")
for n in (1, 2, 5):                     # shell n-1/2 <= |n| < n+1/2
    N = int(r3[int(np.ceil((n - 0.5)**2)):int(np.ceil((n + 0.5)**2))].sum())
    print(f"shell n={n}: {N} lattice vectors, relative scatter "
          f"N^-1/2={N**-0.5:.3f}")

What the relations establish and what they do not

The chapter's results are of three kinds. Exact for the model: the uncorrelatedness of coefficients at different wavenumbers under homogeneity, (13.6)–(13.8), and the definitions (13.11), (13.13), (13.17), which need homogeneity only. Exact under isotropy and incompressibility: the form (13.12) of \(\Phi_{ij}\), the relations (13.14)–(13.16) and (13.18)–(13.19) between the one-dimensional and the three-dimensional spectra, and the constants (13.20) for an infinite power law. Approximate: the box limit (13.10), with an error that the lattice sums measure (0.7 % at \(\mathcal L=1\ \mathrm m\) for the jet), the constants (13.20) over a finite inertial range, and the final-period law (13.4), which assumes a negligible nonlinear term.

The isotropic relations are not tests of isotropy by themselves. A measured pair \(E_{11}\), \(E_{22}\) that satisfies (13.18) is consistent with isotropy at those scales; one that does not shows its absence. Chapter 11 reports Saddoughi's boundary-layer measurements, in which the transverse spectra show the predicted ratio \(E_{22}/E_{11}=\tfrac43\) at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\approx1500\) and deviate from it at 600. Inverting a measured \(E_{11}\) by (13.16) needs two derivatives of data and amplifies noise, and in a shear flow it gives a function whose meaning depends on how far the scales concerned are from isotropy.

The constant values used here are the ones discussed in chapter 11: \(C=1.5\) is one of several reported values, with a spread between sources of 10 to \(20\ \%\), and the conversion to \(C_1\) assumes isotropy. The test spectrum is assumed: its low-wavenumber exponent 2, its exponential decay and the lengths \(\ell_L\) and \(\beta\) are choices that give the right \(k\) and \(\varepsilon\), and none of the program's numbers is a prediction for a measured jet. The model spectrum that the course uses, and the shapes of measured spectra in the dissipation range, are the subject of the chapter "Kolmogorov, model, and dissipation spectra"; the balance that the triads of (13.1) produce for \(E(\kappa)\) is the subject of the chapter "Reynolds-number effects and the energy-spectrum balance".

Exercises

Exercise 1 (compute and design). A direct simulation of the jet station is to be run in a periodic box. (a) Show that the test spectrum, without its exponential factor, has its maximum at \(\kappa_p\ell_L=(6/5)^{1/2}\), and evaluate \(\kappa_p\) for \(\ell_L=75.86\ \mathrm{mm}\). (b) For which box side does \(\kappa_0\) equal \(\kappa_p\)? (c) Estimate the number of lattice vectors with \(|\boldsymbol\kappa|\le\kappa_p\) for \(\mathcal L=0.25\), 1 and 4 m, and use the program's lattice sums to state a criterion for \(\mathcal L\) in terms of \(\kappa_p\).

Solution

(a) Without the exponential factor, \(\ln E=\text{const}-\tfrac53\ln\kappa+\tfrac{11}6[2\ln x-\ln(1+x^2)]\) with \(x=\kappa\ell_L\). The derivative with respect to \(\ln\kappa\) is \(-\tfrac53+\tfrac{11}3/(1+x^2)\), which vanishes at \(1+x^2=\tfrac{11}5\), so \(x=(6/5)^{1/2}=1.095\) and \(\kappa_p=1.095/0.07586=14.4\ \mathrm{rad/m}\). The exponential factor changes this by a relative amount of order \(\beta\kappa_p\eta=2.093\times14.4\times7.9\times10^{-5}=2.4\times10^{-3}\).

(b) \(\kappa_0=2\pi/\mathcal L=\kappa_p\) for \(\mathcal L=2\pi/14.4=0.44\ \mathrm m\).

(c) The number of lattice vectors in a ball of radius \(\kappa_p\) is about \(\tfrac43\pi(\kappa_p/\kappa_0)^3\): 0.8 for \(\mathcal L=0.25\ \mathrm m\) (\(\kappa_p/\kappa_0=0.57\); in fact none, since the shortest nonzero lattice vector has length \(\kappa_0\gt\kappa_p\)), 51 for 1 m (\(\kappa_p/\kappa_0=2.3\); the output block of the program counts 56) and \(3.3\times10^3\) for 4 m (\(\kappa_p/\kappa_0=9.2\); the output block of the program counts 3238). The lattice sums are 22 %, 0.7 % and 0.01 % below \(k\). A criterion consistent with these numbers: \(\kappa_0\) at least a factor of about 2 below \(\kappa_p\) for an error below 1 % in \(k\), that is \(\mathcal L\gtrsim4\pi/\kappa_p=0.87\ \mathrm m\) for the jet. The criterion is for the energy only; statistics of the largest scales, such as \(L_{11}\), need larger boxes, because they are integrals with weight \(1/\kappa\) (13.17).

Exercise 2 (derive). For isotropic turbulence, use (13.14) and (13.18) to show (a) \(\int_0^\infty E_{22}\,d\kappa_1=\tfrac23k\), and (b) \(15\nu\int_0^\infty\kappa_1^2E_{11}\,d\kappa_1=\varepsilon\). (c) Which assumption of (b) does a dissipation estimate from a measured \(E_{11}\) in a shear flow rely on?

Solution

(a) Exchange the order of integration in (13.18): \(\int_0^\infty E_{22}\,d\kappa_1=\tfrac12\int_0^\infty\frac{E}{\kappa}\int_0^\kappa\Bigl(1+\frac{\kappa_1^2}{\kappa^2}\Bigr)d\kappa_1\,d\kappa=\tfrac12\int_0^\infty\frac E\kappa\cdot\frac43\kappa\,d\kappa=\tfrac23k\). This equals \(\langle u_2^2\rangle\) in isotropic turbulence, as the definition (13.13) requires.

(b) \(\int_0^\infty\kappa_1^2E_{11}\,d\kappa_1=\int_0^\infty\frac E\kappa\int_0^\kappa\Bigl(\kappa_1^2-\frac{\kappa_1^4}{\kappa^2}\Bigr)d\kappa_1\,d\kappa=\int_0^\infty\frac E\kappa\Bigl(\frac{\kappa^3}3-\frac{\kappa^3}5\Bigr)d\kappa=\frac2{15}\int_0^\infty\kappa^2E\,d\kappa\). Multiplying by \(15\nu\) gives \(2\nu\int\kappa^2E\,d\kappa=\varepsilon\). With the cosine pair of (13.13), \(\int_0^\infty\kappa_1^2E_{11}\,d\kappa_1=-d^2R_{11}(r\mathbf e_1)/dr^2|_{0}=\langle(\partial u_1/\partial x_1)^2\rangle\), so (b) is chapter 11's \(\varepsilon=15\nu\langle(\partial u_1/\partial x_1)^2\rangle\), (11.24), obtained here from the spectra.

(c) Isotropy of the dissipating scales: (13.14) was derived from (13.12). Saddoughi's dissipation estimate, discussed in chapter 11, uses this relation and is therefore conditional on local isotropy at the scales that dominate \(\kappa_1^2E_{11}\).

Exercise 3 (compute and find a counterexample). Take \(E(\kappa)=A\kappa^4e^{-\kappa^2/q^2}\), with constants \(A\) and \(q\). (a) Compute \(E_{11}\) and \(E_{22}\) from (13.14) and (13.18) in closed form. (b) Compute \(k\), \(\langle u_1^2\rangle\) and \(L_{11}\). (c) Find the maximum of \(E_{22}\) and its ratio to \(E_{22}(0)\). (d) Find \(f(r)\) and check (13.22).

Solution

(a) With \(s=\kappa^2\), \(I_1=\int_{\kappa_1}^\infty A\kappa^3e^{-\kappa^2/q^2}d\kappa=\tfrac12Aq^2(\kappa_1^2+q^2)e^{-\kappa_1^2/q^2}\) and \(I_3=\int_{\kappa_1}^\infty A\kappa e^{-\kappa^2/q^2}d\kappa=\tfrac12Aq^2e^{-\kappa_1^2/q^2}\). Then \(E_{11}=I_1-\kappa_1^2I_3=\tfrac12Aq^4e^{-\kappa_1^2/q^2}\) and \(E_{22}=\tfrac12(I_1+\kappa_1^2I_3)=\tfrac14Aq^2(q^2+2\kappa_1^2)e^{-\kappa_1^2/q^2}\). Check with the first form of (13.18): \(dE_{11}/d\kappa_1=-(2\kappa_1/q^2)E_{11}\), so \(\tfrac12(E_{11}-\kappa_1E_{11}')=\tfrac12E_{11}(1+2\kappa_1^2/q^2)\), the same.

(b) \(k=A\int_0^\infty\kappa^4e^{-\kappa^2/q^2}d\kappa=\tfrac38\sqrt\pi\,Aq^5\). \(\langle u_1^2\rangle=\int_0^\infty E_{11}\,d\kappa_1=\tfrac12Aq^4\cdot\tfrac12\sqrt\pi\,q=\tfrac14\sqrt\pi\,Aq^5=\tfrac23k\). By (13.17), \(L_{11}=\pi\cdot\tfrac12Aq^4/(2\cdot\tfrac14\sqrt\pi\,Aq^5)=\sqrt\pi/q\).

(c) \(dE_{22}/d\kappa_1=\tfrac14Aq^2e^{-\kappa_1^2/q^2}\kappa_1(2-4\kappa_1^2/q^2)\), zero at \(\kappa_1=q/\sqrt2\). There \(E_{22}=\tfrac14Aq^2\cdot2q^2e^{-1/2}=\tfrac12Aq^4e^{-1/2}\), and \(E_{22}(0)=\tfrac14Aq^4\); the ratio is \(2e^{-1/2}=1.213\). The transverse spectrum rises 21 % above its value at zero, which is a counterexample to "the 1D spectra are maximum at zero wavenumber". \(E_{11}\) is a Gaussian and decreases monotonically, as (13.15) requires.

(d) By the cosine pair, \(R_{11}(r\mathbf e_1)=\int_0^\infty\tfrac12Aq^4e^{-\kappa_1^2/q^2}\cos(\kappa_1r)\,d\kappa_1=\tfrac14\sqrt\pi\,Aq^5e^{-q^2r^2/4}\), so \(f(r)=e^{-q^2r^2/4}\), whose integral is \(\sqrt\pi/q=L_{11}\), as in (b). Since \(r^3f\to0\), (13.22) gives zero, and \(E\) must vanish faster than \(\kappa^2\): it vanishes as \(\kappa^4\).

Exercise 4 (compute and check an assumption). A hot-wire in grid turbulence in air (\(\nu=1.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\)) gives, at \(\kappa_1=200\ \mathrm{rad/m}\) inside an apparent \(-5/3\) range, \(E_{11}=2.0\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3/s^2}\) and \(E_{22}=2.2\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^3/s^2}\) (assume both are wavenumber spectra; the conversion from frequency belongs to a later chapter). (a) Estimate \(\varepsilon\) with \(C_1=0.491\) and with \(C=1.5\). (b) Compute \(\kappa_1\eta\). (c) What does the measured \(E_{22}/E_{11}\) say about the first estimate?

Solution

(a) \(E_{11}\kappa_1^{5/3}=2.0\times10^{-3}\times200^{5/3}=2.0\times10^{-3}\times6.84\times10^{3}=13.7\ \mathrm{m^{4/3}/s^2}\). With \(C_1\): \(\varepsilon=(13.7/0.491)^{3/2}=147\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\). With \(C\): \((13.7/1.5)^{3/2}=27.5\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\), smaller by \(5.34\).

(c) Isotropy and a \(-5/3\) law predict \(E_{22}/E_{11}=\tfrac43\); the measured ratio is 1.10. At this wavenumber the turbulence is not isotropic, or the range is not an inertial range, or both. Either way the conversion \(C_1=\tfrac{18}{55}C\) and the estimate of (a) rest on an assumption that the same data contradict; the estimate with \(C_1\) should be reported as conditional on local isotropy, with the uncertainty of \(C\) raised to the power \(\tfrac32\) (about 30 % for a 20 % spread in the constant).

Exercise 5 (derive and check an assumption). (a) In the final period of decay, take \(E(\kappa,0)=A\kappa^se^{-\kappa^2/q^2}\), with constants \(A\), \(q\) and \(s\gt-1\). Show that \(k(t)=k(0)(1+2\nu q^2t)^{-(s+1)/2}\) exactly under (13.2), and give \(\varepsilon(0)\). (b) For \(q=100\ \mathrm{rad/m}\), \(\nu=1.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) and an r.m.s. velocity \(u'=1\ \mathrm{mm/s}\), compute \(u'/(\nu q)\) and \(1/(2\nu q^2)\). Is the use of (13.2) justified?

Solution

(a) By (13.3), \(k(t)=A\int_0^\infty\kappa^se^{-\kappa^2(q^{-2}+2\nu t)}d\kappa=\tfrac12A\Gamma(\tfrac{s+1}2)(q^{-2}+2\nu t)^{-(s+1)/2}\), using \(\int_0^\infty\kappa^se^{-a\kappa^2}d\kappa=\tfrac12\Gamma(\tfrac{s+1}2)a^{-(s+1)/2}\). Dividing by the value at \(t=0\) gives \((1+2\nu q^2t)^{-(s+1)/2}\). For large \(t\) this is (13.4). Then \(\varepsilon=-dk/dt=(s+1)\nu q^2k(0)(1+2\nu q^2t)^{-(s+3)/2}\), so \(\varepsilon(0)=(s+1)\nu q^2k(0)\); the same value follows from \(2\nu\int\kappa^2E\,d\kappa\), since \(\int\kappa^{s+2}e^{-\kappa^2/q^2}d\kappa/\int\kappa^se^{-\kappa^2/q^2}d\kappa=\tfrac{s+1}2q^2\).

(b) \(u'/(\nu q)=10^{-3}/(1.5\times10^{-5}\times100)=0.67\), and \(1/(2\nu q^2)=3.3\ \mathrm s\). The ratio of the nonlinear to the viscous term at the energy-containing wavenumber is of order \(u'/(\nu q)\), here 0.67, which is not small: the nonlinear term is still comparable to the viscous one, and (13.2) is not yet justified. The final period requires this Reynolds number to be well below 1; under (a) it decreases as \(k^{1/2}\) multiplied by the growing length \((q^{-2}+2\nu t)^{1/2}\), that is as \((1+2\nu q^2t)^{-(s+1)/4+1/2}\), which decreases only for \(s\gt1\).

Exercise 6 (find a counterexample). A student states: "if \(E(\kappa)\) follows the \(-5/3\) law on an interval, then \(E_{11}(\kappa_1)\) follows the \(-5/3\) law with \(C_1=\tfrac{18}{55}C\) on the same interval." Take \(E=C\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3}\) for \(\kappa\le\kappa_b\) and \(E=0\) above. Compute \(E_{11}\) for \(\kappa_1\lt\kappa_b\) and its ratio to \(C_1\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa_1^{-5/3}\) at \(\kappa_1/\kappa_b=0.1\), 0.3 and 0.5. (Ignore the behaviour of this \(E\) at small \(\kappa\); only \(\kappa\ge\kappa_1\) enters.)

Solution

With \(B=C\varepsilon^{2/3}\) and \(r=\kappa_1/\kappa_b\), (13.14) gives \(E_{11}=B\int_{\kappa_1}^{\kappa_b}(\kappa^{-8/3}-\kappa_1^2\kappa^{-14/3})\,d\kappa=B\bigl[\tfrac35(\kappa_1^{-5/3}-\kappa_b^{-5/3})-\tfrac3{11}\kappa_1^2(\kappa_1^{-11/3}-\kappa_b^{-11/3})\bigr]\). The terms in \(\kappa_1^{-5/3}\) add to \((\tfrac35-\tfrac3{11})B\kappa_1^{-5/3}=\tfrac{18}{55}B\kappa_1^{-5/3}=C_1\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa_1^{-5/3}\), the student's law. The remainder gives the ratio \(1-\tfrac{55}{18}\bigl(\tfrac35r^{5/3}-\tfrac3{11}r^{11/3}\bigr)\): 0.961 at \(r=0.1\), 0.764 at \(r=0.3\) and 0.488 at \(r=0.5\). The statement holds only where \(\kappa_1\ll\kappa_b\); within a factor of 10 of the end of the power law, \(E_{11}\) is more than 4 % below it, because the line measurement at \(\kappa_1\) collects energy from wavenumbers up to infinity and the energy beyond \(\kappa_b\) is missing. The opposite implication, from \(E_{11}\) to \(E\), holds on the same interval, because (13.16) is local.

Exercise 7 (derive; adapted from the course practice problems, T2 16:29). In the periodic box, write \(u_i(\mathbf x)=\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa'}e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa'\cdot\mathbf x}\hat u_i(\boldsymbol\kappa')=\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa'}e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa'\cdot\mathbf x}\hat u_i^*(\boldsymbol\kappa')\) and \(u_j(\mathbf x+\mathbf r)=\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa}e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot(\mathbf x+\mathbf r)}\hat u_j(\boldsymbol\kappa)\). Show that, in homogeneous turbulence, \(R_{ij}(\mathbf r)=\langle\langle u_i(\mathbf x)u_j(\mathbf x+\mathbf r)\rangle_V\rangle=\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa}e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}\langle\hat u_i^*(\boldsymbol\kappa)\hat u_j(\boldsymbol\kappa)\rangle\). Compare the route with the proof of (13.7).

Solution

Homogeneity makes \(R_{ij}(\mathbf r)=\langle u_i(\mathbf x)u_j(\mathbf x+\mathbf r)\rangle\) independent of \(\mathbf x\), so it equals its own box average over \(\mathbf x\), and the box average and the expectation commute: \(R_{ij}(\mathbf r)=\langle\langle u_i(\mathbf x)u_j(\mathbf x+\mathbf r)\rangle_V\rangle\). (The tutorial slide writes \(\langle R_{ij}(\mathbf r)\rangle_\ell\), which defines \(R_{ij}\) through itself; the product of velocities is meant.) Insert the second form of \(u_i\) and the series of \(u_j\): \(\langle u_iu_j\rangle_V=\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa'}\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa}\hat u_i^*(\boldsymbol\kappa')\hat u_j(\boldsymbol\kappa)e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}\langle e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa'\cdot\mathbf x}\rangle_V\). By orthonormality the last average is \(\delta_{\boldsymbol\kappa,\boldsymbol\kappa'}\), which leaves \(\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa}\hat u_i^*(\boldsymbol\kappa)\hat u_j(\boldsymbol\kappa)e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}\) for each realization; the expectation gives the result, which is (13.7) with (13.6).

The two routes use homogeneity differently. Here it is used once, to replace \(R_{ij}\) by its box average, and orthonormality removes the pairs \(\boldsymbol\kappa'\ne\boldsymbol\kappa\) in every realization. The proof of (13.7) uses homogeneity for every \(\mathbf x\) and obtains more: the covariance of coefficients at \(\boldsymbol\kappa'\ne-\boldsymbol\kappa\) is zero, a statement about the statistics of the coefficients that the box-average route does not make.

Exercise 8 (derive; adapted from the course practice problems, L59 14:46 and T2 24:30; the two recordings pose the same three parts). With \(\hat R_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)=\langle\hat u_i^*(\boldsymbol\kappa)\hat u_j(\boldsymbol\kappa)\rangle=\langle\hat u_i(-\boldsymbol\kappa)\hat u_j(\boldsymbol\kappa)\rangle\), show: (1) \(\hat R_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)\ge0\) for \(i=j\), and \(\hat R_{ii}(\boldsymbol\kappa)\ge0\) (summed); (2) from conjugate symmetry, \(\hat R_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)=\hat R_{ji}(-\boldsymbol\kappa)=\hat R_{ji}^*(\boldsymbol\kappa)\); (3) from the incompressibility condition \(\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\hat{\mathbf u}(\boldsymbol\kappa)=0\), \(\kappa_i\hat R_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)=\kappa_j\hat R_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)=0\). The slides note that the same properties apply to \(\Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)\).

Solution

(1) For fixed \(i\) (no summation), \(\hat u_i^*\hat u_i=|\hat u_i|^2\ge0\) in every realization, so its expectation \(\hat R_{ii}\) is real and non-negative. The sum over \(i\) of non-negative numbers is non-negative.

(2) Replace \(\boldsymbol\kappa\) by \(-\boldsymbol\kappa\) in the second form of the definition with the indices exchanged: \(\hat R_{ji}(-\boldsymbol\kappa)=\langle\hat u_j(\boldsymbol\kappa)\hat u_i(-\boldsymbol\kappa)\rangle=\hat R_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)\), because the two factors commute. For the second equality, \(\hat R_{ji}^*(\boldsymbol\kappa)=\langle(\hat u_j^*(\boldsymbol\kappa)\hat u_i(\boldsymbol\kappa))^*\rangle=\langle\hat u_j(\boldsymbol\kappa)\hat u_i^*(\boldsymbol\kappa)\rangle=\hat R_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)\): the expectation commutes with complex conjugation. The matrix \(\hat R_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)\) is Hermitian. (The recording states at L59 24:30 that conjugate symmetry was \(R_{ij}(-\boldsymbol\kappa)=R_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)\); the board has the correct form with the indices exchanged.)

(3) \(\kappa_j\hat R_{ij}=\langle\hat u_i^*(\boldsymbol\kappa)\,\kappa_j\hat u_j(\boldsymbol\kappa)\rangle=0\), because \(\kappa_j\hat u_j=0\) in every realization and \(\boldsymbol\kappa\) is not random. Similarly \(\kappa_i\hat R_{ij}=\langle(\kappa_i\hat u_i(\boldsymbol\kappa))^*\hat u_j(\boldsymbol\kappa)\rangle=0\), since \(\boldsymbol\kappa\) is real. The recorded solutions change variable from \(\boldsymbol\kappa\) to \(-\boldsymbol\kappa\) for the first of the two; the step is not needed. (The tutorial slide writes the wavenumber components as \(k_i\), \(k_j\) in this part.) For \(\Phi_{ij}\) the three properties follow through the limit (13.10), \(\Phi_{ij}=\lim\hat R_{ij}/\kappa_0^3\), since \(\kappa_0^3\gt0\); part (3) is the incompressibility condition used in the proof of (13.12).
