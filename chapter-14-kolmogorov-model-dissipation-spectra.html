Kolmogorov, model and dissipation spectra: the full shape of the energy spectrum, and where the energy and the dissipation lie

A hot wire in the air jet of chapters 6–8 has a spatial resolution of about 1 mm. The inertial-range law \(E(\kappa)=C\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3}\) of chapter 11 cannot say how much of the dissipation rate such a probe misses, because it describes neither the scales near the Kolmogorov length nor the scales near the size of the jet. A complete spectrum can. This chapter builds one: it scales the spectrum with the Kolmogorov variables, writes the inertial subrange as the limit in which the scaling function becomes constant, and joins the three ranges in a model spectrum with three factors whose constants are fixed by the energy and the dissipation integrals. For the jet, treated as homogeneous isotropic turbulence at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=453\), the model puts 99.8 % of the kinetic energy at scales above 1 mm and only 72.6 % of the dissipation: 27 % of the dissipation lies at smaller scales. It also puts 80 % of the dissipation between 0.69 mm and 5.1 mm (8.7 to 64 Kolmogorov lengths), which is where the lecture's nominal demarcation length \(60\eta\) comes from, and 80 % of the energy between 15 mm and 0.51 m. The chapter derives each constant that the model needs, checks the lecture's tables by recomputing them, and says which statements are hypotheses, which are fits and which are definitions.

The objects of the chapter and the running case

The formal model is a statistically homogeneous velocity fluctuation field \(\mathbf u=\mathbf U-\langle\mathbf U\rangle\) of an incompressible flow with constant kinematic viscosity \(\nu\) (units \(\mathrm{m^2/s}\)), as in chapter 11. Its energy-spectrum function \(E(\kappa)\) (units \(\mathrm{m^3/s^2}\), always written with its argument) satisfies \(k=\int_0^\infty E\,d\kappa\) for the turbulent kinetic energy per unit mass \(k=\tfrac12\langle u_iu_i\rangle\) (chapter 8, (8.33)) and \(\varepsilon=\int_0^\infty2\nu\kappa^2E\,d\kappa\) for the dissipation rate \(\varepsilon\) (chapter 11, (11.3) and its derivation, which needs homogeneity and incompressibility). Isotropy is assumed only where a statement says so; the one- and three-dimensional relations below need it. The Kolmogorov scales are \(\eta=(\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}\), \(u_\eta=(\varepsilon\nu)^{1/4}\) and \(\tau_\eta=(\nu/\varepsilon)^{1/2}\) (chapter 5, section on the Kolmogorov scales, (5.14)); the large-scale length is \(L=k^{3/2}/\varepsilon\); the longitudinal integral scale \(L_{11}\) is that of chapter 8, (8.28); \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=u'\lambda/\nu\) uses the Taylor microscale \(\lambda\) and \(u'=(2k/3)^{1/2}\); and the isotropic identities of chapter 11 (section on the large-scale, Taylor-scale and Kolmogorov scales) give \(\mathrm{Re}_L\equiv k^2/(\varepsilon\nu)=\tfrac{3}{20}\mathrm{Re}_\lambda^2\) and \(L/\eta=\mathrm{Re}_L^{3/4}\). The wavenumber \(\kappa\) belongs to an eddy size \(\ell\) by the slide's convention \(\kappa=2\pi/\ell\) (chapter 11, (11.1)). The nominal limits of the three ranges, \(\ell_{EI}=\ell_0/6\) and \(\ell_{DI}=60\eta\), and the hypotheses (local isotropy, first and second similarity) are those of chapter 10, section on the three ranges, and of chapter 11. The first section below recalls what is needed. The Fourier-mode structure behind \(E(\kappa)\), and the relation between the one-dimensional spectra and \(E(\kappa)\), are the subject of the chapters "Fourier modes and their evolution" and "Spectral energy: Fourier modes, the velocity spectrum tensor, and one-dimensional spectra"; this chapter uses the isotropic relation as given and checks it numerically.

The running case. The running case is the round air jet of chapters 6–8 and 11 with the values of chapter 11: \(u_0=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(\ell_0=0.1\ \mathrm m\), \(\nu=1.4607\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), \(\varepsilon=u_0^3/\ell_0=80\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\) (the estimate of chapter 5, (5.16), with unit constant), and \(k=\tfrac32u_0^2=6\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\). These give \(\eta=79.0\ \mu\mathrm m\), \(L=k^{3/2}/\varepsilon=0.1837\ \mathrm m\), \(L/\eta=2325\), and \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=453\). The jet is neither homogeneous nor known to be isotropic. Every number below for the jet is what the formulas give if its downstream station behaved as unbounded homogeneous isotropic turbulence with these values of \(k\), \(\varepsilon\) and \(\nu\); the numbers illustrate the formulas and are not predictions for a particular jet. The question for the jet is the one of the lead paragraph: where in scale do its energy and its dissipation lie, so that a probe or a grid of finite resolution can be judged.

Two ways of scaling the spectrum L37 7:12

The first similarity hypothesis (chapter 10, section "Transfer, dissipation and the first similarity hypothesis") states that for \(\kappa\gt\kappa_{EI}\), the universal equilibrium range, the statistics of the velocity are determined uniquely by \(\varepsilon\) and \(\nu\) at sufficiently high Reynolds number. For the spectrum this means that \(E\) depends on \(\kappa\), \(\varepsilon\) and \(\nu\) only. These three quantities contain two independent dimensions, length and time, so they form one dimensionless group, which is taken as \(x\equiv\kappa\eta\). The quantity \((\varepsilon\nu^5)^{1/4}\) has the units of \(E\), \((\mathrm{m^2s^{-3}}\cdot\mathrm{m^{10}s^{-5}})^{1/4}=\mathrm{m^3s^{-2}}\), and equals \(u_\eta^2\eta\) because \(u_\eta^2\eta=(\varepsilon\nu)^{1/2}\nu^{3/4}\varepsilon^{-1/4}=\varepsilon^{1/4}\nu^{5/4}\). Therefore

\[ E(\kappa)=(\varepsilon\nu^5)^{1/4}\varphi(\kappa\eta)=u_\eta^2\,\eta\,\varphi(\kappa\eta),\qquad\kappa\gt\kappa_{EI}, \tag{14.1} \]

where \(\varphi\) is a universal dimensionless function, the Kolmogorov spectrum function; hypothesis, not dimensional analysis, makes it the same in every flow, and the dimensional analysis alone does not supply its form. The slides of this module write \(\varphi\) here and \(\Psi\) below; chapter 11 wrote \(\Psi(\kappa\eta)\) for the function that is called \(\varphi\) here, and this chapter keeps the slides' symbols.

A second scaling uses \(\varepsilon\) and \(\kappa\) as the two basic quantities. Writing \(E=\varepsilon^a\kappa^b\) and equating dimensions gives \(-3a=-2\) from the time and \(2a-b=3\) from the length, so \(a=\tfrac23\) and \(b=-\tfrac53\), and the solution is unique because \(\varepsilon\) and \(\kappa\) have independent dimensions. The remaining factor is dimensionless and can depend only on the dimensionless group, which still involves \(\nu\) through \(\eta\):

\[ E(\kappa)=\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3}\,\Psi(\kappa\eta),\qquad\kappa\gt\kappa_{EI}. \tag{14.2} \]

Both forms hold in the same range, so they describe the same function and their scaling functions are related. Divide (14.1) by \(\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3}\) and use \(\varepsilon=u_\eta^3/\eta\), which follows from \(\varepsilon=\nu u_\eta^2/\eta^2\) (chapter 10, (10.12)) and \(\nu=u_\eta\eta\). Then \(\varepsilon^{-2/3}=\eta^{2/3}u_\eta^{-2}\), so

\[ \Psi(\kappa\eta)=u_\eta^2\eta\;\eta^{2/3}u_\eta^{-2}\,\kappa^{5/3}\varphi(\kappa\eta)=(\kappa\eta)^{5/3}\varphi(\kappa\eta),\qquad\kappa\gt\kappa_{EI}. \tag{14.3} \]

This is the slide's relation. The lecture reaches it by the same cancellation of powers of \(u_\eta\) and \(\eta\). Equation (14.3) is an identity between two ways of writing one function; it contains no new physics, and it does not say yet whether \(\Psi\) is constant.

The ranges in terms of \(\kappa\eta\). The second similarity hypothesis applies in the inertial subrange, \(\eta\ll\ell\ll\ell_0\) or \(\ell_{DI}\lt\ell\lt\ell_{EI}\), that is \(\kappa_{EI}\lt\kappa\lt\kappa_{DI}\) (Kolmogorov (1941), p. 12, states the hypothesis for the distributions of velocity differences at separations large compared with the viscous scale: they are determined by \(\varepsilon\) alone and do not depend on \(\nu\)). Multiplying by \(\eta\), the slide writes the range as

\[ \kappa_{EI}\eta=\frac{2\pi\eta}{\ell_{EI}}\lt\kappa\eta\lt\frac{2\pi\eta}{\ell_{DI}}=\kappa_{DI}\eta,\qquad\text{and, as an order-of-magnitude statement,}\qquad 1\gg\kappa\eta\gg\frac{\eta}{\ell_0}. \tag{14.4} \]

With the nominal factors, \(\kappa_{DI}\eta=2\pi/60=0.105\) and \(\kappa_{EI}\eta=12\pi\,\eta/\ell_0=37.7\,\eta/\ell_0\). The first inequality of (14.4) is a definition by the nominal limits. The second uses \(\eta/\ell_0\sim\mathrm{Re}_0^{-3/4}\) (chapter 5 and chapter 10, (10.15)); it is meaningful only when \(\mathrm{Re}_0\) is large enough that the interval is wide, and the section on the energy-containing range shows that the width of the interval at a given Reynolds number depends on how \(\ell_0\) and \(\ell_{EI}\) are defined. The lecture attaches the "only \(\varepsilon\)" form of the spectrum to the dissipation range at one point (L37, about 21 min, in the discussion of the schematic); the schematic on the slide (L37 19:00) and the second hypothesis place that form in the inertial subrange, as here, and the dissipation range is where \(\nu\) enters.

The inertial subrange: why the scaling function is constant, and the one-dimensional constants L38 1:16

Why \(\Psi\) is constant. In the inertial subrange the second hypothesis says that \(E\) does not depend on \(\nu\) at fixed \(\varepsilon\) and \(\kappa\). In (14.2) the viscosity enters only through \(\eta\propto\nu^{3/4}\). At fixed \(\kappa\) and \(\varepsilon\), the hypothesis makes \(E\) one number for every admissible \(\nu\). As \(\nu\) varies, \(\kappa\eta=\kappa\nu^{3/4}\varepsilon^{-1/4}\) sweeps an interval, so \(\Psi\) takes one value on that interval, which is the statement that \(\Psi\) is constant there; no differentiability is needed. In derivative form, assume \(\Psi\) is differentiable and differentiate (14.2) with respect to \(\nu\) at fixed \(\varepsilon\) and \(\kappa\): \(\partial E/\partial\nu=\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3}\Psi'(\kappa\eta)\,\kappa\,\partial\eta/\partial\nu\). The derivative \(\partial\eta/\partial\nu=\tfrac34\eta/\nu\) is not zero, so independence of \(\nu\) requires \(\Psi'=0\) over the interval of \(\kappa\eta\) that varying \(\nu\) sweeps through. In that interval \(\Psi\) is a constant, which the lecture writes \(C\) ("\(\Psi\) becomes independent of its argument"). Therefore

\[ E(\kappa)=C\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3},\qquad\kappa_{EI}\lt\kappa\lt\kappa_{DI}, \tag{14.5} \]

which is (11.7) of chapter 11, obtained there from the dimensional analysis; here it appears as the limit of (14.2). The argument shows that the exponent \(-\tfrac53\) and the form of the prefactor follow from the hypotheses and dimensional analysis, and it shows nothing about the value of \(C\). The constant \(C\) is empirical. The slide gives \(C=1.5\); chapter 11, section "The measured spectrum", collects the values: \(1.5\pm0.1\) in a boundary layer at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\approx600\); 1.62, which is the survey value \(C_1\approx0.53\pm0.055\) converted by \(C=\tfrac{55}{18}C_1\) and also the simulation value of Yeung and Zhou (1997, report pp. 1–2); and 1.83, which is their simulation value \(C_1\approx0.60\) converted. This chapter uses \(C=1.5\), as the slide does, and every constant derived from it inherits its spread, 1.5 to 1.83 across the values above (22 %), against \(\pm0.1\) (7 %) within one experiment. Restated with the two scalings, (14.3) and (14.5) say that in the inertial subrange \(\varphi(x)=Cx^{-5/3}\).

One-dimensional spectra. The slide lists the isotropic relations that connect the one-dimensional longitudinal and transverse spectra \(E_{11}(\kappa_1)\) and \(E_{22}(\kappa_1)\) (spectra of \(u_1\) and \(u_2\) along the direction \(x_1\) of the one-dimensional wavenumber \(\kappa_1\)) to \(E(\kappa)\):

\[ E_{11}(\kappa_1)=\int_{\kappa_1}^\infty\frac{E(\kappa)}{\kappa}\Bigl(1-\frac{\kappa_1^2}{\kappa^2}\Bigr)d\kappa,\qquad E_{22}(\kappa_1)=\tfrac12\Bigl(E_{11}-\kappa_1\frac{dE_{11}}{d\kappa_1}\Bigr), \tag{14.6} \]

and the inverse \(E(\kappa)=\tfrac12\kappa^3\,d[\kappa^{-1}dE_{11}/d\kappa]/d\kappa\) (the form used in chapter 11). The sign in front of \(\kappa_1^2/\kappa^2\) is not legible on the slide (L37 5:30); the minus sign is the one for which the inverse formula holds, as the power-law calculation below confirms, and for which the program below returns \(15\int\kappa_1^2E_{11}\,d\kappa_1=\varepsilon\) for the model, where \(\varepsilon=15\nu\int_0^\infty\kappa_1^2E_{11}\,d\kappa_1\) is the independent isotropic relation that Saddoughi (1993, p. 246) uses to define his dissipation spectra; with the plus sign the same integral would give \(4\varepsilon\). The relations hold for isotropic turbulence only. The mechanism is this: \(E_{11}(\kappa_1)\) collects the energy of all Fourier modes whose wavevector has first component \(\kappa_1\), that is an integral of the spectrum tensor over the plane \(\kappa_1=\) const; in isotropic turbulence the tensor depends on \(E(\kappa)\) alone, and the plane integral in polar coordinates gives the weight \(1-\kappa_1^2/\kappa^2\). The full derivation belongs to the chapter titled "Spectral energy: Fourier modes, the velocity spectrum tensor, and one-dimensional spectra"; this chapter uses them without derivation. Take a power law \(E(\kappa)=A\kappa^{-p}\) with \(1\lt p\lt3\) (the lecture's slide writes \(E_{11}=C_1A\kappa_1^{-p}\) and \(E=CA\kappa^{-p}\)). Then

\[ E_{11}(\kappa_1)=A\kappa_1^{-p}\Bigl(\frac1p-\frac1{p+2}\Bigr)=\frac{2A}{p(p+2)}\kappa_1^{-p},\qquad E_{22}(\kappa_1)=\frac{1+p}{2}E_{11}(\kappa_1), \tag{14.7} \]

because \(\int_{\kappa_1}^\infty\kappa^{-p-1}d\kappa=\kappa_1^{-p}/p\) and \(\int_{\kappa_1}^\infty\kappa^{-p-3}d\kappa=\kappa_1^{-p-2}/(p+2)\), and because \(-\kappa_1dE_{11}/d\kappa_1=pE_{11}\). In the notation of the slide, \(C=\tfrac12p(p+2)C_1\) and \(C_1'=\tfrac12(1+p)C_1\). For \(p=\tfrac53\) these are \(C=\tfrac{55}{18}C_1\) and \(C_1'=\tfrac43C_1\), so \(E_{11}=C_1\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa_1^{-5/3}\) and \(E_{22}=C_1'\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa_1^{-5/3}\) with

\[ C_1=\tfrac{18}{55}C=0.491,\qquad C_1'=\tfrac43C_1=0.655\qquad(C=1.5). \tag{14.8} \]

Both are consequences of (14.5), isotropy and the exact power \(-\tfrac53\); they are not independent constants. The ratio \(E_{22}/E_{11}=\tfrac43\) is a prediction that the measured spectra of shear flows, whose large scales are anisotropic, can test, and the section on measured inertial subranges below does so. Equation (14.7) with \(p\ne\tfrac53\) shows how sensitive the ratio is to the exponent.

The model spectrum L38 7:18

What a model spectrum is for. The hypotheses fix the spectrum in the inertial subrange. They give the shape in the dissipation range only as a universal function of \(\kappa\eta\) (14.1) and say that the energy-containing range depends on the flow. A model spectrum is a function constructed to have the right behaviour in all three ranges, so that integrals over the whole spectrum, such as the cumulative dissipation of the sections below, can be computed. It is an interpolation with fitted parameters; no equation of motion produces it. The slide's form is

\[ E(\kappa)=C\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3}\,f_L(\kappa L)\,f_\eta(\kappa\eta), \tag{14.9} \]

with the two nondimensional factors specified separately. The length \(L\) is not defined on the slides of this part of the lecture. It must be a large-scale length, and the value of \(c_L\) below depends on its definition; with \(L=k^{3/2}/\varepsilon\) the calibration below reproduces the lecture's \(c_L=6.78\), so this is the definition used.

\[ f_L(\kappa L)=\Bigl[\frac{\kappa L}{\bigl((\kappa L)^2+c_L\bigr)^{1/2}}\Bigr]^{5/3+p_0},\qquad f_\eta(\kappa\eta)=\exp\Bigl\{-\beta\Bigl[\bigl((\kappa\eta)^4+c_\eta^4\bigr)^{1/4}-c_\eta\Bigr]\Bigr\}. \tag{14.10} \]

The slide gives \(p_0=2\), \(\beta=5.2\) and, for high Reynolds number, \(c_L=6.78\) and \(c_\eta=0.40\); \(c_L\) and \(c_\eta\) are positive constants, and \(c_\eta=0\) gives the simple exponential \(f_\eta=\exp(-\beta\kappa\eta)\).

Behaviour of the factors. For \(\kappa L\gg\sqrt{c_L}\) the bracket of \(f_L\) tends to 1, so \(f_L\to1\); for \(\kappa L\ll\sqrt{c_L}\) it is \(\kappa L/\sqrt{c_L}\), so \(f_L\propto\kappa^{5/3+p_0}\) and \(E\propto\kappa^{-5/3}\kappa^{5/3+p_0}=\kappa^{p_0}\). With \(p_0=2\) the spectrum rises as \(\kappa^2\) at low wavenumber; the slide gives \(p_0=4\) as the alternative that makes it the von Kármán form with \(E\propto\kappa^4\). The lectures give no argument for either exponent, so \(p_0\) is a model input. The approach to 1 is slow: for \(c_L=6.78\), \(f_L=0.36\), 0.89 and 0.99 at \(\kappa L=3\), 10 and 30 (program below). For \(f_\eta\), the exponent has the limits \(-\beta\kappa\eta\) for \(\kappa\eta\gg c_\eta\) and \(-\beta(\kappa\eta)^4/(4c_\eta^3)\) for \(\kappa\eta\ll c_\eta\), so \(f_\eta\to1\) at small \(\kappa\eta\), and \(E\) decays faster than any power of \(\kappa\) at large \(\kappa\). The slide gives the reason that a velocity field which is infinitely differentiable has a spectrum that decays faster than any power; the exponential is the simplest such decay, and its use is an empirical choice for which the slide cites experiments. The simple exponential (\(c_\eta=0\)) departs from 1 too early: at \(\kappa\eta=0.1\), well inside the range where \(f_\eta\) should still be near 1, \(\exp(-5.2\kappa\eta)=0.60\), whereas the form with \(c_\eta=0.40\) gives 1.00 (program below). This is the slide's reason for the more complicated form.

The integral conditions that fix \(c_L\) and \(c_\eta\). For given \(k\), \(\varepsilon\) and \(\nu\), \(C\) and \(\beta\), the slide says that the constants \(c_L\) and \(c_\eta\) are fixed by requiring that \(E(\kappa)\) and \(2\nu\kappa^2E(\kappa)\) integrate to \(k\) and \(\varepsilon\). The two conditions can be written out in the limit of infinite Reynolds number, where the factors separate: the energy is carried at \(\kappa\eta\ll1\), where \(f_\eta=1\), and the dissipation at \(\kappa L\gg1\), where \(f_L=1\). For the energy set \(y\equiv\kappa L\), the wavenumber scaled by \(L\) instead of \(\eta\), and use \((\varepsilon L)^{2/3}=k\):

\[ 1=C\int_0^\infty y^{-5/3}f_L(y)\,dy=C\int_0^\infty\frac{y^2\,dy}{(y^2+c_L)^{11/6}}=\frac{C}{2}B\bigl(\tfrac32,\tfrac13\bigr)c_L^{-1/3}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c_L=\Bigl[\frac C2B\bigl(\tfrac32,\tfrac13\bigr)\Bigr]^3, \tag{14.11} \]

where \(B\) is the Beta function (the substitution \(y=\sqrt{c_L}\,t\) reduces the integral to \(\tfrac12c_L^{-1/3}B(\tfrac32,\tfrac13)\), valid for \(p_0=2\), where the exponent \(\tfrac53+p_0=\tfrac{11}3\)). For the dissipation the variable is \(x=\kappa\eta\) of (14.1); then \(2\nu\kappa^2E\,d\kappa=2C\varepsilon\,x^{1/3}f_\eta\,dx\), because \(\nu\varepsilon^{2/3}\eta^{-4/3}=\varepsilon\), and

\[ 1=2C\int_0^\infty x^{1/3}f_\eta(x)\,dx. \tag{14.12} \]

For \(c_\eta=0\) the integral is \(\Gamma(\tfrac43)\beta^{-4/3}\), so (14.12) fixes the exponent: \(\beta=\bigl(2C\Gamma(\tfrac43)\bigr)^{3/4}\). With \(C=1.5\) this is 2.094, the value of about 2.1 that the slide gives for the constrained simple exponential. For \(\beta=5.2\) with \(c_\eta=0\) the right side of (14.12) is \(2C\Gamma(\tfrac43)\,5.2^{-4/3}=0.297\): the exponential with the fitted \(\beta\) carries only 30 % of the dissipation, and \(c_\eta\) must be added to restore it. The root of (14.12) for \(\beta=5.2\) is \(c_\eta=0.402\), and (14.11) gives \(c_L=6.783\) (program below), so the two constants of the slide, 6.78 and 0.40, are reproduced from \(C=1.5\), \(\beta=5.2\), \(p_0=2\) and the definition \(L=k^{3/2}/\varepsilon\). At a finite Reynolds number the two conditions couple, because the factors overlap; solved together, they give \(c_L=6.46\) and \(c_\eta=0.402\) at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=600\), \(c_L=5.12\) and \(c_\eta=0.410\) at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=100\) (the program uses \(L/\eta=\mathrm{Re}_L^{3/4}\) and \(k/u_\eta^2=\mathrm{Re}_L^{1/2}\) with \(\mathrm{Re}_L=\tfrac3{20}\mathrm{Re}_\lambda^2\), so \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) alone fixes the nondimensional spectrum).

What is fitted and what is derived. The model has one constant fitted to measurements, \(\beta\) (the slide's \(\beta=5.2\)), one empirical constant, \(C\), and one discrete choice, \(p_0\). The other two constants follow from the energy and dissipation integrals, so the model carries no freedom to adjust the large- and small-scale shapes at a given Reynolds number except through \(p_0\) and \(\beta\). Because \(c_L\) and \(c_\eta\) come from integral constraints and not from fits, the agreement of the model with measured spectra over several decades of Reynolds number, shown in the next section, is a test of the functional forms, with one fitted parameter, and not a fit of many parameters. The agreement in the inertial subrange is by construction, because (14.9) contains the \(-\tfrac53\) law; the lecture says so (L40, 8 to 9 min) and so does the slide quoted in the section on measured inertial subranges.

Using the model for a flow. The slide gives two ways to specify the spectrum of a given flow. Either the dimensional inputs \(k\), \(\varepsilon\) and \(\nu\) are given, and \(L=k^{3/2}/\varepsilon\), \(\eta\) and \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=(20k^2/(3\varepsilon\nu))^{1/2}\) follow, with \(C=1.5\) and \(\beta=5.2\); or \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) is given, and the nondimensional spectrum \(E/(u_\eta^2\eta)\) as a function of \(\kappa\eta\) follows. The two routes agree because \(k\), \(\varepsilon\) and \(\nu\) fix \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) and the two scales. For the jet, \((\varepsilon\nu^5)^{1/4}=u_\eta^2\eta=2.70\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^3/s^2}\), and the solved model (program below) has its maximum \(E=0.100\ \mathrm{m^3/s^2}\) at \(\kappa=15\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\) (\(\ell=2\pi/\kappa=0.42\) m, 2.3 times the \(L=0.18\) m of the jet's \(k\) and \(\varepsilon\)), and \(E=1.4\times10^{-3}\), \(1.7\times10^{-4}\) and \(1.3\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^3/s^2}\) at \(\kappa=377\), 1326 and 5000 \(\mathrm{m^{-1}}\). From the peak to \(\kappa=5000\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\) the spectral density falls by a factor \(7.6\times10^3\) over 2.5 decades of wavenumber, which is why spectra are drawn on logarithmic axes and why a probe must have a dynamic range of about \(10^4\) in spectral density to see the dissipation range.

The model against measured one-dimensional spectra L39 0:37

Collapse in Kolmogorov variables. The slide of Figure 6.14 (L38 21:30; the next lecture returns to it at L39 8:00) plots the measured one-dimensional longitudinal spectra \(E_{11}(\kappa_1)/(\varepsilon\nu^5)^{1/4}\) against \(\kappa_1\eta\) on logarithmic axes for flows of different kinds (grid turbulence, channel, boundary layer, jet, wake; Saddoughi (1993), Figure 3, p. 245, shows a similar compilation with its legend of experiments, starting at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=23\), and a curve labelled "Pao (1965)"). The first similarity hypothesis implies that \(\varphi_{11}\equiv E_{11}/(\varepsilon\nu^5)^{1/4}\) is a universal function of \(\kappa_1\eta\) for \(\kappa_1\gt\kappa_{EI}\), and the slide states what the figure shows: for \(\kappa_1\eta\gt0.1\) all the data lie on a single curve; the high-Reynolds-number data show power-law behaviour below \(\kappa_1\eta=0.1\), the extent of the power-law region growing with \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\); and departures from the common curve come from the energy-containing range, which depends on the flow. What the collapse requires. A plot in Kolmogorov variables is only as good as the measured \(\varepsilon\), because \(\varepsilon\) enters both axes: \((\varepsilon\nu^5)^{1/4}\propto\varepsilon^{1/4}\) scales the ordinate and \(\eta\propto\varepsilon^{-1/4}\) scales the abscissa. If \(\varepsilon\) is wrong by a fraction \(\delta\), a whole curve moves by \((1+\delta)^{-1/4}-1\) in each direction: \(-2.4\ \%\) for \(\delta=0.1\) and \(-4.5\ \%\) for \(\delta=0.2\) (program below). A collapse of different experiments within a few percent is therefore compatible with errors of 10 to 20 % in \(\varepsilon\), and it cannot distinguish a universal function from a family that differs by a few percent. The compensated spectrum is more sensitive, because it carries \(\varepsilon^{-2/3}\): the same errors move the plateau by \(-6.2\ \%\) and \(-11.4\ \%\). This is the size of the \(\pm0.1\) that Saddoughi (1993, p. 247) gives for \(C=1.5\) when his dissipation is uncertain by \(\pm10\ \%\) (0.062 of 1.5 is 0.09), and it shows that the uncertainty of the constant \(C\) in a single experiment is set mostly by the uncertainty of \(\varepsilon\) and not by the plateau. In the data of Saddoughi, \(\varepsilon\) comes from the isotropic relation \(\varepsilon=15\nu\int\kappa_1^2E_{11}d\kappa_1\), so the isotropy that the ratio \(\tfrac43\) tests is also assumed in the scaling that the comparison uses: the relation that fixes \(\varepsilon\) holds only if the turbulence is isotropic at the scales that dominate the integral.

The dissipation spectrum and the cumulative dissipation, in the slide's notation, are

\[ \frac{D(\kappa)}{u_\eta^3}=\frac{2\nu\kappa^2u_\eta^2\eta\,\varphi}{u_\eta^3}=2(\kappa\eta)^2\varphi(\kappa\eta),\qquad\frac{\varepsilon_{(0,\kappa)}}{\varepsilon}=\int_0^{\kappa\eta}2x^2\varphi(x)\,dx, \tag{14.14} \]

Executable check of the model spectrum

The program below solves for \(c_L\) and \(c_\eta\) at a given \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) from the two integral conditions, recomputes the lecture's tables, evaluates the closed forms (14.11), (14.12), (14.16), and applies the model to the jet. Its inputs are in the code: \(C=1.5\), \(\beta=5.2\), \(p_0=2\) (and 4 once), the Reynolds numbers, and for the jet \(k=6\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\), \(\varepsilon=80\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\), \(\nu=1.4607\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). Its outputs are printed numbers only; it reads and writes no files and needs NumPy 2 (numpy.trapezoid). Its assumptions are those of the text: homogeneous isotropic turbulence, the model (14.9) with \(L=k^{3/2}/\varepsilon\), the isotropic relation (14.6), the isotropic identity \(\mathrm{Re}_L=\tfrac3{20}\mathrm{Re}_\lambda^2\), and a spectrum that is zero beyond \(\kappa\eta=40\) and below \(\kappa\eta=10^{-9}\), where the model's contribution to every integral is smaller than the printed precision. The root finder raises RuntimeError if the two integral conditions do not converge. The program checks the algebra and the numbers of the lecture's tables; it does not test any hypothesis against measurements, because the measurements were not available.

"""Chapter 14 checks: model spectrum, its calibration, dissipation and energy shares.

Inputs: Kolmogorov constant C = 1.5, beta = 5.2, p0 = 2 (empirical values of the
model spectrum); Re_lambda; for the jet, k, eps, nu (SI units).
Outputs: printed numbers only. No files are read or written. Needs NumPy 2.
Assumptions: homogeneous isotropic turbulence, one-sided spectra, the isotropic
identity Re_L = k^2/(eps nu) = 0.15 Re_lambda^2 (so L/eta = (0.15)^(3/4) Re_lambda^(3/2)).
Failure behaviour: root finding raises RuntimeError if it does not converge.
"""
import math
import numpy as np
from math import gamma

C, BETA = 1.5, 5.2
S = np.linspace(math.log(1e-9), math.log(40.0), 600001)   # ln(kappa*eta)
X = np.exp(S)


def f_L(xl, c_l, p0):
    return (xl / np.sqrt(xl**2 + c_l)) ** (5.0/3.0 + p0)


def f_eta(x, beta, c_eta):
    return np.exp(-beta * (((x**4 + c_eta**4) ** 0.25) - c_eta))


def integ(f):
    """Integral of f(x) dx over the grid, trapezoid in ln x."""
    return float(np.trapezoid(f * X, S))


def cum(f):
    """Cumulative integral of f(x) dx from the lowest grid point."""
    g = f * X
    out = np.concatenate(([0.0], np.cumsum(0.5*(g[1:] + g[:-1])*np.diff(S))))
    return out


def e_hat(ratio, c_l, c_eta, p0=2, beta=BETA):
    """E/(u_eta^2 eta) as a function of x = kappa*eta, ratio = L/eta."""
    return C * X**(-5/3) * f_L(X*ratio, c_l, p0) * f_eta(X, beta, c_eta)


def l_over_eta(re_lam):
    return (0.15 * re_lam**2) ** 0.75


def tail(f):
    """Integral of f(x) dx from each grid point to the top of the grid."""
    return np.concatenate((np.cumsum((0.5*(f[1:]*X[1:] + f[:-1]*X[:-1])*np.diff(S))[::-1])[::-1], [0.0]))


def calibrate(re_lam, p0=2, beta=BETA):
    """Solve c_L, c_eta so that int E = k and int 2 nu k^2 E = eps."""
    ratio = l_over_eta(re_lam)
    k_target = math.sqrt(0.15) * re_lam            # k/u_eta^2
    p = np.log([6.78, 0.40])
    def res(q):
        e = e_hat(ratio, math.exp(q[0]), math.exp(q[1]), p0, beta)
        return np.array([integ(e)/k_target - 1.0, integ(2*X**2*e) - 1.0])
    for _ in range(40):
        r = res(p)
        if np.max(np.abs(r)) < 1e-10:
            return math.exp(p[0]), math.exp(p[1]), ratio
        J = np.empty((2, 2))
        for j in range(2):
            dq = np.zeros(2); dq[j] = 1e-5
            J[:, j] = (res(p + dq) - r) / 1e-5
        p = p - np.linalg.solve(J, r)
    raise RuntimeError("calibration did not converge")


def crossing(cumv, target, xs):
    """Interpolate the x at which a monotone cumulative curve reaches target."""
    i = int(np.searchsorted(cumv, target))
    t = (target - cumv[i-1]) / (cumv[i] - cumv[i-1])
    return math.exp(math.log(xs[i-1]) + t*(math.log(xs[i]) - math.log(xs[i-1])))

What the statements establish and what they do not

Hypothesis, dimensional argument, fit and definition. The form (14.1) is the first similarity hypothesis plus dimensional analysis. The constancy of \(\Psi\) in the inertial subrange (14.5) is the second hypothesis plus the argument of the second section (the invariance of \(E\) under a change of \(\nu\)). The value \(C=1.5\) is empirical, with a spread of about 20 % across experiments and simulations, and \(C_1=\tfrac{18}{55}C\), \(C_1'=\tfrac43C_1\) hold for isotropic turbulence and an exact \(-\tfrac53\) power law. The functions \(f_L\) and \(f_\eta\) and the exponent \(p_0\) are modelling choices; \(\beta=5.2\) is a fit; \(c_L\) and \(c_\eta\) are determined by integral conditions; the nominal \(\ell_{DI}=60\eta\) is a convention (the 10 % point of the dissipation of one model at one Reynolds number) and \(\ell_{EI}=L_{11}/6\) is a convention (the 90 % point of the energy of one model at a high Reynolds number).

The model is not the spectrum of the flow. The tables are tables of a model constructed to agree with measured spectra. The measured test is indirect: it compares one-dimensional spectra, and the dissipation spectrum \(D(\kappa)\) and the cumulative energy are derived from the model, not measured, so the numbers 0.26, 24, 0.73 and 8.6 hold to the extent that the model describes the true spectrum. The model has no intermittency: the exponent \(-\tfrac53\) of (14.5) is the Kolmogorov (1941) exponent, and the refinement of Kolmogorov (1962), reviewed in chapter 10, predicts a small correction that depends on an intermittency constant. The model describes homogeneous isotropic turbulence; the running case, the jet, is neither, and the band of energy it gives extends above the jet's size.

Reynolds-number limits. The lecture states neither table's Reynolds-number dependence. The recomputation shows that the energy table moves with \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) at its small-scale end (90 % point at \(\ell/L_{11}=0.57\), 0.40 and 0.154 for \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=30\), 60 and 1000) and that the dissipation table moves by at most 10 % between \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=100\) and 1500. A nominal inertial subrange with \(\ell_0=L\), \(\ell_{EI}=L/6\) and \(\ell_{DI}=60\eta\) has width \(\ell_{EI}/\ell_{DI}=(L/\eta)/360\); it exceeds 1 only for \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\gt131\) (0.67 at 100, 9.8 at 600, 39 at 1500), and Yeung and Zhou (1997, abstract, report p. 1) find in simulations that a proper inertial range emerges at \(\kappa\eta\approx0.02\)–0.05 when \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) exceeds about 140. The two numbers are of the same order and come from different definitions. Measured by the local slope or by the compensated amplitude, the inertial range of the model itself is 0.6 to 0.8 decade wider than the nominal one at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=100\) to 5000 and about 0.3 decade wider than the measured range of Saddoughi (1993) at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\approx600\) (section on measured inertial subranges), so a width taken from the nominal factors, from the model and from a measurement are three different numbers.

What was not checked. The measured data of Saddoughi and Veeravalli (1994) and of Comte-Bellot and Corrsin (1971) were not available, so the agreement of the model with them is as the slides and the cited reports state it. The model spectrum's form and constants are those of the slides; no textbook was available to compare them against, and they were verified by recomputation: the constants \(c_L=6.78\) and \(c_\eta=0.40\) follow from the two integral conditions, which is an independent check of those constants and of the form of \(f_L\) and \(f_\eta\) but not of \(p_0\) or \(\beta\). The spectrum of Pao (1965) and the exponential form named in the slide's caption were not analysed.

Exercises

No practice problems are assigned to this chapter; the six exercises are original.

Exercise 1 (derive and evaluate). (a) From (14.1) and (14.2) derive (14.3). (b) Show that in the inertial subrange \(\varphi(x)=Cx^{-5/3}\), and find from the program's output the \(\kappa\eta\) at which the model's \(f_\eta\) lowers \(E\) by 5 % below that law and the nominal \(\kappa_{DI}\eta\); say which is a model property and which a convention. (c) For \(C=1.5\) evaluate \(E/(u_\eta^2\eta)\) at \(\kappa\eta=0.01\) in the inertial subrange, and the corresponding \(E_{11}/(u_\eta^2\eta)\) at \(\kappa_1\eta=0.01\).

Solution

(a) Equating the two forms, \(u_\eta^2\eta\,\varphi=\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3}\Psi\), so \(\Psi=u_\eta^2\eta\,\varepsilon^{-2/3}\kappa^{5/3}\varphi\). With \(\varepsilon=u_\eta^3/\eta\), \(\varepsilon^{-2/3}=\eta^{2/3}u_\eta^{-2}\), so \(\Psi=\eta^{5/3}\kappa^{5/3}\varphi=(\kappa\eta)^{5/3}\varphi\).

(b) In the inertial subrange (14.5) gives \(\Psi=C\), so \(\varphi=(\kappa\eta)^{-5/3}\Psi=Cx^{-5/3}\). The model's \(f_\eta\) lowers \(E\) by 5 % below that law at \(\kappa\eta=0.227\) (program output, section on the width of the inertial range), and the nominal \(\kappa_{DI}\eta=2\pi/60=0.105\). The first is a property of the model, fixed by \(\beta=5.2\) and \(c_\eta=0.40\); the second is a convention, the 60 of chapter 10, and it lies at about half the wavenumber at which the model departs from the law by 5 %.

(c) \(E/(u_\eta^2\eta)=\varphi=Cx^{-5/3}=1.5\times0.01^{-5/3}=1.5\times2154=3232\). For the one-dimensional spectrum, \(C_1=\tfrac{18}{55}\times1.5=0.491\) and \(E_{11}/(u_\eta^2\eta)=0.491\times2154=1058\). The one-dimensional value is 33 % of the three-dimensional one at the same \(\kappa\eta\), the ratio \(\tfrac{18}{55}\); the two spectra are different functions and are not interchangeable.

Exercise 2 (derive and compute). (a) Show that for \(c_\eta=0\) the dissipation condition (14.12) gives \(\beta=(2C\Gamma(\tfrac43))^{3/4}\) and evaluate it for \(C=1.5\) and for \(C=1.62\). (b) What fraction of \(\varepsilon\) does \(\exp(-5.2\kappa\eta)\) carry with \(C=1.5\)? (c) State what the answers imply for the choice between \(c_\eta=0\) and \(c_\eta=0.40\).

Solution

(a) With \(c_\eta=0\), \(\int_0^\infty x^{1/3}e^{-\beta x}dx=\Gamma(\tfrac43)\beta^{-4/3}\), so (14.12) reads \(1=2C\Gamma(\tfrac43)\beta^{-4/3}\) and \(\beta=(2C\Gamma(\tfrac43))^{3/4}\). With \(\Gamma(\tfrac43)=0.8930\): \(\beta=2.094\) for \(C=1.5\) and 2.218 for \(C=1.62\) (program output). The dependence on \(C\) is weak, \(\beta\propto C^{3/4}\): an 8 % change in \(C\) changes \(\beta\) by 6 %.

(b) \(2C\Gamma(\tfrac43)5.2^{-4/3}=0.297\): the exponential with \(\beta=5.2\) carries 30 % of \(\varepsilon\).

(c) A pure exponential can satisfy the dissipation condition only with \(\beta\approx2.1\), and then it departs from 1 too early (\(f_\eta=0.81\) at \(\kappa\eta=0.1\)). The slide's \(\beta=5.2\) fits the shape of the measured dissipation range, and the factor with \(c_\eta=0.40\) restores the integral while keeping \(f_\eta\) near 1 at small \(\kappa\eta\) (0.998 at \(\kappa\eta=0.1\)). The fit constant \(\beta\) and the derived constant \(c_\eta\) are tied: changing \(\beta\) changes \(c_\eta\).

Exercise 3 (check an assumption). The nominal limits are \(\ell_{EI}=\ell_0/6\) and \(\ell_{DI}=60\eta\). Take \(\ell_0=L=k^{3/2}/\varepsilon\). (a) Express \(\ell_{EI}/\ell_{DI}\) in terms of \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) using \(\mathrm{Re}_L=\tfrac3{20}\mathrm{Re}_\lambda^2\). (b) At what \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) does the nominal inertial subrange have zero width, and what are its widths at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=100\), 600 and 1500? (c) Compare with the Reynolds number at which Yeung and Zhou find an inertial range in simulations.

Solution

(a) \((L/\eta)^2=k^3/(\varepsilon^{3/2}\nu^{3/2})=\mathrm{Re}_L^{3/2}\), so \(L/\eta=\mathrm{Re}_L^{3/4}=(0.15\,\mathrm{Re}_\lambda^2)^{3/4}=0.2411\,\mathrm{Re}_\lambda^{3/2}\). Then \(\ell_{EI}/\ell_{DI}=(L/6)/(60\eta)=0.2411\,\mathrm{Re}_\lambda^{3/2}/360\).

(b) The width is 1 (zero decades) when \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=(360/0.2411)^{2/3}=131\). At \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=100\) the ratio is 0.67 (\(\ell_{EI}\lt\ell_{DI}\): the nominal ranges overlap and there is no inertial subrange in this definition), at 600 it is 9.8 and at 1500 it is 39 (program output).

(c) Yeung and Zhou find an approximate inertial range at \(\kappa\eta\approx0.02\)–0.05 when \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) exceeds about 140. The values 131 and 140 are of the same order. They come from different definitions (a nominal width with two assumed factors against a criterion on compensated spectra and isotropy), so the agreement does not validate the nominal factors; it says that the nominal factors do not exclude a Reynolds number at which simulations see an inertial range. Chapter 10, (10.17), gives the same threshold in terms of \(\mathrm{Re}_0\).

Exercise 4 (find a counterexample to a reading of the plateau). A student says that a measured one-dimensional longitudinal spectrum with a flat compensated plateau over one decade proves the \(-\tfrac53\) law, and that a measured ratio \(E_{22}/E_{11}\) within 10 % of \(\tfrac43\) proves local isotropy. (a) Derive \(E_{22}/E_{11}=(1+p)/2\) for \(E_{11}\propto\kappa_1^{-p}\) and find the range of \(p\) with a ratio within 10 % of \(\tfrac43\). (b) Using the model values of the section on measured inertial subranges, say whether a measured ratio of 1.25 at \(\kappa_1\eta=0.003\) and \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=600\) refutes isotropy. (c) Say what a plateau within 3 % of its maximum over a decade does and does not show.

Solution

(a) From (14.6), \(E_{22}=\tfrac12(E_{11}-\kappa_1E_{11}')=\tfrac12(1+p)E_{11}\), because \(-\kappa_1E_{11}'=pE_{11}\). A ratio between \(\tfrac43\times0.9=1.2\) and \(\tfrac43\times1.1=1.467\) needs \(1.40\le p\le1.93\). A ratio within 10 % of \(\tfrac43\) therefore allows exponents that differ from \(\tfrac53\) by up to 0.27 and is a weak test of the exponent. Its use is as a test of isotropy given the exponent, since (14.6) is an isotropic relation.

(b) For the model at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=600\), isotropic by construction, the ratio is 1.304 at \(\kappa_1\eta=0.003\). A measured 1.25 differs by 4 % from 1.304 and by 6 % from \(\tfrac43\), within 10 % either way, so it does not refute isotropy. The comparison must be made with the model value at the same wavenumber and Reynolds number, since the isotropic ratio is \(\tfrac43\) only at one wavenumber (1.334 at 0.01).

(c) The compensated model spectrum is 0.476, 0.488 and 0.479 at \(\kappa_1\eta=0.003\), 0.01 and 0.03: within 3 % of its maximum, over one decade, for a spectrum that is not a power law, since the energy-containing and dissipation factors still differ from 1. A plateau of this quality is necessary for an inertial range; it is not sufficient. The lecture's ratio test (isotropy) and an exponent check are separate requirements (Yeung and Zhou make this point for simulations).

Exercise 5 (design and judge). A probe in the jet resolves scales down to \(\ell_c=1\) mm; treat this as a sharp spectral cut at \(\kappa_c=2\pi/\ell_c\). Use the program's jet numbers. (a) What fractions of the energy and of the dissipation lie below the cut? (b) What cut length resolves 90 % of the dissipation? (c) The probe's resolved dissipation is used as the estimate of \(\varepsilon\): by what factor is it too low, and what does the correction assume?

Solution

(a) With the model at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=453\), \(\kappa_c\eta=2\pi\times79.0\ \mu\mathrm m/1\ \mathrm{mm}=0.496\). The cumulative dissipation below the cut is 0.726 and the cumulative energy is 0.998 (program output). The probe resolves 99.8 % of the energy and 72.6 % of the dissipation.

(b) The 90 % point is at \(\kappa\eta=0.723\), \(\ell=8.7\eta=0.69\) mm; the probe must resolve scales down to 0.69 mm, a 31 % finer cut.

(c) The estimate is low by the factor 0.726, which means the true value is 1.38 times the resolved one. The correction assumes that the model spectrum is correct in shape, that the turbulence is homogeneous and isotropic, that the cut is sharp, and that the dissipation is evaluated from the three-dimensional spectrum and not from a one-dimensional record; the jet violates the first two to an extent that is not known. A one-dimensional probe with a spatial average of finite length attenuates the spectrum gradually, and the factor would differ.

Exercise 6 (reconcile conventions). The slide of Lecture 40 states \(\ell_0=6L_{11}\) and \(\ell_{EI}=\tfrac16L_{11}\), while chapter 10 states \(\ell_{EI}=\ell_0/6\). (a) Derive \(L_{11}/L\) for the model at infinite Reynolds number, (14.16). (b) Express \(6L_{11}\), \(L_{11}/6\) and \(L/6\) as multiples of \(L\) and of \(L_{11}\) (compare Table 14.4). (c) Using the model energy fractions at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=1000\), say which of the two readings of \(\ell_{EI}\) leaves 10 % of the energy at smaller scales.

Solution

(a) With \(f_\eta=1\), \(y=\kappa L\) and \((\varepsilon L)^{2/3}=k\): \(\int E/\kappa\,d\kappa=C\,kL\int_0^\infty y(y^2+c_L)^{-11/6}dy=C\,kL\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac65c_L^{-5/6}=\tfrac35Cc_L^{-5/6}kL\). Then \(L_{11}=\tfrac{3\pi}{4k}\int E/\kappa\,d\kappa=\tfrac{9\pi}{20}Cc_L^{-5/6}L\). With \(C=1.5\) and \(c_L=6.783\), \(L_{11}=0.430L\) (program output).

(b) \(6L_{11}=2.58L\), \(L_{11}/6=0.072L\), \(L/6=0.167L=0.39L_{11}\) (the program gives 2.65, 0.073 and 0.38 at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=1000\), where \(L_{11}/L=0.441\)). So \(\ell_0/6\) equals \(L_{11}\) if \(\ell_0=6L_{11}\), and equals \(0.39L_{11}\) if \(\ell_0=L\); neither equals the slide's \(\ell_{EI}=L_{11}/6\).

(c) The model at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=1000\) has 19 % of the energy below \(L/6\) and 10.6 % below \(L_{11}/6\). The slide's \(\ell_{EI}=L_{11}/6\) is the length that leaves about 10 % of the energy at smaller scales, which is the 90 % point of the table; \(\ell_0/6\) with \(\ell_0=L\) leaves 19 %. The two conventions therefore differ by a factor 2.3 in length, and \(\ell_{EI}\) must be stated with its definition when a number is used.
