Random processes, spectra and random fields: from one velocity record to the energy spectrum

A hot-wire probe on the centreline of an air jet samples the axial velocity 20 000 times in one second. The average of the 20 000 values is 8.0 m/s and their standard deviation is 2.0 m/s. If the values were independent samples, the standard error of the mean would be \(2.0/\sqrt{20\,000}=0.014\ \mathrm{m/s}\). If the velocity at the probe stays correlated with itself for about 0.01 s, the standard error is about 0.28 m/s, twenty times larger, and a mean known to 1 % needs a record of about 12.5 s. The number that decides the difference is the integral time scale. The probability density function of chapter 7 cannot supply it, because that function describes the velocity at one instant. This chapter extends the description from a random variable to a random process, a velocity that is random at every time, and then to a random field, a velocity that is random at every point and every time. It defines the autocovariance, the integral time scale and the frequency spectrum, derives the error of a time average, and sets out the spatial counterparts: homogeneity, isotropy, the two-point correlation, the velocity-spectrum tensor and the energy spectrum.

The objects of the chapter and the running case

Chapter 7 used a repeatable experiment that records one number per run: one velocity component \(U\) at a fixed position and a fixed time. This chapter keeps the experiment and its conditions and changes what is recorded. In each run the probe records a function of time, \(U^{(n)}(t)\) for the \(n\)-th run, over an interval of time. At every fixed \(t\), the values \(U^{(1)}(t),U^{(2)}(t),\ldots\) over the runs are the outcomes of a random variable in the sense of chapter 7, and the collection of these random variables for all \(t\) is a random process, written \(U(t)\). One recorded function \(U^{(n)}(t)\) is a sample path of the process. The angle brackets \(\langle\cdot\rangle\) keep their meaning from chapter 7: the expectation, estimated by an average over runs at a fixed time. The fluctuation is \(u(t)\equiv U(t)-\langle U(t)\rangle\). In the second half of the chapter the recorded object becomes the velocity vector at every point of the flow, a random field.

The running case is the air jet of chapters 6 and 7 at its downstream station. On the centreline the mean axial velocity is \(\langle U_1\rangle=8\ \mathrm{m/s}\), the root-mean-square fluctuation is \(\sigma_u=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\) (chapter 6 writes \(u_0\)), the energy-containing eddies are about \(\ell_0=0.1\ \mathrm m\) across, and the Kolmogorov time scale is \(\tau_\eta=0.43\ \mathrm{ms}\). These values were chosen in chapter 6; they are not measurements of a particular jet. A hot-wire probe at the station records the axial velocity at a sampling rate of 20 kHz, an interval of \(50\ \mu\mathrm s\), about one ninth of \(\tau_\eta\).

One input that the earlier chapters did not need is the integral time scale \(\bar\tau\), defined in (8.6). The running case assumes \(\bar\tau=0.01\ \mathrm s\). This is of the order of \(\ell_0/\langle U_1\rangle=12.5\ \mathrm{ms}\), the time the mean flow takes to carry an eddy of size \(\ell_0\) past the probe. Why the correlation time at a fixed probe should be related to this convection time is the subject of Taylor's hypothesis, which a later chapter treats; here \(\bar\tau\) is an assumed input, and each result that uses it is stated as a function of it. Beside the jet, the chapter uses two synthetic processes that can be computed exactly as often as needed, an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process and a jump process, constructed in the section "Executable processes". They play the role that Lorenz's equations played in chapter 7.

Four symbols need a decision at the start. The time lag between two instants is \(s\) (chapter 7 used \(s\) for the argument of the characteristic function and said that the use was local). A shift of the time origin is \(t_s\) and a shift of position is \(\mathbf x_s\), as in chapter 6; the slides write \(T\) and \(\mathbf X\). The length of a record is \(T\), as on the practice-problem slide, and the period of a periodic process is \(T_p\).

A velocity recorded in time is a random process L20 1:01

At one fixed time, the process is a random variable, and chapter 7's description applies to it. The slide calls this the one-time description:

\[ F(V,t)\equiv P\{U(t)\lt V\},\qquad f(V;t)\equiv\frac{\partial F(V,t)}{\partial V}. \tag{8.1} \]

The one-time CDF \(F(V,t)\) and the one-time PDF \(f(V;t)\) are chapter 7's \(F(V)\) and \(f(V)\) (equations (7.3) and (7.5)) with the time written as an argument. The semicolon in \(f(V;t)\) is the slides' convention: \(f\) is a density with respect to \(V\), and \(t\) is a parameter. A density in \(V\) integrates to 1 over \(V\) at each \(t\); nothing is integrated over \(t\).

The one-time PDF contains no joint information about \(U(t)\) at two or more times. It gives the probability that the velocity at 2 s is below 9 m/s, and it gives the same kind of statement at 2.001 s, but it says nothing about whether a high value at 2 s makes a high value at 2.001 s more probable. The object that does is the \(N\)-time CDF, the joint CDF of the \(N\) random variables \(U(t_1),\ldots,U(t_N)\):

\[ F_N(V_1,t_1;V_2,t_2;\ldots;V_N,t_N)\equiv P\{U(t_1)\lt V_1,\,U(t_2)\lt V_2,\,\ldots,\,U(t_N)\lt V_N\}, \tag{8.2} \]

with the \(N\)-time PDF \(f_N\) obtained from it by differentiating once with respect to each \(V_n\). For \(N=2\), (8.2) is chapter 7's joint CDF (7.14) with \(U_1=U(t_1)\) and \(U_2=U(t_2)\). The slide states that a complete characterization of the process needs \(F_N\) for every \(N\) and every choice of the times, "which in general is an almost impossible task".

The slide's first figure (Figure 3.19 in the slides' numbering) shows three sample paths of \(U(t)\) from three repetitions of a turbulent-flow experiment, for \(0\le t\le10\) in the figure's units. All three start at zero, rise during the first two units of time and then fluctuate about a value near 3.5, between about 1.5 and 5.5. The three paths differ in every detail after the first unit of time. The figure illustrates both parts of the definition: at each \(t\) the three values are three outcomes of one random variable, and the one-time PDF at \(t=1\) is not the one at \(t=8\).

The second figure (Figure 3.20) makes the main point of the section. It shows sample paths of five processes, labelled (a)–(e) as in the caption: (a) a measured turbulent velocity; (b) a measured turbulent velocity of a higher frequency than that of (a); (c) a Gaussian process with the same spectrum as that of (a); (d) an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process with the same integral time scale as that of (a); (e) a jump process with the same spectrum as that of (d). The caption states that the one-time PDF of each is a standardized Gaussian. The paths differ visibly: (b) fluctuates faster than (a), (d) is rougher than (a) and (c), and (e) is constant between isolated instants at which it jumps to a new value. One-time PDFs that are identical cannot distinguish them. The terms "spectrum", "integral time scale" and "Gaussian process" are defined in the next sections, and the section "What the autocorrelation distinguishes" returns to the five processes with them.

Statistical stationarity L20 8:08

The slide's simplification is to consider processes whose statistics do not change with time. A process is statistically stationary if all its multi-time statistics are invariant under a shift of the time origin: for every \(N\), every choice of the times \(t_1,\ldots,t_N\) and every shift \(t_s\gt0\),

\[ f_N(V_1,t_1+t_s;\,V_2,t_2+t_s;\,\ldots;\,V_N,t_N+t_s)=f_N(V_1,t_1;\,V_2,t_2;\,\ldots;\,V_N,t_N). \tag{8.3} \]

(The slide writes the shift as \(T\).) Two consequences are used throughout the chapter. With \(N=1\), the one-time PDF does not depend on \(t\), so the mean \(\langle U\rangle\), the variance \(\langle u^2\rangle\) and every one-time moment are constants. With \(N=2\), the joint PDF of \(U(t_1)\) and \(U(t_2)\) depends on the two times only through their difference \(t_2-t_1\), because a shift \(t_s=-t_1\) moves the pair to the times \(0\) and \(t_2-t_1\). (The definition states \(t_s\gt0\); a negative shift is the same statement read from right to left.)

Stationarity is a property of the statistics, not of the signal. The sample paths of Figure 3.20 vary all the time, and all five processes are stationary. The slide states that a turbulent flow, after an initial transient period, can reach a statistically stationary state in which the flow variables vary with time and the statistics do not. Its Figure 3.21 shows the mean \(\langle U(t)\rangle\) and the variance \(\langle u(t)^2\rangle\) of the process of Figure 3.19: both start at zero, rise, and are constant after about \(t=6\) in the figure's units. The transient is the part of the record before that time. An estimate of a stationary statistic must exclude it.

Many turbulent flows are not stationary. The turbulence behind a grid towed through still water decays with time, so its variance changes; a flow started from rest is in its transient while it develops; a flow whose boundary conditions change with time has statistics that change with them. For the running case, the jet issues from a nozzle supplied at constant flow rate, and after the start-up transient its statistics at the station are assumed to be stationary. That is an assumption about the experiment, not a consequence of the equations of motion.

Stationarity cannot be established from one finite record, because the definition is a statement about all times and about the ensemble. What a record can do is fail a consistency test. Beyhaghi, Alimo and Bewley review procedures that identify and remove the initial transient of a record and tests of second-order stationarity, the stationarity of the mean and of the two-time covariance only (Beyhaghi, Alimo and Bewley 2018, arXiv preprint p. 5). Chapter 7's Lorenz ensemble is an example of the open question: its variance was 57.8 at \(t^*=60\) and 61.6 at \(t^*=100\), a difference of 3.8 against a standard error of about \(1.1\sqrt2=1.6\) for the difference (1.1 for each estimate under the independence assumption that chapter 7 states), about 2.4 standard errors, which does not decide whether that ensemble had reached a stationary state.

Autocovariance, autocorrelation function and integral time scale L20 12:04

For a stationary process, the slide's simplest multi-time statistic is the autocovariance, and its normalized form is the autocorrelation function:

\[ R(s)\equiv\langle u(t)\,u(t+s)\rangle,\qquad \rho(s)\equiv\frac{\langle u(t)\,u(t+s)\rangle}{\langle u(t)^2\rangle}=\frac{R(s)}{R(0)}. \tag{8.4} \]

The lag \(s\) is the time between the two values. By the two-time consequence of (8.3), neither \(R\) nor \(\rho\) depends on \(t\). \(R\) has the units of a variance, \(\mathrm{m^2/s^2}\) for a velocity; \(\rho\) has no units. The slide lists the properties

\[ \rho(0)=1,\qquad |\rho(s)|\le1,\qquad \rho(-s)=\rho(s). \tag{8.5} \]

Each follows in one step. \(\rho(0)=1\) is the definition at \(s=0\). For the bound, \(\rho(s)\) is the correlation coefficient \(\rho_{12}\) of chapter 7 (equation (7.17)) for the pair \(U_1=U(t)\), \(U_2=U(t+s)\), whose variances are equal by stationarity; the Cauchy–Schwarz argument of (7.18) gives \(|\rho_{12}|\le1\). For the symmetry, write \(R(-s)=\langle u(t)u(t-s)\rangle\). This holds for every \(t\), so it holds with \(t\) replaced by \(t+s\): \(R(-s)=\langle u(t+s)u(t)\rangle=R(s)\). The hinge of this step is the shift, which uses stationarity; the order of the two factors in a product does not matter, but without stationarity \(\langle u(t)u(t-s)\rangle\) and \(\langle u(t)u(t+s)\rangle\) are covariances at different pairs of times and need not be equal. The slide adds that if every sample path is periodic with period \(T_p\), so that \(U(t+T_p)=U(t)\), then \(\rho(s+T_p)=\rho(s)\): the factor \(u(t+s+T_p)\) equals \(u(t+s)\) in every run.

The integral time scale. The slide states that for processes in turbulent flows the correlation diminishes as the lag increases, so that the integral

\[ \bar\tau\equiv\int_0^\infty\rho(s)\,ds \tag{8.6} \]

converges; it is called the integral time scale of the process. It is the area under the autocorrelation function for positive lags, and it measures how long the process remembers its value. That the integral converges is an assumption about the process, and decay of \(\rho\) is not enough to guarantee it: \(\rho(s)=1/(1+|s|)\) decays to zero and has an infinite integral. Two simple processes violate the assumption more strongly. A process that is constant in each run but varies from run to run has \(\rho(s)=1\) for all \(s\), and (8.6) diverges; Exercise 2 shows what this does to a time average. A periodic process such as \(U(t)=\cos(t+\Theta)\), with a phase \(\Theta\) uniformly distributed over \([0,2\pi)\), has \(\rho(s)=\cos s\), and \(\int_0^S\cos s\,ds=\sin S\) has no limit as \(S\to\infty\). The integral can also converge to zero, so that the process has no positive time scale in the sense of (8.6); Exercise 3 constructs such a case and shows that it is not pathological.

A worked example. The simplest autocorrelation with a finite integral time scale is the exponential,

\[ \rho(s)=e^{-|s|/\bar\tau},\qquad \int_0^\infty e^{-s/\bar\tau}\,ds=\bar\tau. \tag{8.7} \]

Here \(\bar\tau\) is also the lag at which \(\rho\) has fallen to \(e^{-1}=0.37\). For the running case, with the assumed \(\bar\tau=10\ \mathrm{ms}\), an exponential autocorrelation would be 0.37 at a lag of 10 ms and \(e^{-3}=0.050\) at 30 ms. The exponential has a corner at \(s=0\): its slope is \(-1/\bar\tau\) just after the origin and \(+1/\bar\tau\) just before it. The section "The spectrum distributes the variance" shows that this corner means that the process has no derivative, so (8.7) is a model for the velocity at lags larger than the smallest time scales of the flow, not at the smallest lags.

A computed estimate of \(\bar\tau\) integrates an estimated \(\hat\rho(s)\) up to a cutoff lag, because \(\hat\rho\) at large lags is dominated by sampling noise. The program of the section "Executable processes" integrates \(\hat\rho\) of two processes with \(\bar\tau=1\) from 0 to 5 and obtains 0.989 and 0.985. The exact integral to 5 is \(1-e^{-5}=0.993\); the rest of the difference is sampling error. The cutoff is a choice that the estimate inherits.

What the autocorrelation distinguishes, and what it does not L20 16:55

The slide's Figure 3.22 shows the autocorrelation functions of the five processes of Figure 3.20 for lags from \(-6\) to 6. Processes (a) and (c) have the same autocorrelation, and an inset shows that it is smooth at the origin: it has a rounded top, with zero slope at \(s=0\). Process (b), the turbulent velocity of higher frequency, has a much narrower autocorrelation than (a). Processes (d) and (e) have the exponential autocorrelation (8.7), with a corner at \(s=0\), and, the slide states, the same integral time scale as process (a). Hence apart from process (b) all five processes have the same integral time scale.

The narrower autocorrelation of (b) has a direct reading. A process whose values change faster stops being correlated with its own past after a shorter lag, so \(\rho\) falls faster and its area \(\bar\tau\) is smaller. Fast fluctuation is a statement about the sample paths; a narrow \(\rho\) is the corresponding statement about the statistics.

The other comparison is the important one. Processes (a), (c), (d) and (e) have the same one-time PDF and the same integral time scale, and (d) and (e) have the same autocorrelation function as well. Their sample paths are still very different: (e) is piecewise constant and (d) is not. The one-time PDF and the autocorrelation function together are not enough to characterize a process. The slide draws this conclusion for (d) and (e).

The program of the section "Executable processes" builds a pair of the same kind and measures the difference. Its Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process is a Gaussian process with the exponential autocorrelation (8.7). Its jump process holds its value and, in each time step \(\Delta t\), replaces it with probability \(p=1-e^{-\Delta t/\bar\tau}\) (\(p\) is a probability in this chapter, which contains no pressure) by a new value drawn independently from the same Gaussian distribution. The probability of no jump over a lag of \(j\) steps is \((1-p)^j=e^{-j\Delta t/\bar\tau}\), and after any jump the new value is independent of the old one, so the autocorrelation is \(e^{-s/\bar\tau}\) at every lag \(s=j\,\Delta t\): the same as that of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process. With \(\bar\tau=1\), \(\Delta t=0.05\) and 4000 records of 1000 samples each, the program finds the one-time variance 0.999 and flatness 3.01 and 3.03 (Gaussian: 1 and 3), and \(\hat\rho(1)=0.364\) and 0.370 against \(e^{-1}=0.368\). The difference shows in the increment \(U(t+\Delta t)-U(t)\). For the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process it is Gaussian, with flatness 3.0. For the jump process it is zero unless a jump occurs; with probability \(p\) it is the difference of two independent Gaussian values, with variance \(2\sigma_u^2\) and fourth moment \(3(2\sigma_u^2)^2\). Its flatness is therefore \(p\cdot12\sigma_u^4/(p\cdot2\sigma_u^2)^2=3/p=61.5\), and the program measures 62.0. Figure 8.1 shows one sample path of each.

The rounded top of \(\rho\) for processes (a) and (c) and the corner for (d) and (e) are a second difference, and the next two sections show that it decides whether the process has a time derivative.

The frequency spectrum and the autocovariance form a Fourier pair L20 21:31

The frequency spectrum \(E(\omega)\) of a stationary process is defined from its autocovariance by the slide's formula

\[ E(\omega)\equiv\frac1\pi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}R(s)\,e^{-i\omega s}\,ds=\frac2\pi\int_0^\infty R(s)\cos(\omega s)\,ds, \tag{8.8} \]

where \(\omega\) is the angular frequency in rad/s and \(i\) the imaginary unit. The inverse relation is

\[ R(s)=\frac12\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}E(\omega)\,e^{i\omega s}\,d\omega=\int_0^\infty E(\omega)\cos(\omega s)\,d\omega. \tag{8.9} \]

The second form of each follows from the first in one step. Write \(e^{-i\omega s}=\cos\omega s-i\sin\omega s\). Because \(R\) is even by (8.5), \(R(s)\sin\omega s\) is odd in \(s\) and integrates to zero over \((-\infty,\infty)\), while \(R(s)\cos\omega s\) is even, and its integral over \((-\infty,\infty)\) is twice its integral over \((0,\infty)\). The same argument applied to (8.8) shows that \(E\) is real and even, and then gives the second form of (8.9). So \(R(s)\) and \(E(\omega)\) carry the same information in two forms: given either, (8.8) or (8.9) returns the other.

Equation (8.8) is a definition only when its integral exists. This chapter uses it for processes whose autocovariance is absolutely integrable, \(\int_0^\infty|R(s)|\,ds\lt\infty\); then \(E(\omega)\) is a bounded continuous function of \(\omega\), and (8.9) returns \(R\). The exponential (8.7) satisfies the condition. Two processes of the section "Autocovariance, autocorrelation function and integral time scale" do not. For the process that is constant in each run, \(R(s)=\langle u^2\rangle\) for all \(s\); for the periodic process \(\cos(t+\Theta)\), \(R(s)=\tfrac12\cos s\). For both, the integral in (8.8) does not converge: each is a single Fourier mode, at \(\omega=0\) and at \(\omega=1\), its variance sits at one frequency instead of being spread over a range, and no function \(E(\omega)\) describes it. Wherever this chapter writes \(E(\omega)\), the condition \(\int_0^\infty|R|\,ds\lt\infty\) is assumed; the integral time scale (8.6) is then finite, though possibly zero (Exercise 3). The converse does not hold: a process whose spectrum is \(\langle u^2\rangle\) on \(0\le\omega\le1\) and zero above has, by (8.9), \(R(s)=\langle u^2\rangle\int_0^1\cos\omega s\,d\omega\), that is \(\rho(s)=\sin s/s\); its integral time scale is \(\int_0^\infty\sin s/s\,ds=\pi/2\), and \(\int_0^\infty|\rho|\,ds\) diverges, because the area of \(|\rho|\) over the \(n\)-th half-period, \((n-1)\pi\le s\le n\pi\), is at least \(2/(\pi n)\), and \(\sum1/n\) diverges.

The convention. The slide's wording is that the autocovariance "and twice the frequency spectrum form a Fourier-transform pair". With the pair \(\hat g(\omega)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int g(s)e^{-i\omega s}ds\), \(g(s)=\int\hat g(\omega)e^{i\omega s}d\omega\), the transform of \(R\) is \(E/2\), and \(E\) is twice it. The factor 2 makes \(E\) a one-sided spectrum: the second form of (8.9) uses only \(\omega\ge0\), and the contribution of negative frequencies has been folded onto positive ones. Other texts put the factor \(1/(2\pi)\) elsewhere, use a two-sided spectrum, or use the cyclic frequency \(f=\omega/(2\pi)\) in Hz. A spectrum per unit cyclic frequency, \(E_f(f)\), must satisfy \(E_f\,df=E\,d\omega\), so \(E_f(f)=2\pi E(\omega)\) at \(\omega=2\pi f\). Any comparison of spectra from two sources starts by checking which convention each uses. For a velocity, \(E(\omega)\) has the units of \(\mathrm{m^2/s^2}\) per rad/s, that is \(\mathrm{m^2/s}\).

The slide adds that the velocity fluctuation has a spectral representation as a weighted sum of Fourier modes \(e^{i\omega t}=\cos\omega t+i\sin\omega t\) of different frequencies. The precise form of that representation for a stationary process, whose sample paths do not decay and therefore have no ordinary Fourier transform, is beyond this chapter; the chapter uses only (8.8) and (8.9). The pair (8.8)–(8.9) and the non-negativity of \(E\) shown in the next section are the only parts of the spectral theory of stationary processes that this chapter uses, and it derives both under the condition stated above; the general theorem of which they are a special case is not cited in this book.

The exponential pair. For the exponential autocorrelation (8.7), \(R(s)=\langle u^2\rangle e^{-|s|/\bar\tau}\), and the integral \(\int_0^\infty e^{-s/\bar\tau}\cos\omega s\,ds=\bar\tau/(1+\omega^2\bar\tau^2)\) (two integrations by parts) gives

\[ E(\omega)=\frac{2\langle u^2\rangle\bar\tau}{\pi}\,\frac{1}{1+\omega^2\bar\tau^2}. \tag{8.10} \]

Two checks. At \(s=0\), (8.9) gives \(\int_0^\infty E\,d\omega=(2\langle u^2\rangle/\pi)\,[\arctan(\omega\bar\tau)]_0^\infty=\langle u^2\rangle=R(0)\), as it must. And the spectrum is flat for \(\omega\bar\tau\ll1\), falls to half its zero-frequency value at \(\omega=1/\bar\tau\), and decays as \(\omega^{-2}\) for \(\omega\bar\tau\gg1\). For the running case with the assumed \(\bar\tau\), \(E(0)=2\times4\times0.01/\pi=0.0255\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), and the half-value frequency is \(\omega=100\ \mathrm{rad/s}\), that is \(f=15.9\ \mathrm{Hz}\). In the program's output the jump process has the same spectrum as the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, within sampling error, as (8.8) requires of two processes with the same autocovariance.

The spectrum distributes the variance over frequencies L21 4:19

Setting \(s=0\) in (8.9) gives the slide's result

\[ R(0)=\langle u(t)^2\rangle=\int_0^\infty E(\omega)\,d\omega. \tag{8.11} \]

The slide states, and calls the fundamental property of a frequency spectrum, that for \(\omega_a\lt\omega_b\) the integral \(\int_{\omega_a}^{\omega_b}E(\omega)\,d\omega\) is the contribution to the variance \(\langle u^2\rangle\) of all modes in the frequency range \(\omega_a\le\omega\le\omega_b\). Equation (8.11) is an identity. The band statement is an interpretation, and it needs two facts to be meaningful: that \(E\) is never negative, so that every band contributes a non-negative amount, and that \(E\) measures the squared amplitude of the fluctuation at each frequency. Both follow from one computation. For a record of length \(T\) of a zero-mean stationary process, define the record's squared Fourier amplitude per unit time,

\[ \hat E_T(\omega)\equiv\frac1{\pi T}\Bigl|\int_0^Tu(t)e^{-i\omega t}dt\Bigr|^2\ge0,\qquad \langle\hat E_T(\omega)\rangle=\frac2\pi\int_0^T\Bigl(1-\frac sT\Bigr)R(s)\cos(\omega s)\,ds. \tag{8.12} \]

The expectation follows by writing the squared modulus as a double integral over \(t\) and \(t'\), taking the mean inside, which turns \(\langle u(t)u(t')\rangle\) into \(R(t'-t)\), and collecting the pairs with the same lag \(s=t'-t\): in the square \([0,T]^2\) these pairs form a line segment whose length in \(t\) is \(T-|s|\). The evenness of \(R\) then gives the cosine form. If \(\int_0^\infty|R|\,ds\) is finite, the weight \(1-s/T\) tends to 1 at every fixed \(s\) and the integral tends to (8.8) as \(T\to\infty\). So \(E(\omega)\) is the limit of the mean of the non-negative quantity \(\hat E_T(\omega)\), and \(E(\omega)\ge0\). The quantity \(\hat E_T\) is the periodogram of the record in the convention of (8.8); the program uses its average over many records to estimate \(E\).

The spectrum at zero frequency and the integral time scale. Setting \(\omega=0\) in the cosine form of (8.8) gives \(E(0)=(2/\pi)\int_0^\infty R(s)\,ds=(2/\pi)\langle u^2\rangle\bar\tau\) by (8.6), that is

\[ \bar\tau=\frac{\pi E(0)}{2\langle u^2\rangle}. \tag{8.13} \]

The integral time scale is fixed by the level of the spectrum at the lowest frequencies, relative to the variance. For the exponential pair, (8.10) at \(\omega=0\) and (8.13) return \(\bar\tau\); for the running case, \(\pi\times0.0255/(2\times4)=0.0100\ \mathrm s\).

The slide applies (8.13) to the five processes. Process (b) has a smaller integral time scale than (a) and a correspondingly smaller spectrum at the origin, but its spectrum extends to higher frequencies. The two facts are linked by (8.11): both processes have unit variance, so the area under each spectrum is 1, and a spectrum that extends to higher frequencies must be lower at low frequencies. The slide's Figure 3.23 shows the five spectra on logarithmic axes. The curves of (a) and (c) coincide, as do those of (d) and (e). At high frequency the curve of (d) and (e) appears as a straight line on these axes, consistent with the \(\omega^{-2}\) decay of (8.10) (the slide does not state the slope), and the curves of (a), (c) and (b) fall much faster. The slide's conclusion is that the autocorrelation function or the spectrum is usually the only quantity used to characterize the multi-time properties of a random process, and that, together with the one-time PDF, it gives only a partial characterization. Processes (d) and (e) have the same one-time PDF, the same autocorrelation and therefore, by (8.8), the same spectrum, and they are qualitatively different.

The spectrum of the derivative. The difference between the rounded and the cornered autocorrelation is a statement about derivatives. For a stationary process, the mean square of a difference quotient is

\[ \Bigl\langle\Bigl(\frac{u(t+h)-u(t)}{h}\Bigr)^2\Bigr\rangle=\frac{2[R(0)-R(h)]}{h^2}=\int_0^\infty E(\omega)\,\frac{2(1-\cos\omega h)}{h^2}\,d\omega \;\longrightarrow\;\langle\dot u^2\rangle=-R''(0)=\int_0^\infty\omega^2E(\omega)\,d\omega, \tag{8.14} \]

where \(\dot u=du/dt\). The first equality expands the square and uses stationarity; the second uses (8.9). As \(h\to0\), \(2(1-\cos\omega h)/h^2=\omega^2\,[\sin(\omega h/2)/(\omega h/2)]^2\) tends to \(\omega^2\) at every fixed \(\omega\) and never exceeds \(\omega^2\); it is not monotone in \(h\) (it vanishes at \(\omega h=2\pi\)), so the limit needs two cases. If \(\int_0^\infty\omega^2E\,d\omega\) is finite, the integrand is bounded by \(\omega^2E(\omega)\) and the right side tends to that integral (dominated convergence). If it is infinite, the right side also grows without bound, because the integrands are non-negative and converge pointwise to \(\omega^2E\), and the limit of their integrals is then at least the integral of the limit (Fatou's lemma). On the left, if \(R\) has a second derivative at \(0\), then \(R'(0)=0\) because \(R\) is even, and \(R(h)=R(0)+\tfrac12R''(0)h^2+o(h^2)\) gives the limit \(-R''(0)\). A process whose autocorrelation is smooth at the origin, as for (a) and (c), has a derivative with finite variance. For the exponential (8.7), \(2[R(0)-R(h)]/h^2=2\langle u^2\rangle(1-e^{-h/\bar\tau})/h^2\approx2\langle u^2\rangle/(\bar\tau h)\), which grows without bound as \(h\to0\); equivalently, the integrand \(\omega^2E\) of (8.10) tends to a constant and its integral diverges. The program's last three lines show the growth: the variance of the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck difference quotient is 9.1, 19.0 and 39.1 for \(h=0.2\), 0.1 and 0.05, doubling as \(h\) halves. The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck and jump processes have no time derivative. A turbulent velocity, which viscosity smooths below the Kolmogorov scales of chapter 5, does; Exercises 7 and 8 derive the covariances of its derivatives.

Time averages, ergodicity and the length of a record

An ensemble average is defined over runs. A laboratory, a field station or a simulation usually supplies one long record instead, and uses its time average. This section states what licenses the substitution and how large its error is. The material is not on the lecture slides; the practice problem of Exercise 9 asks for the central formula.

The time average of one record of length \(T\) is

\[ \langle U\rangle_T\equiv\frac1T\int_0^TU(t)\,dt. \tag{8.15} \]

(The subscript \(T\) marks the time average; the angle brackets without subscript remain the expectation.) \(\langle U\rangle_T\) is itself a random variable: each run gives a different value. For a stationary process its mean is \(\langle U\rangle\), because the expectation can be taken inside the integral and \(\langle U(t)\rangle\) is the same at every \(t\). Its variance is the mean square of \(T^{-1}\int_0^Tu\,dt\), a double integral of \(R(t-t')\) over the square \([0,T]^2\). Collecting the pairs with the same lag, as in (8.12), gives

\[ \mathrm{var}\bigl(\langle U\rangle_T\bigr)=\frac1{T^2}\int_0^T\!\!\int_0^TR(t-t')\,dt\,dt'=\frac2T\int_0^T\Bigl(1-\frac sT\Bigr)R(s)\,ds. \tag{8.16} \]

This is exact for every \(T\). Its long-time form follows by splitting the integral: \(\int_0^TR\,ds\) tends to \(\langle u^2\rangle\bar\tau\) by (8.6), and the remainder \(T^{-1}\int_0^TsR\,ds\) tends to zero provided \(T^{-1}\int_0^Ts|\rho(s)|\,ds\to0\), which holds, for example, when \(\int_0^\infty s|\rho|\,ds\) is finite. Then

\[ \mathrm{var}\bigl(\langle U\rangle_T\bigr)\approx\frac{2\bar\tau}{T}\,\langle u^2\rangle\qquad(T\gg\bar\tau). \tag{8.17} \]

A sample of \(n\) independent values has a sample mean with variance \(\langle u^2\rangle/n\). Comparing with (8.17), a continuous record of length \(T\) is worth \(T/(2\bar\tau)\) independent samples, and a sampled record approaches that worth once its sampling interval is much shorter than \(\bar\tau\) (Exercise 1(c)). Equation (8.17) requires \(\bar\tau\) to exist and to be positive. If \(\bar\tau=0\), (8.17) gives zero and the error is set by the next term (Exercise 3); if \(\bar\tau\) is infinite, the error does not decrease as \(1/T\) at all (Exercise 2).

For the exponential autocorrelation (8.7), (8.16) can be evaluated exactly:

\[ \mathrm{var}\bigl(\langle U\rangle_T\bigr)=\frac{2\bar\tau\langle u^2\rangle}{T}\Bigl[1-\frac{\bar\tau}{T}\bigl(1-e^{-T/\bar\tau}\bigr)\Bigr], \tag{8.18} \]

so (8.17) exceeds the exact variance by the fraction \((\bar\tau/T)(1-e^{-T/\bar\tau})\le\bar\tau/T\) of its own value. The program computes 4000 records of length \(T=50\bar\tau\) for each process and finds the variance of the 4000 time averages to be 0.0383 and 0.0381 (in units of \(\langle u^2\rangle\)); (8.18) gives 0.0392, and the sampling error of a variance estimated from 4000 values is about \((2/4000)^{1/2}=2.2\,\%\) of it. The value \(\langle u^2\rangle/n\) for \(n=1000\) independent samples, the number of samples in each record (the listing's N; the \(N\) of (8.2) is the number of times), is 0.0010, smaller by a factor of 38.

The error is set by the spectrum at zero frequency. At \(\omega=0\), (8.12) and (8.16) are the same integral up to a factor: \(\langle\hat E_T(0)\rangle=T\,\mathrm{var}(\langle U\rangle_T)/\pi\). As \(T\to\infty\), with (8.13),

\[ \mathrm{var}\bigl(\langle U\rangle_T\bigr)\approx\frac{\pi E(0)}{T}. \tag{8.19} \]

The error of a time average is controlled by the slowest fluctuations in the record, those near zero frequency. Sampling faster adds information about high frequencies and, once the sampling interval is much shorter than \(\bar\tau\), almost none about the mean. Beyhaghi, Alimo and Bewley (2018) state the same model for the averaging error, \(\hat\varepsilon(T)=\sigma(2\tau_f/T)^{1/2}\) in their notation, attribute it to Lumley and Panofsky (1964), and note that it is effective in practice only if \(T/\tau_f\) is relatively large (arXiv preprint pp. 2–3; \(\tau_f\) is their integral time scale and \(\hat\varepsilon\) is not the dissipation rate). They also argue that sampling much more often than about once per \(2\tau_f/c\), with \(c\) a constant of order 10, does not substantially reduce the averaging error (p. 3), and their discrete-sample formula (eq. (6), p. 6) is the sampled form of (8.16). The journal version of this paper has not been checked; the page numbers are those of the arXiv preprint.

Ergodicity. The time average estimates the ensemble mean only if it converges to it. A stationary process is called ergodic in the mean if \(\mathrm{var}(\langle U\rangle_T)\to0\) as \(T\to\infty\); then, by the unbiasedness above, the time average of one record converges in mean square to \(\langle U\rangle\). Equation (8.16) gives a sufficient condition: \(\mathrm{var}(\langle U\rangle_T)\le(2/T)\int_0^T|R(s)|\,ds\), which tends to zero if \(\int_0^\infty|R|\,ds\) is finite. Stationarity alone is not sufficient (Exercise 2). The same argument for the time average of \(u^2\), which estimates the variance, needs the autocovariance of the process \(u^2\), a fourth-order statistic, to decay; ergodicity of one statistic does not imply ergodicity of another. For turbulence, ergodicity is an assumption that cannot be checked from one record. Beyhaghi, Alimo and Bewley state that the statistics of many turbulent flows "are generally considered as stationary and ergodic after some initial transient is identified and set aside" (p. 1). The book follows this practice: an ensemble average is a definition, a time average over a stationary record is its estimate, and (8.16) gives the estimate's error.

The running case. A record of \(T=1\ \mathrm s\) on the jet centreline, with \(\langle u^2\rangle=4\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\) and the assumed \(\bar\tau=0.01\ \mathrm s\), has by (8.17) \(\mathrm{var}(\langle U\rangle_T)=2\times0.01\times4/1=0.080\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\), a standard error of 0.28 m/s; the exact exponential form (8.18) multiplies the variance by \(1-0.01=0.99\). The naive standard error of the lead, \(2/\sqrt{20\,000}=0.014\ \mathrm{m/s}\), treats 20 000 correlated samples as independent; the record is worth \(T/(2\bar\tau)=50\) independent samples. A standard error of 1 % of the mean, 0.08 m/s, needs \(T=2\bar\tau\langle u^2\rangle/0.08^2=12.5\ \mathrm s\). The answer is proportional to the assumed \(\bar\tau\): 6.3 s for \(\bar\tau=5\ \mathrm{ms}\), 25 s for 20 ms. The 20-kHz sampling rate is chosen for the small scales, whose time scale \(\tau_\eta=0.43\ \mathrm{ms}\) must be resolved to measure derivatives; it does nothing for the accuracy of the mean.

The stationary Gaussian process, and why a turbulent velocity is not one L21 16:08

A process is Gaussian if every \(N\)-time PDF is joint normal. Chapter 7's bivariate normal density (7.22) is the case \(N=2\). For general \(N\), with the vector \(\mathbf V=(V_1,\ldots,V_N)\), the means \(m_n=\langle U(t_n)\rangle\) and the covariance matrix \(C_{nm}=\langle u(t_n)u(t_m)\rangle\) (written \(\mathsf C\) in this section only; \(C\) without indices stays the Kolmogorov constant),

\[ f_N=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{N/2}(\det\mathsf C)^{1/2}}\exp\Bigl[-\tfrac12\sum_{n,m}(V_n-m_n)\,(\mathsf C^{-1})_{nm}\,(V_m-m_m)\Bigr],\qquad C_{nm}=R(t_n-t_m)=\langle u^2\rangle\,\rho(t_n-t_m), \tag{8.20} \]

where the second form of \(C_{nm}\) holds for a stationary process (the slide's statement) and \(\mathsf C\) is assumed invertible. For \(N=2\), \(\mathsf C\) has diagonal entries \(\langle u^2\rangle\) and off-diagonal entries \(\langle u^2\rangle\rho(t_2-t_1)\), and (8.20) is (7.22) with \(\rho_{12}=\rho(t_2-t_1)\). The distribution is fully characterized by the means and the covariances. For a stationary Gaussian process the means are one constant, so the slide's conclusion follows: the process is completely characterized by its mean \(\langle U\rangle\), its variance \(\langle u^2\rangle\) and its autocorrelation function \(\rho(s)\), or equivalently by its mean and its spectrum \(E(\omega)\), because (8.11) recovers the variance from the spectrum. Both conditions matter. Without stationarity, a Gaussian process needs the mean as a function of time and the covariance as a function of both times; without the mean, the spectrum, which is built from fluctuations, says nothing about \(\langle U\rangle\).

Process (c) of Figure 3.20 is defined as the Gaussian process with the same spectrum as process (a), the measured turbulent velocity. It therefore has the same one-time PDF (a standardized Gaussian), the same autocorrelation, the same integral time scale and, by (8.14), the same variance of the derivative as (a). Its sample path looks much like that of (a). The slide shows where they differ: in the sample paths of the second derivative \(\ddot U\equiv d^2U/dt^2\) (its Figure 3.24). For (c), \(\ddot U\) is obtained from a Gaussian process by a linear operation (a limit of differences of values at nearby times, which are jointly normal), so it is Gaussian, and its kurtosis, the flatness \(\hat\mu_4\) of chapter 7, is 3. For the turbulent velocity (a), \(\ddot U\) is far from Gaussian: the figure shows long quiet stretches interrupted by bursts of large amplitude, against an even band for (c), and the slide gives its kurtosis as 11. The spectrum fixes every second-order statistic of the derivatives: applying (8.14) to \(\dot u\), whose spectrum is \(\omega^2E(\omega)\) (Exercise 8), gives \(\langle\ddot u^2\rangle=\int_0^\infty\omega^4E\,d\omega\), the same for (a) and (c); the flatness is a fourth-order statistic, and the spectrum does not determine it.

This agrees with the two results that chapter 7 cited (section "Mean, variance and standardized moments"): the one-time PDF of a velocity component in isotropic turbulence is close to Gaussian, and the flatness of a first velocity derivative in simulations rises from 3.39 to 5.11 as the Reynolds number increases. A turbulent velocity has a nearly Gaussian one-time PDF and is not a Gaussian process. The lecture's conclusion is a warning: a Gaussian process with the right spectrum is not a model of the turbulent velocity. The precise boundary of that warning is set by the order of the statistic. A quantity that depends only on \(R(s)\), such as the error (8.16) of a time average, has the same value for (a) and (c), and a Gaussian model with the right spectrum gives it correctly. A quantity that depends on fourth-order statistics, such as the flatness of a derivative or the error of an estimated variance, is wrong in the Gaussian model.

Random fields: the one-point, one-time PDF L21 21:44

A turbulent velocity depends on position as well as time, and it has three components. The slide calls \(\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)\) a time-dependent random vector field. At one point and one time, its three components are three random variables, and the slide defines the one-point, one-time joint CDF and PDF:

\[ F(\mathbf V,\mathbf x,t)\equiv P\{U_i(\mathbf x,t)\lt V_i,\ i=1,2,3\},\qquad f(\mathbf V;\mathbf x,t)\equiv\frac{\partial^3F(\mathbf V,\mathbf x,t)}{\partial V_1\,\partial V_2\,\partial V_3}, \tag{8.21} \]

where \(\mathbf V=(V_1,V_2,V_3)\) is the sample-space vector. The semicolon again marks \(f\) as a density with respect to \(V_1\), \(V_2\), \(V_3\), with \(\mathbf x\) and \(t\) as parameters; its units are those of \(1/(\text{velocity})^3\). At each point and time this PDF fully characterizes the random velocity vector, and it contains no joint information at two or more times or positions.

The two-variable and one-variable PDFs of chapter 7 are marginals of (8.21). Integrating (8.21) over \(V_3\) gives the joint PDF of the axial and radial components, \(f_{12}(V_1,V_2;\mathbf x,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(\mathbf V;\mathbf x,t)\,dV_3\), by the argument of (7.16), and a second integration gives the one-component PDF of chapter 7. For the running case, a probe that measures two components at the jet station gives an estimate of \(f_{12}\) at one point; the hot-wire of this chapter, which measures one component, gives only the marginal \(f_1(V_1;\mathbf x,t)=\iint f(\mathbf V;\mathbf x,t)\,dV_2\,dV_3\), the one-time PDF (8.1) of the axial component, at each time, and its time record gives the process statistics of the earlier sections, not the field.

Mean, fluctuation, Reynolds stresses and the N-point PDF L22 2:46

From the one-point PDF (8.21), the mean velocity field, the fluctuating velocity field and the one-point, one-time covariance of the velocity are

\[ \langle\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)\rangle=\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbf V f(\mathbf V;\mathbf x,t)\,dV_1\,dV_2\,dV_3,\qquad \mathbf u(\mathbf x,t)\equiv\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)-\langle\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)\rangle,\qquad \langle u_i(\mathbf x,t)\,u_j(\mathbf x,t)\rangle. \tag{8.22} \]

(The slide prints the lower limit of the mean integral as \(\infty\); the limits run over the whole sample space.) The decomposition of \(\mathbf U\) into its mean and the fluctuation is the Reynolds decomposition, and the covariances \(\langle u_iu_j\rangle\) are the Reynolds stresses. They form a symmetric \(3\times3\) matrix whose diagonal entries are the variances of the three components; chapter 9 develops their role in the mean momentum equation. On the axis of the running-case jet, chapter 7's reflection argument gives \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle=0\).

The slide states that turbulent velocity fields are differentiable, in the sense that the mean of a derivative is the derivative of the mean: \(\langle\partial U_i/\partial t\rangle=\partial\langle U_i\rangle/\partial t\) and \(\langle\partial U_i/\partial x_j\rangle=\partial\langle U_i\rangle/\partial x_j\). The expectation is linear and a derivative is a limit of difference quotients, so the interchange holds when the difference quotients converge in mean, which (8.14) shows is a property of the smoothness of the autocovariance at zero separation. The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process fails it; a turbulent velocity at finite Reynolds number, smoothed by viscosity below the Kolmogorov scales, satisfies it.

The complete description of the field is the \(N\)-point, \(N\)-time joint PDF

\[ f_N\bigl(\mathbf V^{(1)},\mathbf x^{(1)},t^{(1)};\ \mathbf V^{(2)},\mathbf x^{(2)},t^{(2)};\ \ldots;\ \mathbf V^{(N)},\mathbf x^{(N)},t^{(N)}\bigr), \tag{8.23} \]

the joint PDF of the three velocity components at \(N\) points and times, for every \(N\). The slide states that it is impossible to determine and that a random velocity field therefore cannot be completely characterized. It also states that turbulent velocity fields are found not to be Gaussian, where a Gaussian field would be completely characterized by its mean and its autocovariance; the section "The stationary Gaussian process" gave the evidence for one component at one point. The remaining sections develop the partial descriptions that are used instead.

Stationarity and homogeneity of a field, and the channel flow L22 6:38

The definitions of the previous sections extend to the field through (8.23). The field is statistically stationary if \(f_N\) is unchanged when every \((\mathbf x^{(n)},t^{(n)})\) is replaced by \((\mathbf x^{(n)},t^{(n)}+t_s)\), and statistically homogeneous if \(f_N\) is unchanged when every \((\mathbf x^{(n)},t^{(n)})\) is replaced by \((\mathbf x^{(n)}+\mathbf x_s,t^{(n)})\):

\[ f_N\bigl(\ldots;\mathbf V^{(n)},\mathbf x^{(n)},t^{(n)}+t_s;\ldots\bigr)=f_N\bigl(\ldots;\mathbf V^{(n)},\mathbf x^{(n)},t^{(n)};\ldots\bigr)=f_N\bigl(\ldots;\mathbf V^{(n)},\mathbf x^{(n)}+\mathbf x_s,t^{(n)};\ldots\bigr), \tag{8.24} \]

the first equality for stationarity and the second for homogeneity, each for all \(N\), all points and times, and all shifts. (The slide writes the shifts as \(T\) and \(\mathbf X\).) Stationarity is invariance under a shift in time; homogeneity is invariance under a shift in position. They are independent properties: a flow can have either without the other.

If the velocity field \(\mathbf U\) is homogeneous, then with \(N=1\) its mean \(\langle\mathbf U\rangle\) is the same at every point: the mean is uniform. In a frame moving with that uniform velocity, the mean is zero, and the fluctuations, which a change to a uniformly moving frame does not change, are the whole velocity. The slide's definition of homogeneous turbulence is less restrictive: only the fluctuating field \(\mathbf u\) is required to be homogeneous, and the mean may vary with position. The definition does not exclude, for example, a mean velocity that increases linearly across the flow with fluctuations whose statistics are the same at every point; whether the equations of motion admit such a flow is a question for the chapters on the Reynolds equations, not for the definition. The slide notes that homogeneous turbulence can be achieved in wind-tunnel experiments and is the simplest class of flows to study by direct numerical simulation.

Statistically two- and one-dimensional flows. The slide's example is a channel between two parallel walls (its Figure 3.25), with the flow direction \(x_1\), the wall-normal direction \(x_2\), the spanwise direction \(x_3\), the distance \(h\) between the walls and the spanwise width \(b\) (the slide's symbols; \(h\) is the full height, where chapter 4 wrote \(d\) for a half-width, and it is a length here, unrelated to the time step \(h\) of (8.14) and of the program's last three lines). For a large aspect ratio, \(b/h\gg1\), and away from the end walls, \(|x_3|/b\ll1\), the statistics vary very little with \(x_3\), and the flow is statistically two-dimensional. Sufficiently far downstream, \(x_1/h\gg1\), the flow becomes statistically fully developed, and the statistics are independent of both \(x_1\) and \(x_3\): the flow is statistically one-dimensional, and with a steady supply it is also stationary. The statistics then depend on \(x_2\) only, while the velocity field itself remains three-dimensional and time dependent. "Very little" and "sufficiently far" make these approximations, not exact properties, of a channel of finite width and length.

A simulation can impose the idealization exactly. Lee and Moser (2014) simulate turbulent channel flow with periodic boundary conditions in the streamwise and spanwise directions and no-slip walls, in a domain \(8\pi\delta\) long and \(3\pi\delta\) wide, where \(\delta\) is the channel half-width, \(h/2\) in the slide's notation (here \(\delta\) is a length, not a delta function; arXiv preprint p. 3); they write the mean velocity as a function \(U(y)\) of the wall-normal coordinate \(y\) only (p. 4). Their largest case, at friction Reynolds number 5186, was run for a time of \(7.80\,\delta/u_\tau\), not counting the initial transient, with \(u_\tau\) the friction velocity (Table 1, p. 4). They estimate the standard deviation of the sampling error as less than 0.2 % for the mean velocity, and for the variances and covariances less than 0.5 % near the wall and 3 % in the outer region (p. 4). They test stationarity through the momentum balance, whose total stress must be linear in the wall distance in a stationary channel (p. 5). The finite-record error of the section "Time averages" is thus a quantity that such a simulation reports. In this chapter's reading, homogeneity in two directions is what makes averages over planes parallel to the walls available in addition to averages over time.

The running-case jet is not homogeneous: its statistics change with distance from the axis and with distance from the nozzle. At the station it is assumed stationary, so its statistics are estimated by time averages at fixed points, not by averages over space.

Isotropy L22 13:10

A statistically homogeneous field is, by definition, statistically invariant under translations, that is, under shifts of the origin of the coordinate system. If it is also statistically invariant under rotations and reflections of the coordinate system, it is statistically isotropic. In terms of (8.23): \(f_N\) is unchanged when the positions and the velocities are expressed in any coordinate system obtained from the original one by a rotation or a reflection of the axes. (The slide writes the transformed position and velocity with an overbar; this book reserves the overbar for the filtered velocity of the simulation chapters. Chapter 6 writes such a transformation with the direction cosines \(a_{ij}\).)

The lecture's example is the equality of the three variances. The full consequence for the Reynolds stresses is

\[ \langle u_iu_j\rangle=\tfrac23k\,\delta_{ij},\qquad k\equiv\tfrac12\langle u_iu_i\rangle, \tag{8.25} \]

where \(k\) is the turbulent kinetic energy per unit mass, \(\delta_{ij}\) is the Kronecker delta (1 if \(i=j\), 0 otherwise) and repeated indices are summed. Two transformations prove it. The reflection \(x_2\to-x_2\) changes the sign of \(u_2\) and leaves \(u_1\), \(u_3\) unchanged, so invariance gives \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle=\langle u_1(-u_2)\rangle\), hence \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle=0\); reflections of the other axes remove the other off-diagonal entries. The rotation by \(90^\circ\) about \(x_3\) maps the components \((u_1,u_2)\) to \((u_2,-u_1)\), so invariance gives \(\langle u_1^2\rangle=\langle u_2^2\rangle\); a rotation about \(x_1\) gives \(\langle u_2^2\rangle=\langle u_3^2\rangle\). The three equal variances sum to \(2k\), so each is \(\tfrac23k\).

The slide states that the concept is extremely important in turbulence, that hundreds of wind-tunnel experiments have been performed on it, and that much of turbulence theory depends on it. Its scope must be kept. Isotropy, as the slide defines it, is a property of a homogeneous field, and a flow in which (8.25) fails, through unequal variances or a nonzero \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle\), is not isotropic; away from a symmetry axis, a flow with a mean shear is of that kind (chapter 9 develops the shear stress). On the axis of the running-case jet, the reflection symmetry of chapter 7 gives \(\langle u_1u_2\rangle=0\), which is also what (8.25) gives, but nothing in the geometry of a jet requires \(\langle u_1^2\rangle=\langle u_2^2\rangle\); a measurement of the two root-mean-square values is the test, and an isotropic formula applied to the jet without it is an assumption. Kolmogorov's local isotropy (chapter 5) is a weaker statement, about the statistics of velocity differences over small separations only.

The two-point correlation and the integral length scale L22 15:03

The simplest statistic that contains information about the spatial structure of the field is, in the slide's words, the two-point, one-time autocovariance,

\[ R_{ij}(\mathbf r,\mathbf x,t)\equiv\langle u_i(\mathbf x,t)\,u_j(\mathbf x+\mathbf r,t)\rangle, \tag{8.26} \]

also called the two-point correlation. The separation vector \(\mathbf r\) plays the role that the lag \(s\) played in (8.4), and the indices \(i\), \(j\) select the two velocity components. At \(\mathbf r=0\), \(R_{ij}\) is the Reynolds-stress matrix of (8.22). For homogeneous turbulence, the two-point correlation is independent of \(\mathbf x\) by (8.24) with \(N=2\), and it is written \(R_{ij}(\mathbf r,t)\). It then has the symmetry

the counterpart of the evenness (8.5), proved the same way: \(R_{ij}(\mathbf r,t)=\langle u_j(\mathbf x+\mathbf r,t)u_i(\mathbf x,t)\rangle\), and replacing \(\mathbf x\) by \(\mathbf x-\mathbf r\), which homogeneity allows, gives \(\langle u_j(\mathbf x,t)u_i(\mathbf x-\mathbf r,t)\rangle=R_{ji}(-\mathbf r,t)\). For \(i=j\) it states that \(R_{11}\), \(R_{22}\) and \(R_{33}\) are even functions of \(\mathbf r\). From the two-point correlation the slide defines integral length scales, for instance

\[ L_{11}\equiv\frac{1}{R_{11}(0,\mathbf x,t)}\int_0^\infty R_{11}(\mathbf e_1r,\mathbf x,t)\,dr, \tag{8.28} \]

where \(\mathbf e_1\) is the unit vector in the \(x_1\) direction and \(r\) is the scalar separation along it (the slide prints \(d\mathbf r\) in bold; the integration variable is the scalar \(r\)). \(L_{11}\) is the longitudinal integral scale: it correlates the \(x_1\) component of the velocity at two points separated along \(x_1\). It is the spatial counterpart of (8.6), the area under the normalized correlation, and it carries the same assumption that the integral converges. Chapter 5 used it, in words, as the length in McComb and co-authors' measured dissipation constant; (8.28) is the definition chapter 5 pointed to.

A single probe cannot measure (8.26). The hot-wire on the jet centreline gives the autocovariance in time at one point, \(R(s)\) of (8.4), and the spatial correlation at one time needs two probes at a variable separation or a measurement of the whole field. Relating a time correlation at a fixed point to a spatial correlation is the purpose of Taylor's hypothesis, which a later chapter states with its conditions.

Wavenumber spectra: the velocity-spectrum tensor and the energy spectrum L22 18:28

The spatial counterpart of a frequency is a wavenumber. The slide's spatial Fourier mode is \(e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}=\cos(\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x)+i\sin(\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x)\), where \(\boldsymbol\kappa=(\kappa_1,\kappa_2,\kappa_3)\) is the wavenumber vector, in rad/m. The mode is constant on every plane perpendicular to \(\boldsymbol\kappa\) and varies sinusoidally in the direction of \(\boldsymbol\kappa\), with wavelength \(2\pi/|\boldsymbol\kappa|\) (the slide writes the wavelength \(l\); this book reserves \(\ell\) for an eddy size). The magnitude is \(\kappa=|\boldsymbol\kappa|=(\kappa_1^2+\kappa_2^2+\kappa_3^2)^{1/2}\). The lecture uses \(k\) and \(\kappa\) for the wavenumber interchangeably; this book writes \(\kappa\), because \(k\) is the turbulent kinetic energy.

For homogeneous turbulence, the velocity-spectrum tensor \(\Phi_{ij}\) and the two-point correlation are a Fourier pair:

\[ \Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)=\frac1{(2\pi)^3}\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}R_{ij}(\mathbf r,t)\,d\mathbf r, \tag{8.29} \] \[ R_{ij}(\mathbf r,t)=\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}\Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)\,d\boldsymbol\kappa, \tag{8.30} \]

where \(d\mathbf r=dr_1\,dr_2\,dr_3\) and \(d\boldsymbol\kappa=d\kappa_1\,d\kappa_2\,d\kappa_3\). The normalization differs from that of the frequency pair (8.8)–(8.9): here the whole factor \((2\pi)^{-3}\) sits on the forward transform, and the inverse integrates over all wavenumber vectors, positive and negative components alike, so \(\Phi_{ij}\) is two-sided. Both conventions are those of the slides. \(\Phi_{ij}\) has the units of \(R_{ij}\) times a volume, \(\mathrm{m^5/s^2}\).

Setting \(\mathbf r=0\) in (8.30), as \(s=0\) was set in (8.9), gives

\[ R_{ij}(0,t)=\langle u_iu_j\rangle=\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)\,d\boldsymbol\kappa: \tag{8.31} \]

each Reynolds stress is distributed over wavenumber vectors. The slide observes that the two-point correlation and the spectrum carry two different kinds of directional information. The dependence of \(R_{ij}\) on \(\mathbf r\) and of \(\Phi_{ij}\) on \(\boldsymbol\kappa\) describes the direction of a separation or of a wave; the indices \(i\), \(j\) describe the directions of the velocity components. The nine components of \(\Phi_{ij}\) correspond to the nine of \(R_{ij}\). For \(i=j\), \(R_{ii}\) is even by (8.27), so the sine part of \(e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}\) integrates to zero in (8.29), and the trace \(\Phi_{ii}\) is real.

The energy spectrum. The slide's useful scalar is the energy-spectrum function

\[ E(\kappa,t)\equiv\iiint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tfrac12\Phi_{ii}(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)\,\delta\bigl(|\boldsymbol\kappa|-\kappa\bigr)\,d\boldsymbol\kappa, \tag{8.32} \]

where \(\delta\) is the Dirac delta of chapter 7 (equation (7.13)), here with the argument \(|\boldsymbol\kappa|-\kappa\). (The lecture calls it a Kronecker delta; the slide's symbol has a continuous argument, and the Kronecker delta \(\delta_{ij}\) of (8.25) is a different object.) Two operations happen in (8.32). The trace \(\tfrac12\Phi_{ii}=\tfrac12(\Phi_{11}+\Phi_{22}+\Phi_{33})\) keeps only the products of a component with itself, as in the kinetic energy \(\tfrac12(u_1^2+u_2^2+u_3^2)\), and drops the mixed components. The delta function keeps only the wavenumber vectors on the spherical shell of radius \(\kappa\) and integrates over the shell, so \(E\) depends on the magnitude \(\kappa\) only. The slide's description is that \(E\) may be viewed as \(\Phi\) stripped of all directional information. \(E(\kappa,t)\) has units of \(\mathrm{m^3/s^2}\): energy per unit mass per unit wavenumber.

Integrating (8.32) over \(\kappa\) from 0 to \(\infty\), and using \(\int_0^\infty\delta(|\boldsymbol\kappa|-\kappa)\,d\kappa=1\) for every \(\boldsymbol\kappa\ne0\), returns the integral of \(\tfrac12\Phi_{ii}\) over all wavenumber vectors, which is \(\tfrac12R_{ii}(0,t)\) by (8.31):

\[ \int_0^\infty E(\kappa,t)\,d\kappa=\tfrac12R_{ii}(0,t)=\tfrac12\langle u_iu_i\rangle=k. \tag{8.33} \]

So \(E(\kappa,t)\,d\kappa\) is the contribution to the turbulent kinetic energy from all modes with \(\kappa\le|\boldsymbol\kappa|\lt\kappa+d\kappa\), the wavenumber counterpart of (8.11). When \(\Phi_{ii}\) depends on \(\boldsymbol\kappa\) only through its magnitude, as in isotropic turbulence, the shell integral can be done in spherical coordinates, \(d\boldsymbol\kappa=|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2\,d|\boldsymbol\kappa|\,d\Omega\) with \(d\Omega\) the element of solid angle: the delta function sets \(|\boldsymbol\kappa|=\kappa\), and the solid angle contributes \(4\pi\). Hence

\[ E(\kappa,t)=2\pi\kappa^2\,\Phi_{ii}(\kappa,t)\qquad\text{(isotropic }\Phi_{ii}). \tag{8.34} \]

The factor \(\kappa^2\) is the area of the shell, \(4\pi\kappa^2\), times \(\tfrac12\). Units check: \(\kappa^2\Phi_{ii}\) has units \(\mathrm{m^{-2}}\times\mathrm{m^5/s^2}=\mathrm{m^3/s^2}\).

The two spectra of this chapter must not be confused. \(E(\omega)\) is a one-sided frequency spectrum of one velocity component at one point, obtained from a record in time; \(E(\kappa,t)\) is the energy spectrum of the whole field at one time, obtained from spatial correlations. They share a letter, so the argument is always written. A hot-wire record gives \(E(\omega)\); a statement about \(E(\kappa,t)\) from that record needs Taylor's hypothesis, and for the full energy spectrum also isotropy. The resolution requirement of chapter 5, \(\kappa_{\max}\eta\), is stated in wavenumbers because a simulation represents a field in space.

Executable processes: two processes with the same autocorrelation

The program below computes the two processes used in this chapter and every number quoted from it. Its inputs, set in the code, are the integral time scale \(\bar\tau=1\), the standard deviation \(\sigma_u=1\), the time step \(\Delta t=0.05\), the record length \(T=50\), the number of records \(M=4000\) and the seed 8 of NumPy's random-number generator. The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process is computed by the recursion \(u_{n+1}=a\,u_n+(1-a^2)^{1/2}\xi_n\), with \(a=e^{-\Delta t/\bar\tau}\) and independent standard Gaussian numbers \(\xi_n\). This recursion gives \(\langle u_{n+j}u_n\rangle=a^j\langle u_n^2\rangle\) and, started from a standard Gaussian value, keeps the variance equal to 1 at every step, so the process is stationary from the first step with the exponential autocorrelation (8.7) at the grid times. The jump process replaces its value in each step with probability \(1-a\) by a new standard Gaussian value, which gives the same autocorrelation \(a^j\) (section "What the autocorrelation distinguishes").

For each process the program prints one-time moments, the estimated autocorrelation (8.4) at three lags and its trapezoidal integral (8.6) from 0 to 5, the variance of the 4000 time averages (8.15), the record-averaged periodogram (8.12) at three frequencies and the flatness of the one-step increment; it ends with the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck difference quotient of (8.14). Two assertions per process check results the text derives: the estimated autocorrelation is within 0.01 of \(e^{-s}\) at the three lags, and the variance of the time averages is within 10 % of (8.18). The function ou_and_jump raises ValueError for fewer than two steps or no records. The program reads no files, writes only to standard output, requires NumPy, and runs in about 10 s. The teaching simplifications are explicit: discrete time steps instead of continuous time, dimensionless units, one seed, and statistical estimates whose sampling errors (about 1 % to 2 % for the quoted variances and spectra) are stated in the text rather than computed.

import numpy as np

TAU, SIGMA = 1.0, 1.0        # integral time scale, standard deviation
DT, T, M = 0.05, 50.0, 4000  # time step, record length, number of records
N = int(round(T/DT))         # samples per record
A = np.exp(-DT/TAU)          # autocorrelation at one time step


def ou_and_jump(rng, n_steps, m):
    """Return two (n_steps, m) arrays: an OU process and a jump process.

    Both start in their stationary state, both have a Gaussian one-time
    PDF with standard deviation SIGMA, and both have the autocorrelation
    A**k at the lag k*DT.
    """
    if n_steps < 2 or m < 1:
        raise ValueError("need n_steps >= 2 and m >= 1")
    ou = np.empty((n_steps, m))
    jump = np.empty((n_steps, m))
    ou[0] = rng.standard_normal(m)
    jump[0] = rng.standard_normal(m)
    b = np.sqrt(1 - A**2)
    for n in range(1, n_steps):
        ou[n] = A*ou[n - 1] + b*rng.standard_normal(m)
        new = rng.random(m) < 1 - A
        jump[n] = np.where(new, rng.standard_normal(m), jump[n - 1])
    return SIGMA*ou, SIGMA*jump


def rho_hat(x, k):
    """Autocorrelation at lag k*DT, averaged over time and records."""
    u = x - x.mean()
    return np.mean(u[:N - k]*u[k:])/np.mean(u*u)


def flatness(v):
    v = v - v.mean()
    return np.mean(v**4)/np.mean(v**2)**2


rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
ou, jump = ou_and_jump(rng, N, M)
t = DT*np.arange(N)
for name, x in (("OU", ou), ("jump", jump)):
    print(f"{name}: one-time mean={x.mean():+.3f} var={x.var():.3f} "
          f"flatness={flatness(x):.2f}")
    for s in (0.5, 1.0, 2.0):
        k = int(round(s/DT))
        r = rho_hat(x, k)
        assert abs(r - A**k) < 0.01
        print(f"  rho({s}) = {r:.3f} (exp(-s) = {np.exp(-s):.3f})")
    rho = np.array([rho_hat(x, k) for k in range(int(5/DT) + 1)])
    tau_est = DT*(rho.sum() - 0.5*(rho[0] + rho[-1]))
    print(f"  integral of rho from 0 to 5: {tau_est:.3f}")
    # Variance of the time average over one record of length T.
    var_T = x.mean(axis=0).var()
    exact = 2*TAU*SIGMA**2/T*(1 - TAU/T*(1 - np.exp(-T/TAU)))
    assert abs(var_T/exact - 1) < 0.1
    print(f"  var of time average={var_T:.4f}  (8.18) gives {exact:.4f}  "
          f"sigma^2/N gives {SIGMA**2/N:.4f}")
    # Spectrum from the record-averaged periodogram, convention (8.8).
    for w in (0.5, 1.0, 4.0):
        X = DT*(np.exp(-1j*w*t) @ x)
        E_hat = np.mean(np.abs(X)**2)/(np.pi*T)
        E_true = 2*SIGMA**2*TAU/(np.pi*(1 + (w*TAU)**2))
        print(f"  E({w}) = {E_hat:.4f} (8.10 gives {E_true:.4f})")
    dx = x[1:] - x[:-1]
    print(f"  flatness of the increment over DT: {flatness(dx):.1f}")

# The OU process has no derivative: difference quotients grow as h -> 0.
for k in (4, 2, 1):
    h = k*DT
    q = (ou[k:] - ou[:-k])/h
    print(f"h={h:.2f}: var of OU difference quotient={q.var():.1f} "
          f"(2(1-exp(-h))/h^2 = {2*(1 - np.exp(-h))/h**2:.1f})")

What the description establishes and what it does not

The definitions of this chapter are exact mathematics, valid whenever the stated integrals and derivatives exist, and the derived results, from the properties (8.5) to the shell formula (8.34), follow from them without approximation except where a long-time limit is marked, as in (8.17) and (8.19).

Each derived result carries conditions. Stationarity, homogeneity, isotropy and ergodicity are assumptions about a flow and an experiment, with different domains: the running-case jet is assumed stationary and is neither homogeneous nor known to be isotropic. The integral time scale and the integral length scale exist only if the correlation decays fast enough, and (8.17) needs \(\bar\tau\) to be finite and positive. Equation (8.14) separates processes that have derivatives from processes that do not. The spectrum (8.8) is a bounded continuous function whenever the autocovariance is absolutely integrable, the condition this chapter assumes wherever it writes \(E(\omega)\); without it, \(E\) may still be a function (the example \(\sin s/s\) of the frequency-spectrum section) or no function at all (the constant and periodic processes). The convention of every spectrum must be stated: (8.8) is one-sided with angular frequency, (8.29) is two-sided in three dimensions, and a spectrum per hertz differs from (8.8) by \(2\pi\).

The \(N\)-point, \(N\)-time PDF that would determine the field is not available, and the second-order descriptions that replace it leave fourth-order statistics open (processes (a) and (c)).

The running-case record lengths rest on the assumed integral time scale (section "Time averages"), and a measured autocorrelation would replace the assumption. The computed processes check the formulas and demonstrate two mechanisms, the error of a time average and the limits of second-order statistics; they are not models of a turbulent velocity. The chapters that follow use these objects: the Reynolds stresses in the mean momentum equation, the energy spectrum in the description of the energy cascade, and the spectrum tensor in the analysis of Fourier modes.

Exercises

Exercise 1 (compute). The hot-wire on the jet centreline (\(\langle U_1\rangle=8\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(\sigma_u=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\)) records for \(T\) seconds at 20 kHz. Assume an exponential autocorrelation with \(\bar\tau=0.01\ \mathrm s\). (a) Find the standard error of the time-averaged velocity for \(T=1\) s and \(T=10\) s, from (8.17) and from (8.18). (b) How long must the record be for a standard error of 0.02 m/s? How many samples does it contain, and how many independent samples is it worth? (c) An experimenter halves the sampling rate to save storage. What happens to the standard error of the mean, and what is lost?

Solution

(a) By (8.17), \(\mathrm{var}=2\bar\tau\sigma_u^2/T=0.08/T\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\). For \(T=1\) s, 0.080, standard error 0.283 m/s; (8.18) multiplies the variance by \(1-(0.01/1)(1-e^{-100})=0.990\), standard error 0.281 m/s. For \(T=10\) s, 0.0080, standard error 0.0894 m/s; (8.18) multiplies by \(1-0.001=0.999\), 0.0894 m/s. The long-time form is accurate to better than \(\bar\tau/T\) in the variance.

(b) \(T=0.08/0.02^2=200\ \mathrm s\). At 20 kHz the record contains \(4\times10^{6}\) samples and is worth \(T/(2\bar\tau)=10^{4}\) independent samples. Treating the \(4\times10^{6}\) samples as independent would give a standard error of \(2/2000=0.001\ \mathrm{m/s}\), 20 times too small.

(c) The standard error of the mean does not change as long as the sampling interval remains much shorter than \(\bar\tau\): at 10 kHz the interval is 0.1 ms, 1 % of \(\bar\tau\), and (8.16), which depends only on \(T\) and \(R\), is unchanged to that accuracy (Beyhaghi, Alimo and Bewley 2018, p. 3, make the same point for sampling intervals of order \(2\bar\tau/10\)). What is lost is time resolution: the interval rises from \(0.12\tau_\eta\) to \(0.23\tau_\eta\), which reduces the accuracy of measured derivatives and of the high-frequency spectrum. The two requirements, a long record for the mean and a short interval for the small scales, are independent.

Exercise 2 (counterexample). In each run of an experiment the nozzle velocity is set slightly differently, so that the centreline velocity is \(U(t)=A+w(t)\), where \(A\) is a random variable that is constant during a run, with mean 8 m/s and variance \(\sigma_A^2\), and \(w(t)\) is a zero-mean stationary process independent of \(A\), with variance \(\sigma_w^2\) and autocorrelation \(e^{-|s|/\tau_c}\), \(\tau_c\gt0\) a correlation time. (a) Show that \(U(t)\) is stationary and find \(R(s)\), \(\rho(s)\) and \(\bar\tau\). (b) Find \(\mathrm{var}(\langle U\rangle_T)\) as \(T\to\infty\). (c) To what does the time average of one record converge? Is the process ergodic in the mean? (d) What experimental design recovers \(\langle U\rangle\)?

Solution

(a) A shift of the time origin leaves \(A\) unchanged and leaves the statistics of \(w\) unchanged; because the two are independent, every \(N\)-time PDF of \(U\) is unchanged, and (8.3) holds. The mean is \(\langle U\rangle=8\ \mathrm{m/s}\), the fluctuation is \(u=(A-8)+w\), and by independence the cross terms have zero mean: \(R(s)=\sigma_A^2+\sigma_w^2e^{-|s|/\tau_c}\). Then \(\rho(s)=(\sigma_A^2+\sigma_w^2e^{-|s|/\tau_c})/(\sigma_A^2+\sigma_w^2)\), which tends to \(\sigma_A^2/(\sigma_A^2+\sigma_w^2)\gt0\) as \(s\to\infty\). The integral (8.6) diverges: \(\bar\tau\) is infinite.

(b) By (8.16), the constant part of \(R\) contributes \((2/T)\sigma_A^2\int_0^T(1-s/T)\,ds=\sigma_A^2\), and the exponential part contributes a term that tends to zero like \(2\sigma_w^2\tau_c/T\). So \(\mathrm{var}(\langle U\rangle_T)\to\sigma_A^2\), not zero.

(c) \(\langle U\rangle_T=A+\langle w\rangle_T\), and \(\langle w\rangle_T\to0\) in mean square because \(w\) satisfies the condition of the ergodicity paragraph. So the time average of a record converges to the value of \(A\) in that run, not to \(\langle U\rangle=8\ \mathrm{m/s}\). The process is stationary and not ergodic in the mean: a long record measures one run's setting.

(d) Repeat the experiment, resetting the nozzle each time, and average the run means; the variance of that average is about \(\sigma_A^2/n\) for \(n\) runs plus the small time-averaging term. The general lesson: a slowly varying or run-dependent control parameter adds a constant to the autocovariance, and no record length removes its effect. A spectrum estimated from one record would show the same thing as a large value near \(\omega=0\), by (8.19).

Exercise 3 (check an assumption). Consider a stationary process with variance \(\sigma_u^2\) and autocorrelation \(\rho(s)=(1-|s|/\tau_c)e^{-|s|/\tau_c}\), \(\tau_c\gt0\) a correlation time. (a) Show that \(\bar\tau=0\). (b) Compute \(E(\omega)\) from (8.8), and show that it is non-negative, that it vanishes at \(\omega=0\), and that its integral is \(\sigma_u^2\). (c) Compute \(\mathrm{var}(\langle U\rangle_T)\) for \(T\gg\tau_c\). What does (8.17) predict, and what is wrong with the prediction? (d) What kind of signal has such an autocorrelation?

Solution

(a) With \(x=s/\tau_c\), \(\bar\tau=\tau_c\int_0^\infty(1-x)e^{-x}dx=\tau_c(1-1)=0\). The positive correlation at small lags is cancelled by the negative correlation (\(\rho\lt0\) for \(s\gt\tau_c\)) at larger lags.

(b) With \(\int_0^\infty e^{-s/\tau_c}\cos\omega s\,ds=\tau_c/(1+X^2)\) and \(\int_0^\infty se^{-s/\tau_c}\cos\omega s\,ds=\tau_c^2(1-X^2)/(1+X^2)^2\), where \(X=\omega\tau_c\), (8.8) gives \(E(\omega)=(2\sigma_u^2/\pi)[\tau_c/(1+X^2)-\tau_c(1-X^2)/(1+X^2)^2]=(4\sigma_u^2\tau_c/\pi)X^2/(1+X^2)^2\). It is non-negative, as (8.12) requires, and zero at \(\omega=0\), as (8.13) requires for \(\bar\tau=0\). Its integral is \((4\sigma_u^2\tau_c/\pi)(1/\tau_c)\int_0^\infty X^2/(1+X^2)^2dX=(4\sigma_u^2/\pi)(\pi/4)=\sigma_u^2\).

(c) From (8.16), with \(X_T=T/\tau_c\): \(\int_0^T\rho\,ds=Te^{-X_T}\) and \(\int_0^Ts\rho\,ds=\tau_c^2[(X_T^2+X_T+1)e^{-X_T}-1]\). For \(T\gg\tau_c\) the exponentials vanish, and \(\mathrm{var}(\langle U\rangle_T)\to(2\sigma_u^2/T)(\tau_c^2/T)=2\sigma_u^2\tau_c^2/T^2\). Equation (8.17) predicts zero, which is the correct leading coefficient of \(1/T\) and the wrong answer: the error decays as \(1/T^2\), and its size is set by the next term, which (8.17) drops. That is why the practice problem of Exercise 9 assumes that \(\bar\tau\) exists and is positive.

(d) A signal whose low-frequency content is suppressed, \(E(0)=0\): for example the time derivative of a stationary process (whose spectrum is \(\omega^2\) times that of the process, by the argument of (8.14)), or a signal that has passed through a filter that removes its mean and slow drifts. A time average of such a signal converges unusually fast.

Exercise 4 (derive). A stationary process has the Gaussian-shaped autocorrelation \(\rho(s)=\exp[-\pi s^2/(4\bar\tau^2)]\). (a) Show that its integral time scale is \(\bar\tau\). (b) Compute \(E(\omega)\) and check (8.11) and (8.13). (c) Compute \(\langle\dot u^2\rangle\) from (8.14) in two ways. (d) Compare with the exponential autocorrelation of the same \(\bar\tau\), and evaluate \(\langle\dot u^2\rangle^{1/2}\) for the running case (\(\sigma_u=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(\bar\tau=0.01\ \mathrm s\)).

Solution

(a) With \(\alpha=\pi/(4\bar\tau^2)\), \(\int_0^\infty e^{-\alpha s^2}ds=\tfrac12(\pi/\alpha)^{1/2}=\tfrac12(4\bar\tau^2)^{1/2}=\bar\tau\).

(b) \(\int_0^\infty e^{-\alpha s^2}\cos\omega s\,ds=\tfrac12(\pi/\alpha)^{1/2}e^{-\omega^2/(4\alpha)}=\bar\tau e^{-\omega^2\bar\tau^2/\pi}\), so \(E(\omega)=(2\sigma_u^2\bar\tau/\pi)e^{-\omega^2\bar\tau^2/\pi}\). Then \(\int_0^\infty E\,d\omega=(2\sigma_u^2\bar\tau/\pi)\cdot\tfrac12(\pi\cdot\pi/\bar\tau^2)^{1/2}=\sigma_u^2\), which is (8.11), and \(\pi E(0)/(2\sigma_u^2)=\bar\tau\), which is (8.13). The value \(E(0)\) is the same as for the exponential pair (8.10), because both have the same \(\sigma_u\) and \(\bar\tau\).

(c) \(R(s)=\sigma_u^2e^{-\alpha s^2}\) gives \(R''(0)=-2\alpha\sigma_u^2\), so \(\langle\dot u^2\rangle=2\alpha\sigma_u^2=\pi\sigma_u^2/(2\bar\tau^2)\). From the spectrum: \(\int_0^\infty\omega^2e^{-\omega^2\bar\tau^2/\pi}d\omega=\tfrac14\pi^{1/2}(\pi/\bar\tau^2)^{3/2}=\pi^2/(4\bar\tau^3)\), times \(2\sigma_u^2\bar\tau/\pi\), gives \(\pi\sigma_u^2/(2\bar\tau^2)\). The two agree.

(d) The exponential autocorrelation with the same \(\bar\tau\) has the same variance and the same \(E(0)\) and an infinite \(\langle\dot u^2\rangle\). Two processes can agree in every quantity that this chapter computes from \(\bar\tau\), including the error (8.17) of a time average, and differ in whether they have a derivative. For the running case, \(\langle\dot u^2\rangle=\pi\times4/(2\times10^{-4})=6.3\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{m^2/s^4}\), a root-mean-square acceleration of 251 m/s². This number is a property of the assumed shape of \(\rho\), not a prediction for the jet: the derivative of a turbulent velocity is set by the small scales, whose time scale \(\tau_\eta=0.43\ \mathrm{ms}\) is much shorter than \(\bar\tau\), and which this smooth model does not contain.

Exercise 5 (judge a claim). A group needs a time-dependent inflow velocity for a simulation. They generate a signal with a Gaussian one-time PDF, the measured mean and variance of the jet velocity, and the measured frequency spectrum, and they claim that "the signal is statistically equivalent to the measured velocity". Judge the claim, using processes (a) and (c) of the lecture. State which properties of the inflow are correct, which are not, and which simulated quantities could be affected.

Solution

The generated signal is process (c) for the measured process (a): a stationary Gaussian process with the measured mean and spectrum. It matches the measured velocity in its one-time PDF (to the extent that the measured PDF is Gaussian) and in every second-order statistic: the autocovariance, \(\bar\tau\), the variance of every derivative (8.14) and the error (8.16) of time averages.

It does not match higher-order multi-time statistics. The slide's comparison shows that the second derivative of the measured velocity has kurtosis 11 and that of the Gaussian process has kurtosis 3; the measured signal has intermittent bursts that the Gaussian one does not. The claim "statistically equivalent" is false; "equivalent in its one-time PDF and second-order statistics" is correct. Simulated quantities that depend on the intermittent, large-amplitude events at the inflow, for example the statistics of large accelerations or of rare peak values, can be affected, and the inflow lacks any coherent structure the measured flow has. Whether the difference matters downstream depends on how quickly the simulated flow generates its own structure; that question cannot be settled by the statistics of this chapter. The precise claim the group can make is the list of statistics matched.

Exercise 6 (derive and judge). In homogeneous isotropic turbulence the trace of the spectrum tensor has the model form \(\Phi_{ii}(\boldsymbol\kappa)=A\exp(-|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2/\kappa_0^2)\), with constants \(A\gt0\) and \(\kappa_0\gt0\). (a) Find \(E(\kappa)\) from (8.34) and the wavenumber of its maximum. (b) Find \(k\) by (8.33) and check it against (8.31) directly. (c) A student proposes to obtain \(E(\kappa)\) from the hot-wire record of the running case. List what the proposal assumes.

Solution

(a) \(E(\kappa)=2\pi A\kappa^2e^{-\kappa^2/\kappa_0^2}\). Its derivative is proportional to \(2\kappa-2\kappa^3/\kappa_0^2\), which vanishes at \(\kappa=\kappa_0\): the energy is concentrated near the wavenumber \(\kappa_0\), wavelength \(2\pi/\kappa_0\), even though \(\Phi_{ii}\) itself is largest at \(\boldsymbol\kappa=0\). The factor \(\kappa^2\), the shell area, makes the difference.

(b) \(k=\int_0^\infty2\pi A\kappa^2e^{-\kappa^2/\kappa_0^2}d\kappa=2\pi A\cdot\tfrac14\pi^{1/2}\kappa_0^3=\tfrac12\pi^{3/2}A\kappa_0^3\). Directly from (8.31), \(\langle u_iu_i\rangle=\iiint Ae^{-|\boldsymbol\kappa|^2/\kappa_0^2}d\boldsymbol\kappa=A(\pi^{1/2}\kappa_0)^3=\pi^{3/2}A\kappa_0^3\), a product of three one-dimensional Gaussian integrals, and \(k=\tfrac12\langle u_iu_i\rangle=\tfrac12\pi^{3/2}A\kappa_0^3\). The two agree.

(c) The probe gives \(R(s)\) and \(E(\omega)\) for one component at one point. Obtaining \(E(\kappa)\) assumes, first, that the time record can be converted into a spatial record along the flow direction, which is Taylor's hypothesis (a later chapter), with its conditions on the turbulence intensity \(\sigma_u/\langle U_1\rangle\), here 25 %; second, that the field is homogeneous over the region swept past the probe, which the jet is not; third, that it is isotropic, so that one component along one direction determines \(\Phi_{ii}\) and \(E(\kappa)\); and fourth, that the record is stationary and long enough for the spectrum estimate. On the jet centreline none of the second and third assumptions is guaranteed, and each must be stated as an assumption with its error.

Exercise 7 (derive; adapted from the course practice problems, L59 0:06). If \(u(t)\) is a zero-mean, statistically stationary, differentiable random process with autocovariance \(R(s)\) and autocorrelation function \(\rho(s)\), show that (1) \(u(t)\) and \(\dot u(t)=du/dt\) are uncorrelated, and (2) \(u(t)\) and \(\ddot u(t)=d^2u/dt^2\) are negatively correlated. (As on the slide, two functions \(a(t)\), \(b(t)\) are called negatively correlated, positively correlated or uncorrelated if \(\langle ab\rangle\lt0\), \(\gt0\) or \(=0\).)

Solution

(1) The mean of a derivative is the derivative of the mean, so \(\langle u\dot u\rangle=\langle\tfrac12\,d(u^2)/dt\rangle=\tfrac12\,d\langle u^2\rangle/dt=0\), because \(\langle u^2\rangle\) is constant for a stationary process. In terms of \(R\): \(\langle u(t)\dot u(t+s)\rangle=R'(s)\), and \(R'(0)=0\) because \(R\) is even and differentiable.

(2) \(d\langle u\dot u\rangle/dt=\langle\dot u^2\rangle+\langle u\ddot u\rangle\), and the left side is zero by part (1), so \(\langle u\ddot u\rangle=-\langle\dot u^2\rangle\le0\). The inequality is strict if \(\langle\dot u^2\rangle\gt0\), that is unless \(u\) is constant in time in almost every run; the slide's statement needs this proviso. In terms of \(R\), \(\langle u\ddot u\rangle=R''(0)\), and (8.14) gives \(R''(0)=-\langle\dot u^2\rangle\). For the Gaussian-shaped autocorrelation of Exercise 4, \(\langle u\ddot u\rangle=-\pi\sigma_u^2/(2\bar\tau^2)\); for the exponential autocorrelation the hypothesis "differentiable" fails and neither part applies.

Exercise 8 (derive; adapted from the course practice problems, T1 40:28). Show that the autocovariance \(B(s)\equiv\langle\dot u(t)\dot u(t+s)\rangle\) of the process \(\dot u(t)\) is \(B(s)=-d^2R(s)/ds^2\), where \(R(s)=\langle u(t)u(t+s)\rangle\) and \(u\) is stationary and twice differentiable.

Solution

Differentiate \(R(s)=\langle u(t)u(t+s)\rangle\) with respect to \(s\) at fixed \(t\): \(R'(s)=\langle u(t)\dot u(t+s)\rangle\). This value does not depend on \(t\), so it can be written with \(t\) replaced by \(t-s\): \(R'(s)=\langle u(t-s)\dot u(t)\rangle\). Differentiate again with respect to \(s\), now at fixed \(t\): \(d u(t-s)/ds=-\dot u(t-s)\), so \(R''(s)=-\langle\dot u(t-s)\dot u(t)\rangle\). By stationarity \(\langle\dot u(t-s)\dot u(t)\rangle=\langle\dot u(t)\dot u(t+s)\rangle=B(s)\), and \(B(s)=-R''(s)\). The hinge is the change from differentiating the second factor to differentiating the first, which stationarity permits; differentiating \(\langle u(t)\dot u(t+s)\rangle\) twice in the second factor alone would give \(\langle u(t)\ddot u(t+s)\rangle\), a different quantity that equals \(R''(s)\) and not \(-B(s)\) until stationarity is used. At \(s=0\) the result is (8.14), \(\langle\dot u^2\rangle=-R''(0)\). In spectral form, (8.9) gives \(B(s)=\int_0^\infty\omega^2E(\omega)\cos\omega s\,d\omega\): the spectrum of \(\dot u\) is \(\omega^2E(\omega)\).

Exercise 9 (derive; adapted from the course practice problems, T1 46:59). For a statistically stationary flow, show that

\[ \mathrm{var}\bigl(\langle U(t)\rangle_T\bigr)=\frac{\mathrm{var}(U)}{T^2}\int_0^T\!\!\int_0^T\rho(t-t')\,dt'\,dt, \]

where \(\rho(s)\) is the autocorrelation function of \(U(t)\) and \(\langle U(t)\rangle_T\) is the time average (8.15). (The slide writes \(s\) for the second time variable; here it is \(t'\), because \(s\) is the lag.) Assuming that the integral time scale \(\bar\tau\) exists and is positive, obtain the long-time result \(\mathrm{var}(\langle U(t)\rangle_T)\sim(2\bar\tau/T)\,\mathrm{var}(U)\). Then, for the exponential autocorrelation, find the record length above which the long-time result is within 10 % of the exact variance.

Solution

\(\langle U\rangle_T-\langle U\rangle=T^{-1}\int_0^Tu(t)\,dt\), because the mean is constant. Its variance is the mean of its square, \(T^{-2}\int_0^T\int_0^T\langle u(t)u(t')\rangle\,dt'\,dt\). By stationarity \(\langle u(t)u(t')\rangle=R(t'-t)=\mathrm{var}(U)\,\rho(t-t')\), using the evenness of \(\rho\), which gives the stated double integral. For the long-time result, substitute \(s=t-t'\): for fixed \(s\in(-T,T)\) the pairs in the square with that difference form a segment of length \(T-|s|\), so the double integral is \(\int_{-T}^{T}(T-|s|)\rho(s)\,ds=2\int_0^T(T-s)\rho(s)\,ds\), which is (8.16). Hence \(\mathrm{var}=(2\,\mathrm{var}(U)/T)[\int_0^T\rho\,ds-T^{-1}\int_0^Ts\rho\,ds]\). As \(T\to\infty\) the first integral tends to \(\bar\tau\). The second term tends to zero only if \(T^{-1}\int_0^Ts\rho(s)\,ds\to0\), a condition the slide does not state; it holds when \(\int_0^\infty s|\rho|\,ds\) is finite. With it, \(\mathrm{var}\sim2\bar\tau\,\mathrm{var}(U)/T\), and the assumption \(\bar\tau\gt0\) makes this leading term nonzero (Exercise 3 shows the case \(\bar\tau=0\)).

For \(\rho=e^{-|s|/\bar\tau}\), (8.18) gives the ratio of the exact variance to the long-time form as \(1-(\bar\tau/T)(1-e^{-T/\bar\tau})\). The long-time form overestimates the exact variance by the factor \(1/[1-(\bar\tau/T)(1-e^{-T/\bar\tau})]\), which is at most 1.1 when \((\bar\tau/T)(1-e^{-T/\bar\tau})\le1/11\); since \(1-e^{-T/\bar\tau}\) is within \(5\times10^{-5}\) of 1 at \(T=11\bar\tau\), the condition is \(T\ge11\bar\tau\) to the accuracy shown. For the running case, \(11\bar\tau=0.11\ \mathrm s\): any record long enough to be useful for the mean is far beyond it.
