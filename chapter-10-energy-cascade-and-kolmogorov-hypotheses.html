The energy cascade and Kolmogorov's hypotheses: which small-scale statistics are universal, and when

A probe at a station in a laboratory air jet and a probe in the atmospheric boundary layer record turbulent velocities whose smallest eddies differ in size by a factor of 42 (79 \(\mu\mathrm m\) against 3.3 mm) and whose largest eddies differ by a factor of \(10^{4}\) (0.1 m against 1 km). A model of the small scales calibrated in one flow, for example a model of the motions smaller than the grid of a simulation, is useful in the other only if the small-scale statistics of the two flows are the same function after a rescaling. Kolmogorov's hypotheses of 1941 state that they are: at sufficiently high Reynolds number, the statistics of the small scales depend only on the viscosity and the dissipation rate, and, in an intermediate range of sizes, on the dissipation rate alone. This chapter states the three hypotheses with their conditions, derives their consequences for the velocity and time scales of eddies of each size, and computes what "sufficiently high" means for the two flows: by the lecture's demarcation lengths, the jet has an inertial subrange 0.55 decades wide and the atmospheric layer one 2.9 decades wide.

The objects of the chapter and the running case

The formal model of this chapter is a turbulent flow regarded as a collection of motions of different sizes. A flow as a whole has a characteristic velocity \(\mathcal U\) and a characteristic length \(\mathcal L\), and the Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}=\mathcal U\mathcal L/\nu\), where \(\nu\) is the kinematic viscosity in \(\mathrm{m^2/s}\). An eddy of size \(\ell\) has a characteristic velocity \(u(\ell)\), a time scale \(\tau(\ell)\equiv\ell/u(\ell)\) and a Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}(\ell)\equiv u(\ell)\ell/\nu\). The largest eddies, the energy-containing eddies, have size \(\ell_0\), velocity \(u_0\equiv u(\ell_0)\), time scale \(\tau_0\equiv\ell_0/u_0\) and Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}_0\equiv u_0\ell_0/\nu\). The slides write \(\mathrm{Re}_0\); chapters 5 and 6 wrote the same quantity \(\mathrm{Re}_\ell\). This chapter uses the slide symbol, because here \(\ell\) is a variable eddy size and \(\mathrm{Re}(\ell)\) is a function of it. The fluctuating velocity is \(\mathbf u=\mathbf U-\langle\mathbf U\rangle\), where \(\mathbf U\) is the full velocity and \(\langle\cdot\rangle\) the mean; the turbulent kinetic energy per unit mass is \(k\equiv\tfrac12\langle u_iu_i\rangle\), with summation over the repeated index; and \(\varepsilon\) is the mean rate of dissipation of turbulent kinetic energy per unit mass (units \(\mathrm{m^2/s^3}\)), defined in chapter 5, (5.13). The symbol \(\sim\) means "of the same order of magnitude"; it fixes a power law and not its coefficient.

The running case is the round air jet of chapters 6–8 at its downstream station. There the root-mean-square axial velocity fluctuation is \(u_0=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\), the energy-containing eddies are about \(\ell_0=0.1\ \mathrm m\) across, and the kinematic viscosity of air is \(\nu=1.4607\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) (chapter 6). These values were chosen in chapter 6; they are not measurements of a particular jet. They give \(\mathrm{Re}_0=1.37\times10^{4}\) and \(\tau_0=0.05\ \mathrm s\). With the dissipation estimate of chapter 5 and unit constant, \(\varepsilon=u_0^3/\ell_0=80\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\), and the Kolmogorov scales defined in chapter 5, (5.14), computed with this \(\nu\) as in chapter 6, are \(\eta=79\ \mu\mathrm m\), \(u_\eta=0.185\ \mathrm{m/s}\) and \(\tau_\eta=0.43\ \mathrm{ms}\) (chapter 5 found \(\eta=81\ \mu\mathrm m\) with its larger \(\nu\)). The contrast case is the neutral atmospheric boundary layer of chapter 5, with \(u_0=0.3\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(\ell_0=10^{3}\ \mathrm m\) and the lecture's \(\nu=1.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) (2.7 % larger than the jet's value): \(\mathrm{Re}_0=2\times10^{7}\), \(\varepsilon=2.7\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\), \(\eta=3.3\ \mathrm{mm}\). The question the chapter answers for both flows is which statistics are the same after rescaling, and over which range of eddy sizes.

Turbulent motions span a range of sizes that widens with the Reynolds number L26 1:28

A laminar flow has one characteristic length. This does not mean that every length in the flow is equal to it. In the lecture's example of a laminar jet from a nozzle 1 cm in diameter, the lengths over which the velocity changes may be 0.6, 0.8 or 1.2 cm, but none is 10 cm and none is 0.01 cm: all are of the order of the characteristic length. A turbulent flow differs in kind. The slide states that turbulent motions range in size from the width of the flow (the slide writes \(\delta\)) to much smaller scales, which become progressively smaller as the Reynolds number increases. The width of the flow is a length such as the diameter of a jet, the distance between the walls of a channel or the diameter of a cylinder in a cross flow.

The smallest scales remain far larger than molecular distances. Chapter 6 (section "The continuum hypothesis and the two descriptions of a flow") showed for the running-case air that the mean free path is 66 nm and that its ratio to \(\eta\), the Knudsen number, is \(8.4\times10^{-4}\), so the continuum equations hold at every scale considered here.

The lecture illustrates the growth of the range with two Reynolds numbers, \(10^{5}\) and \(10^{10}\): the smallest scale of the second flow is much smaller than that of the first. The section "The ratios of the smallest to the largest scales" quantifies the statement. If the smallest length relative to the largest varies as \(\mathrm{Re}_0^{-3/4}\) and \(\mathrm{Re}_0\) is proportional to \(\mathrm{Re}\), the ratio of smallest to largest length is \((10^{5})^{3/4}=5.6\times10^{3}\) times smaller in the second flow. That calculation uses a hypothesis and an empirical scaling, both developed below; this section records only the observation that the range widens.

Richardson's energy cascade: inviscid transfer and viscous dissipation L26 7:50

The slide states the idea of the energy cascade, which it attributes to Richardson (1922): kinetic energy enters the turbulence, through the production mechanism, at the largest scales of motion; this energy is then transferred by inviscid processes to smaller and smaller scales until, at the smallest scales, it is dissipated by viscous action. The lecture's examples of the entry of energy are the inflow through the nozzle of a jet, the flow entering a channel from a tank, and the free stream that approaches a cylinder. In precise terms, the energy of the fluctuations is drawn from the mean flow by the interaction of the fluctuations with the mean velocity gradient; the preceding chapter, "Reynolds equations, closure, and turbulent viscosity", treats that exchange. This chapter needs only its location: it acts on the largest eddies.

Richardson's own statement is one sentence of prose in Weather Prediction by Numerical Process (1922, p. 66, section 4/8/0). After quoting an observation that the updrafts of large cumulus clouds produce turbulence within and around the clouds, he writes: "We realize thus that: big whirls have little whirls that feed on their velocity, and little whirls have lesser whirls and so on to viscosity—in the molecular sense." The context is the choice of one eddy coefficient for eddies of all sizes. The statement is a qualitative picture drawn from observation, not a derived result. Kolmogorov's 1941 paper describes the same succession of "pulsations" of increasing order (chapter 5, section "Viscosity cannot be removed: the small scales").

Which term transfers energy. The slide's annotation writes the Navier–Stokes equations, chapter 6, (6.9), in vector form, \(\partial\mathbf U/\partial t+\mathbf U\cdot\nabla\mathbf U=-\rho^{-1}\nabla p+\nu\nabla^2\mathbf U\), with \(p\) the pressure and \(\rho\) the constant density, and labels \(\mathbf U\cdot\nabla\mathbf U\) the inertia term and \(\nu\nabla^2\mathbf U\) the viscous term. The lecture states that the inertia term breaks large scales into small ones, and that the viscous term becomes dominant only at the small scales. Two exact properties of the equations give this statement its content. Both use a periodic box of volume \(V\) (in this section \(V\) is a volume, not the sample-space variable of chapters 7–8), with the box average \(\langle\cdot\rangle_V\) of chapter 6; the box removes boundary terms and is the standard idealization of homogeneous turbulence.

First, the inertia and pressure terms do not change the total kinetic energy. Multiply (6.9) by \(U_j\) and average over the box. With \(\partial U_i/\partial x_i=0\),

\[ \Bigl\langle U_jU_i\frac{\partial U_j}{\partial x_i}\Bigr\rangle_V=\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\bigl(\tfrac12U_iU_jU_j\bigr)\Bigr\rangle_V=0,\qquad \Bigl\langle U_j\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_j}\Bigr\rangle_V=\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial(pU_j)}{\partial x_j}\Bigr\rangle_V=0, \tag{10.1} \]

because the average of a derivative of a periodic function over a period is zero. The hinge is incompressibility: it allows \(U_jU_i\,\partial U_j/\partial x_i\) and \(U_j\,\partial p/\partial x_j\) to be written as divergences. What remains is

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\Bigl\langle\tfrac12U_jU_j\Bigr\rangle_V=-\nu\Bigl\langle\frac{\partial U_j}{\partial x_i}\frac{\partial U_j}{\partial x_i}\Bigr\rangle_V\le0, \tag{10.2} \]

after one integration by parts of the viscous term. Without forcing, kinetic energy in the box can only decrease, and only through viscosity; the inertia term moves energy from one part of the motion to another without creating or destroying it. This is the precise meaning of "transferred by inviscid processes".

Second, the inertia term is a product of the velocity with its own gradient, and a product of two waves contains new wavelengths. In one dimension,

\[ \sin(\kappa_1x)\,\sin(\kappa_2x)=\tfrac12\bigl[\cos\bigl((\kappa_1-\kappa_2)x\bigr)-\cos\bigl((\kappa_1+\kappa_2)x\bigr)\bigr], \tag{10.3} \]

where \(\kappa_1\), \(\kappa_2\) are wavenumbers (\(2\pi\) divided by a wavelength). Two motions of wavenumbers \(\kappa_1\) and \(\kappa_2\) drive, through the quadratic term, motions of wavenumbers \(\kappa_1+\kappa_2\) and \(|\kappa_1-\kappa_2|\). Repeated, this generates ever higher wavenumbers, that is, ever smaller scales. The viscous term multiplies a component of wavenumber \(\kappa\) by \(-\nu\kappa^2\), so its damping rate grows as the square of the wavenumber and becomes comparable to the transfer only at small scales.

Neither property proves the cascade. Identity (10.1) shows that the inertia term conserves energy; it does not say in which direction the energy moves. Identity (10.3) shows that both larger and smaller wavenumbers are generated; it does not say that the net transfer is from large to small scales, nor that it proceeds in steps between eddies of similar size, as in Richardson's "little whirls" and the slide's "somewhat smaller eddies". The direction depends on the dynamics: chapter 6 showed that vortex stretching, the mechanism that intensifies small-scale vorticity in three dimensions, is absent in two dimensions. The cascade is therefore a phenomenological picture, supported by observation and by the hypotheses that follow; the remaining sections use it as one.

Eddies, their scales and the Reynolds number of the largest eddies L26 13:21

The slide defines an eddy as a turbulent motion, localized within a region of size \(\ell\), that is at least moderately coherent over that region. "Coherent" means that the motion within the region is organized enough to be recognized as one unit; the lecture's sketch draws an eddy with a definite boundary. The definition is deliberately loose: an eddy is not a solution of the equations and has no unique boundary in a real flow. What the theory uses is the assignment of scales to a size. According to Richardson's view, as the slide states it, the turbulence consists of eddies of different sizes, and eddies of size \(\ell\) have a characteristic velocity \(u(\ell)\) and time scale \(\tau(\ell)\equiv\ell/u(\ell)\). The functions \(u(\ell)\) and \(\tau(\ell)\) are the main unknowns of this chapter.

The flow scales and the eddy scales are different kinds of quantity. The characteristic velocity and length of the flow, \(\mathcal U\) and \(\mathcal L\), are known before the flow is started: for a jet, the mean velocity at the nozzle exit and the nozzle diameter; for a channel, the bulk velocity and the channel width; for a cylinder, the free-stream velocity and the diameter. The size \(\ell_0\) of the largest eddies, by contrast, is a property of the turbulence that develops. The slide states that \(\ell_0\) is comparable to \(\mathcal L\), and that \(u_0\equiv u(\ell_0)\) is of the order of the root-mean-square turbulence intensity

\[ u'\equiv\bigl(\tfrac23k\bigr)^{1/2}, \tag{10.4} \]

which is comparable to \(\mathcal U\). The factor \(\tfrac23\) has a precise origin. Since \(k=\tfrac12(\langle u_1^2\rangle+\langle u_2^2\rangle+\langle u_3^2\rangle)\), if the three variances are equal, each is \(\tfrac23k\) and \(u'\) is the root-mean-square value of each component; this is the isotropic relation (8.25) of chapter 8. If the variances differ, (10.4) defines \(u'\) as an average over the three components. (In the simulation chapters \(u'\) will also denote a residual velocity; those chapters state the change.)

The Reynolds number of the largest eddies is \(\mathrm{Re}_0=u_0\ell_0/\nu\). The slide states that it is large, comparable to \(\mathrm{Re}\), so that the direct effects of viscosity on these eddies are negligibly small. Chapter 5, (5.17), made "negligibly small" quantitative: the rate at which viscosity removes energy directly from eddies of size \(\ell_0\) is a fraction \(\mathrm{Re}_0^{-1}\) of the rate \(u_0^3/\ell_0\) at which they lose it by the cascade.

The running case. At the nozzle of the jet, \(\mathcal U=56\ \mathrm{m/s}\) and \(\mathcal L=0.025\ \mathrm m\), so \(\mathrm{Re}=9.6\times10^{4}\) (chapter 6). At the station, \(\ell_0=0.1\ \mathrm m=4\mathcal L\) and \(u_0=2\ \mathrm{m/s}=\mathcal U/28\). The ratio 28 does not contradict "comparable". The jet spreads and slows downstream, and for a developing flow the comparison must use the local width and the local mean velocity: at the station the mean centreline velocity is 8 m/s, four times \(u_0\). The station's \(\mathrm{Re}_0=1.37\times10^{4}\) is a factor 7 below the nozzle \(\mathrm{Re}\). Its reciprocal, \(7.3\times10^{-5}\), is the fraction of the large-eddy energy loss that viscosity takes directly. The measured axial fluctuation \(\sigma_u=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\) of chapter 8 equals \(u'\) only if the three variances are equal, which chapter 8 identified as an untested assumption for this jet; with it, \(k=\tfrac32u'^2=6\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\).

The dissipation rate is set by the largest eddies L26 22:10

The slide restates Richardson's notion: the large eddies are unstable and break up, transferring their energy to somewhat smaller eddies, which undergo a similar break-up and transfer their energy to yet smaller eddies. The cascade continues until the Reynolds number of the eddies is small enough that their motion is stable and molecular viscosity is effective in dissipating the kinetic energy. The slide then states the result that the rest of the chapter depends on: the rate of dissipation \(\varepsilon\) is determined by the transfer of energy from the largest eddies, the first process in the sequence, and scales as

\[ \varepsilon\sim\frac{u_0^2}{\tau_0}=\frac{u_0^3}{\ell_0}. \tag{10.5} \]

The first form reads: an energy per unit mass of order \(u_0^2\) is passed on in a time of order \(\tau_0\). The second follows from \(\tau_0=\ell_0/u_0\). (The board note beside the slide writes \(\tau_0=u_0/\ell_0\); the slide's definition \(\tau(\ell)\equiv\ell/u(\ell)\) gives \(\ell_0/u_0\), and only that form makes (10.5) dimensionally correct.) Chapter 5, (5.16), obtained the same estimate from the assumption that the large eddies lose a significant fraction of their energy in one turnover time; it is not derived again here.

Why the first step sets the rate. In statistically stationary and homogeneous turbulence, no group of scales accumulates energy on average, and no energy is carried from one place to another. The energy that leaves the largest eddies per unit time must then pass through every intermediate size and arrive at the smallest eddies at the same rate, where it is dissipated. The rate is fixed where the energy enters the sequence, by the dynamics of the large eddies; viscosity, acting at the end, does not set the rate but the size at which the dissipation occurs. The section "Transfer, dissipation and the first similarity hypothesis" writes this argument as an energy budget and estimates its error when the flow is not stationary.

What "independent of viscosity" means. The slide adds that (10.5) is consistent with experimental observations in free shear flows, in which \(\varepsilon\) is independent of \(\nu\). That statement has an exact form. Suppose the dissipation rate of a flow of fixed geometry depends only on \(u_0\), \(\ell_0\) and \(\nu\). These three quantities contain two independent dimensions, length and time, so they form one dimensionless group, \(\mathrm{Re}_0\), and the Buckingham pi theorem gives

\[ \varepsilon=\frac{u_0^3}{\ell_0}\,F(\mathrm{Re}_0) \tag{10.6} \]

with an unknown function \(F\). Equation (10.6) is not an estimate; it is the most general form compatible with the assumed list of parameters. The physical claim of the slide is that \(F\) tends to a nonzero constant as \(\mathrm{Re}_0\to\infty\). Then \(\varepsilon\) stays finite as \(\nu\to0\) at fixed \(u_0\) and \(\ell_0\), although the dissipation is a viscous process. Dimensional analysis cannot decide this; measurement and simulation do.

McComb, Berera, Yoffe and Linkmann (2015) write (10.6) in the form they attribute to Taylor (1935), \(\varepsilon=C_\varepsilon U^3/L\), with \(U\) the root-mean-square velocity and \(L\) the integral length scale (accepted manuscript, p. 2), the quantity that chapter 8 defines as \(L_{11}\). They report that Batchelor in 1953 presented evidence that \(C_\varepsilon\) tends to a constant as the Reynolds number increases, and that Sreenivasan in 1984 found \(C_\varepsilon\) constant in grid turbulence for Taylor-microscale Reynolds numbers above about 50 (p. 2; the attributions are theirs). From direct numerical simulations of forced isotropic turbulence at integral-scale Reynolds numbers \(R_L\) up to 5875, they fit

\[ C_\varepsilon=C_{\varepsilon,\infty}+\frac{C'}{R_L},\qquad C_{\varepsilon,\infty}=0.468\pm0.006,\quad C'=18.9\pm1.3 \tag{10.7} \]

(p. 1; they write \(C\) for \(C'\), and this book reserves \(C\) for the Kolmogorov constant). In the notation of (10.6), \(F\to C_{\varepsilon,\infty}\) with a correction proportional to \(1/R_L\).

For the running case, identify \(R_L\) with \(\mathrm{Re}_0=1.37\times10^{4}\); this is an assumption, because \(\ell_0\) is the size of the energy-containing eddies and not a measured \(L_{11}\). Then (10.7) gives \(C_\varepsilon=0.4694\). Doubling the viscosity halves \(R_L\) and gives \(C_\varepsilon=0.4708\), a change of 0.3 %: at this Reynolds number the dissipation rate is independent of viscosity to within 0.3 % per factor of 2 in \(\nu\), if the jet behaves like the simulated isotropic turbulence. With \(C_\varepsilon=0.47\) in place of 1, \(\varepsilon=37.6\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\) instead of 80, and every Kolmogorov length is larger by \(0.47^{-1/4}=1.21\). This chapter keeps the unit constant of chapters 5–8 for the running-case numbers and states the factor where it matters. Two boundaries apply to (10.7): the value 0.468 belongs to forced isotropic turbulence in a periodic box with the authors' definitions of \(U\) and \(L\); and it is a statement about statistically stationary homogeneous turbulence, the case in which the argument of the previous paragraph is exact.

The hypothesis of local isotropy L27 1:10

The cascade picture leaves two questions, which the slide states: what is the size of the smallest eddies, the ones that dissipate the energy; and as \(\ell\) decreases, do \(u(\ell)\) and \(\tau(\ell)\) increase, decrease or stay the same? The slide states that Kolmogorov formulated three hypotheses on the premise that \(u(\ell)\) and \(\tau(\ell)\) decrease as \(\ell\) decreases. The section "Velocity and time scales in the inertial subrange" shows that the hypotheses return this premise as a consequence, which makes the set consistent but does not test it.

The first hypothesis concerns direction. The large eddies are in general anisotropic: their statistics depend on direction, because they are shaped by the boundary conditions of the flow. In a jet, the axial direction differs from the radial ones. Kolmogorov argued, in the slide's words, that the directional biases of the large scales are lost in the chaotic scale-reduction process by which energy is transferred to successively smaller eddies. The slide states:

Kolmogorov's hypothesis of local isotropy. At sufficiently high Reynolds number, the small-scale turbulent motions (\(\ell\ll\ell_0\)) are statistically isotropic.

"Statistically isotropic" has the meaning of chapter 8, section "Isotropy", applied to the small scales only: the joint probability density functions of the velocity differences between nearby points, at separations small compared with \(\ell_0\), are unchanged when the coordinate axes are rotated or reflected. Kolmogorov's paper makes the restriction to differences explicit (Kolmogorov 1941, English translation 1991, Definitions 1 and 2, pp. 9–10). Turbulence is locally homogeneous in a domain if the joint distribution of the velocity differences \(\mathbf w=\mathbf u(P)-\mathbf u(P^{(0)})\), at points \(P\) placed relative to a reference point \(P^{(0)}\) (\(P\) is Kolmogorov's symbol for a point of space-time, not the probability \(P\{\cdot\}\) of chapter 7), does not depend on the reference point, its time or its velocity; it is locally isotropic if, in addition, these distributions are invariant under rotations and reflections of the axes. Kolmogorov notes that this is narrower than Taylor's isotropy in demanding steadiness in time, and wider in restricting only the differences of velocities, not the velocities themselves (p. 10).

The lecture insists on the qualifier: every hypothesis begins with "at sufficiently high Reynolds number", and the lecture states that the hypothesis may not hold at moderately high Reynolds numbers and becomes more accurate as the Reynolds number increases. Kolmogorov's paper qualifies it in space and time as well: he considers it "rather likely" that local isotropy holds to a good approximation in sufficiently small domains not lying near the boundary of the flow or its other singularities, where "small" means small compared with the length and time scales of the flow as a whole (p. 10). He adds that the proposition, in so general a formulation, cannot be rigorously proved, and that isotropy in Taylor's full sense is well confirmed for turbulence behind a grid but is otherwise only a rather far approximation (p. 10).

A test that the hypothesis makes possible. Local isotropy has consequences that a measurement can check. Consider the transverse derivative \(\partial u_1/\partial x_2\), a small-scale quantity because it is the limit of a velocity difference over a vanishing separation. Reflect the \(x_2\)-axis: \(x_2\to-x_2\) leaves \(u_1\) unchanged and reverses the derivative, so \(\partial u_1/\partial x_2\to-\partial u_1/\partial x_2\). Invariance under the reflection requires every odd moment to equal its own negative:

\[ \Bigl\langle\Bigl(\frac{\partial u_1}{\partial x_2}\Bigr)^{2n+1}\Bigr\rangle=0,\qquad n=1,2,\ldots \tag{10.8} \]

In a flow with a mean velocity gradient \(d\langle U_1\rangle/dx_2\), as in a jet away from its axis, the large scales are not reflection-symmetric, and (10.8) is the statement that the asymmetry does not reach the derivatives. Schumacher, Sreenivasan and Yeung (2003) collect the measurements in shear flows (arXiv preprint, pp. 1–2). They express the Reynolds number through the Taylor-microscale Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=\langle u_1^2\rangle^{1/2}\lambda/\nu\) (they write \(R_\lambda\)), where \(\lambda^2=\langle u_1^2\rangle/\langle(\partial u_1/\partial x_1)^2\rangle\) defines the Taylor microscale, a length treated in a later chapter, and \(x_1\) is the streamwise direction. They report that the collected data for the normalized third moment (skewness) of \(\partial u_1/\partial x_2\) appear, on average, to fall off with \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) less steeply than \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda^{-1}\), the rate that follows from assuming a linear dependence on the shear (p. 1); that the normalized fifth moment does not diminish perceptibly even at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) as high as 1000 (p. 2); and that Shen and Warhaft measured a seventh moment that increased with \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) (p. 2). Their abstract states their own reading: that the odd moments do not vanish as expected from elementary scaling does not necessarily imply that small-scale anisotropy persists at all Reynolds numbers, because intermittency also affects these moments (abstract, p. 1; the argument from intermittency is on p. 2). The evidence therefore supports local isotropy as an approximation whose rate of approach with Reynolds number is not settled, not as an exact property of laboratory or atmospheric shear flows.

For the running case the consequence is practical. On the axis of the jet the mean shear vanishes; off the axis it does not. An isotropic relation applied to the small scales at the station, such as the one for \(\varepsilon\) in terms of one derivative in chapter 5 (section "Energy is carried by large scales and vorticity by small scales"), is an application of the hypothesis, and its error is the error of the hypothesis at \(\mathrm{Re}_0=1.37\times10^{4}\).

A demarcation length and the loss of the flow's geometry L27 5:21

The hypothesis needs a boundary between "large" and "small". The slide introduces a length scale \(\ell_{EI}\), with \(\ell_{EI}\approx\tfrac16\ell_0\) "say", as the demarcation between the anisotropic large eddies (\(\ell\gt\ell_{EI}\)) and the isotropic small eddies (\(\ell\lt\ell_{EI}\)). The lecture calls the factor \(\tfrac16\) arbitrary for the present and states that later modules give its reason; a later chapter, on the spectra of the inertial and dissipation ranges, takes it up. Until then, \(\ell_{EI}\) is a nominal boundary, and every number below that uses it inherits the factor. For the jet, \(\ell_{EI}=16.7\ \mathrm{mm}\); for the atmospheric layer, \(\ell_{EI}=167\ \mathrm m\).

Kolmogorov extended the argument from direction to geometry. The slide states that, by the same reasoning, the geometry of the large eddies, which is determined by the mean flow and the boundary conditions, is also lost, so that the statistics of the small-scale motions are in a sense universal: similar in every high-Reynolds-number turbulent flow. The lecture's illustration is a picture of a whole jet, which anyone can identify as a jet, against a magnified view of a small region inside it, which shows many small scales and does not reveal whether it came from a jet, a channel or the wake of a cylinder.

The illustration concerns pictures; the hypothesis concerns statistics. "Universal" means that a statistic of the small scales, for example the probability density function of a velocity difference over a given separation, is the same function in every flow once the parameters on which it depends have been fixed. The statement is empty until those parameters are named. The next section names them.

Transfer, dissipation and the first similarity hypothesis L27 10:10

On what parameters does the universal state depend? The slide answers through the two dominant processes acting on the small scales: the transfer of energy to successively smaller scales, at a rate \(\mathcal T_{EI}\), the rate at which the small scales (\(\ell\lt\ell_{EI}\)) receive energy from the large scales; and viscous dissipation, characterized by \(\nu\). It then states that the dissipation rate is determined by the transfer rate, \(\varepsilon\approx\mathcal T_{EI}\). The slide gives no condition for this approximation. It holds under two assumptions, which an energy budget makes visible.

The energy budget of the small scales. Let \(k_\lt\) be the kinetic energy per unit mass of the motions smaller than \(\ell_{EI}\), and let \(\varepsilon_\lt\) be the part of the dissipation that occurs at those scales. Consider statistically homogeneous turbulence, such as the periodic box of the section "Richardson's energy cascade" or turbulence behind a grid. Then no small-scale energy is carried in or out of a region by the flow or by pressure, energy enters the group only by transfer and leaves it only by dissipation, and

\[ \frac{dk_\lt}{dt}=\mathcal T_{EI}-\varepsilon_\lt. \tag{10.9} \]

The first assumption is that the dissipation at scales larger than \(\ell_{EI}\) is negligible, \(\varepsilon_\lt\approx\varepsilon\). The argument of chapter 5, (5.17), applies at any size \(\ell\): viscosity removes energy from eddies of size \(\ell\) at a rate of order \(\nu u(\ell)^2/\ell^2\), against a cascade loss of order \(u(\ell)^3/\ell\), a fraction \(\nu/(u(\ell)\ell)=\mathrm{Re}(\ell)^{-1}\). For \(\ell\gt\ell_{EI}\) this fraction is largest at \(\ell_{EI}\), where \(\mathrm{Re}(\ell_{EI})=(\ell_{EI}/\eta)^{4/3}\) by (10.21) of the section "Velocity and time scales in the inertial subrange": \(1/1256=8.0\times10^{-4}\) for the jet and \(5.5\times10^{-7}\) for the atmospheric layer, against \(\mathrm{Re}_0^{-1}=7.3\times10^{-5}\) at \(\ell_0\) itself. The second assumption concerns the left side. In statistically stationary homogeneous turbulence its mean is zero and \(\mathcal T_{EI}=\varepsilon_\lt\) holds exactly in the mean. In an inhomogeneous flow such as the running-case jet, (10.9) needs an additional term for the transport of small-scale energy from one point to another by the mean flow, the fluctuations and the pressure; this chapter does not estimate that term, and the balance at the jet station is an assumption. If instead the large-scale state changes on a time \(T_s\), and the small scales follow it, then \(|dk_\lt/dt|\sim k_\lt/T_s\). The energy \(k_\lt\) is of order \(u(\ell_{EI})^2\), and the rate at which it is passed on is of order \(u(\ell_{EI})^2/\tau(\ell_{EI})\), the estimate (10.5) applied at the size \(\ell_{EI}\). Dividing,

\[ \frac{|\mathcal T_{EI}-\varepsilon|}{\varepsilon}\sim\frac{\tau(\ell_{EI})}{T_s}. \tag{10.10} \]

The approximation \(\varepsilon\approx\mathcal T_{EI}\) therefore requires the small scales to be fast compared with the changes of the large scales. In decaying turbulence, where \(k\) decreases at the rate \(\varepsilon\), the large-scale state changes on \(T_s\sim k/\varepsilon\). With \(k=\tfrac32u_0^2\) and \(\varepsilon=u_0^3/\ell_0\), \(T_s\sim1.5\,\tau_0\); with \(\tau(\ell_{EI})=0.30\,\tau_0\), which the section "Velocity and time scales in the inertial subrange" derives, the ratio (10.10) is about 0.2. The balance is then approximate at the level of tens of percent, and (10.10) gives only the order of the departure, not its value. Estimate (10.10) is a teaching construction of this chapter; the term "universal equilibrium range", used below, names the assumption that the departure is small.

The first similarity hypothesis. With the parameters named, the slide states the second of the three hypotheses:

Kolmogorov's first similarity hypothesis. In every turbulent flow at sufficiently high Reynolds number, the statistics of the small-scale motions (\(\ell\lt\ell_{EI}\)) have a universal form that is uniquely determined by \(\nu\) and \(\varepsilon\).

Kolmogorov's formulation reads: "For the locally isotropic turbulence the distributions \(F_n\) are uniquely determined by the quantities \(\nu\) and \(\bar\varepsilon\)" (Kolmogorov 1941, p. 12), where \(F_n\) is the joint distribution of the velocity differences at \(n\) points and \(\bar\varepsilon\) is the mean dissipation rate. In his formulation the hypothesis applies wherever local isotropy holds; the slide states the same range as \(\ell\lt\ell_{EI}\).

The consequence for dimensions is immediate. The Kolmogorov scales of chapter 5, (5.14), are the length, velocity and time formed from \(\nu\) and \(\varepsilon\):

\[ \eta\equiv\Bigl(\frac{\nu^3}{\varepsilon}\Bigr)^{1/4},\qquad u_\eta\equiv(\nu\varepsilon)^{1/4},\qquad\tau_\eta\equiv\Bigl(\frac{\nu}{\varepsilon}\Bigr)^{1/2}. \]

\[ \langle s_{ij}s_{ij}\rangle=\frac{1}{2\tau_\eta^2}. \tag{10.13} \]

The second similarity hypothesis L28 12:24

If \(\ell_0/\eta\) is large, there is a range of sizes \(\ell\) that are very small compared with \(\ell_0\) and very large compared with \(\eta\), \(\ell_0\gg\ell\gg\eta\). The slide argues that, since these eddies are much bigger than the dissipative eddies, their Reynolds number may be supposed large, and their motion little affected by viscosity. It states:

Kolmogorov's second similarity hypothesis. In every turbulent flow at sufficiently high Reynolds number, the statistics of the motions of scale \(\ell\) in the range \(\ell_0\gg\ell\gg\eta\) have a universal form that is uniquely determined by \(\varepsilon\) and independent of \(\nu\).

Kolmogorov's formulation reads: "If the moduli of the vectors \(y^{(k)}\) and of their differences \(y^{(k)}-y^{(k')}\) (where \(k\neq k'\)) are large in comparison with \(\lambda\), then the distribution laws \(F_n\) are uniquely determined by the quantity \(\bar\varepsilon\) and do not depend on \(\nu\)" (Kolmogorov 1941, p. 12). Two differences of form matter. First, the statement writes \(\lambda\) for the length that this book writes \(\eta\); the paper's equation (17), \(\eta=\lambda=\nu^{3/4}/\bar\varepsilon^{1/4}\), equates the scaling length \(\eta\) of its coordinate transformation (15) to \(\lambda\) (p. 12). In this book bare \(\lambda\) is the Taylor microscale. Second, the paper states only the lower bound; the upper bound comes from the domain of local isotropy, which the paper restricts to separations small compared with the scale of the flow. The slide combines the two bounds into \(\ell_0\gg\ell\gg\eta\). A footnote to the hypothesis gives its physical sense: the mechanism of transfer of energy from larger pulsations to finer ones is, for pulsations of intermediate orders, independent of viscosity (p. 12).

The consequence for dimensions. If the statistics of eddies of size \(\ell\) depend only on \(\varepsilon\) and \(\ell\), every velocity that characterizes them is a product \(\varepsilon^b\ell^c\). With \([\varepsilon]=\mathrm{L^2T^{-3}}\) and \([\ell]=\mathrm L\), a velocity requires \(2b+c=1\) and \(3b=1\), so \(b=c=\tfrac13\). The solution is unique, because \(\varepsilon\) and \(\ell\) have independent dimensions. Therefore

\[ u(\ell)=C_u\,(\varepsilon\ell)^{1/3},\qquad \eta\ll\ell\ll\ell_0, \tag{10.16} \]

with a pure number \(C_u\) that the argument cannot determine. The two similarity hypotheses are consistent on the overlap of their ranges. Kolmogorov's paper draws the corresponding conclusion for the mean square of the velocity difference \(w_1\) between two points separated by a distance \(r\) along the direction of \(w_1\), the quantity he writes \(B_{dd}(r)\): for \(r\) large in comparison with his \(\lambda\), that is \(\eta\), \(B_{dd}(r)\sim C\bar\varepsilon^{2/3}r^{2/3}\), "where \(C\) is an absolute constant" (p. 13, equations (22)–(23)). It is a consequence of the hypotheses, and the hypotheses do not give the value of \(C\). Such mean-square differences, the structure functions, and the energy spectrum that corresponds to them are treated in later chapters, beginning with the next, "The energy spectrum".

The program below implements the chapter's model for the two flows. Its inputs, set in the code in SI units, are \(u_0\), \(\ell_0\) and \(\nu\) for each flow and the constant \(C_\varepsilon\). The function kolmogorov raises ValueError for a non-positive input. The program reads no files and writes only to standard output; it requires NumPy. import numpy as np

# Dimensions as (length, time) exponents.
NU, EPS, ELL = np.array([2., -1.]), np.array([2., -3.]), np.array([1., 0.])
VELOCITY = np.array([1., -1.])


def inertial_exponents():
    """Return (b, c) such that eps**b * ell**c is a velocity."""
    return np.linalg.solve(np.column_stack([EPS, ELL]), VELOCITY)


def small_scale_group():
    """Return (a, b, c) with nu**a * eps**b * ell**c dimensionless, c = 1."""
    _, _, vt = np.linalg.svd(np.column_stack([NU, EPS, ELL]))
    return vt[-1]/vt[-1][2]


def kolmogorov(u0, ell0, nu, c_eps=1.0):
    """Return eps, eta, u_eta, tau_eta from eps = c_eps*u0**3/ell0."""
    if min(u0, ell0, nu, c_eps) <= 0:
        raise ValueError("u0, ell0, nu and c_eps must be positive")
    eps = c_eps*u0**3/ell0
    return eps, (nu**3/eps)**0.25, (nu*eps)**0.25, (nu/eps)**0.5


def threshold(width, c_eps=1.0):
    """Re_0 at which ell0/6 = width*60*eta, found by bisection."""
    lo, hi = 1.0, 1e9
    for _ in range(200):
        re0 = np.sqrt(lo*hi)
        eta = kolmogorov(1.0, 1.0, 1/re0, c_eps)[1]
        lo, hi = (lo, re0) if 1/6 > width*60*eta else (re0, hi)
    return re0

What the hypotheses establish and what they do not

The status of the statements. The chapter contains four kinds of statement, and a conclusion is no stronger than the weakest it uses. The cascade is a phenomenological picture (Richardson 1922), of which the equations of motion supply two exact ingredients, (10.1)–(10.3), but not the direction or locality of the transfer. The scaling \(\varepsilon\sim u_0^3/\ell_0\), (10.5), is an empirical result, supported for stationary isotropic turbulence by simulations such as those behind (10.7). Local isotropy and the two similarity hypotheses are hypotheses; Kolmogorov himself states that the first cannot be rigorously proved in its general formulation (1941, p. 10). The forms (10.11), (10.16), (10.18)–(10.21) are dimensional consequences of the hypotheses, exact given them, with undetermined constants. The demarcations \(\ell_{EI}\approx\tfrac16\ell_0\) and \(\ell_{DI}\approx60\eta\) are nominal values that the lecture defers; (10.17) and Figure 10.1 depend on them.

Intermittency and the refined hypotheses. The Kolmogorov scales and the universal statistics of (10.14) are built from the mean dissipation rate. Kolmogorov's 1962 paper (J. Fluid Mech. 13, 1962) records an objection that Landau raised soon after the 1941 hypotheses: the hypotheses do not take into account that, because the transfer of energy from coarser to finer vortices is random, the variation of the dissipation should increase without limit as the ratio of the external to the internal scale increases (p. 82). The refinement, which Kolmogorov credits to Oboukhov, replaces \(\bar\varepsilon\) by \(\varepsilon_r\), the dissipation averaged over a sphere of radius \(r\), assumes that \(\log\varepsilon_r\) has a normal distribution, and takes its variance as \(A(\mathbf x,t)+9\chi\log(L/r)\), where \(L\) is the external scale, \(\chi\) a universal constant and \(A\) depends on the macrostructure of the flow (p. 83; the paper writes \(k\) for \(\chi\), and this book reserves \(k\) for the turbulent kinetic energy). The similarity hypotheses are restated with the local scales formed from \(\varepsilon_r\) (pp. 83–85). Their consequence for the moments of the longitudinal velocity difference \(\Delta_{dd}(r)\) over a distance \(r\) is

\[ \overline{|\Delta_{dd}(r)|^p}=C_p(\mathbf x,t)\,(r\bar\varepsilon)^{p/3}\Bigl(\frac Lr\Bigr)^{\chi p(p-3)/2} \tag{10.22} \]

(p. 84, equation (10)), where the overbar is the mean. Two changes from the 1941 picture follow. The exponent of \(r\) is no longer \(p/3\), except for \(p=3\), where the correction vanishes; for \(p=2\) the factor is \((L/r)^{-\chi}\), and for \(p\gt3\) the correction grows with \(p\) and with \(L/r\). And the coefficient \(C_p(\mathbf x,t)\) depends on the macrostructure of the flow, so the statistics are no longer strictly universal. The paper gives no value of \(\chi\). Exercise 6 evaluates the size of the correction for an assumed value. The consequence for this chapter is that the hypotheses describe low-order statistics better than high-order ones; chapter 5 noted the same point for the resolution of simulations (Yeung, Sreenivasan and Pope 2018).

Local isotropy in shear flows. The measurements collected by Schumacher, Sreenivasan and Yeung (section "The hypothesis of local isotropy") show odd derivative moments that decrease more slowly with Reynolds number than the simplest estimate, or not perceptibly up to a Taylor-microscale Reynolds number of about 1000. The jet is a shear flow, so isotropic relations at its station are approximations of unstated accuracy.

Finite Reynolds number. The hypotheses are statements about the limit of high Reynolds number. The jet station's \(\mathrm{Re}_0=1.37\times10^{4}\) gives, by the lecture's demarcations, an inertial subrange a factor 3.5 wide; a power law such as (10.18) cannot be distinguished from a neighbouring one over half a decade. The atmospheric layer offers 2.9 decades, but it has buoyancy, a wall and rotation that the hypotheses do not contain, and their effects on its large scales are among the things that local isotropy asserts the small scales forget. The equilibrium assumption (10.10) is exact in the mean only for statistically stationary homogeneous turbulence; in decaying grid turbulence its estimated departure is of order 0.2, and in an inhomogeneous flow such as the jet the budget (10.9) also needs a transport term that this chapter does not estimate. The next chapter, "The energy spectrum", restates the hypotheses for the distribution of energy over wavenumbers, where these conditions become measurable.

Exercises

Exercise 1 (derive). (a) From (10.18) and (10.19), derive \(\mathrm{Re}(\ell)=(\ell/\eta)^{4/3}\) and \(\tau(\ell)/\tau_\eta=(\ell/\eta)^{2/3}\). (b) Two eddies in the inertial subrange differ in size by a factor of 2. By what factors do their velocities, time scales and velocity gradients differ? (c) How many halvings of size lie in the inertial subrange of the jet and of the atmospheric layer, by the lecture's demarcations?

Solution

(a) \(\mathrm{Re}(\ell)=u(\ell)\ell/\nu=\varepsilon^{1/3}\ell^{4/3}/\nu\). From \(\eta=(\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}\), \(\eta^{4/3}=\nu\varepsilon^{-1/3}\), so \(\varepsilon^{1/3}/\nu=\eta^{-4/3}\) and \(\mathrm{Re}(\ell)=(\ell/\eta)^{4/3}\). For the time, \((\ell^2/\varepsilon)^{1/3}/(\nu/\varepsilon)^{1/2}=\ell^{2/3}\nu^{-1/2}\varepsilon^{1/6}\), and \(\eta^{-2/3}=\nu^{-1/2}\varepsilon^{1/6}\), so the ratio is \((\ell/\eta)^{2/3}\). Both results use only the definitions and the inertial forms; at \(\ell=\eta\) both equal 1, consistent with (5.15).

(b) By (10.18) the velocities differ by \(2^{1/3}=1.26\); by (10.19) the times by \(2^{2/3}=1.59\); the gradients \(u(\ell)/\ell=1/\tau(\ell)\) by \(2^{2/3}=1.59\), larger for the smaller eddy. The energy per unit mass differs by \(2^{2/3}\) and the time by the same factor, so the transfer rate \(u^2/\tau\) is the same for both, which is (10.20).

(c) The number of halvings is \(\log_2(\ell_{EI}/\ell_{DI})\). For the jet, \(\log_23.5=1.8\); for the atmospheric layer, \(\ell_{EI}/\ell_{DI}=167/0.201=831\) and \(\log_2831=9.7\). In Richardson's picture of eddies passing energy to "somewhat smaller" eddies, the jet's inertial subrange accommodates fewer than two such steps, and the layer's about ten.

Exercise 2 (compute). A water flow in a laboratory flume has energy-containing eddies with \(u_0=0.05\ \mathrm{m/s}\) and \(\ell_0=0.04\ \mathrm m\); take \(\nu=1.0\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) and \(C_\varepsilon=1\). (a) Compute \(\mathrm{Re}_0\), \(\varepsilon\), \(\eta\), \(\ell_{EI}\) and \(\ell_{DI}\). Does the flow have an inertial subrange by the lecture's demarcations? (b) At fixed \(\ell_0\) and \(\nu\), what velocity \(u_0\) would give an inertial subrange one decade wide? (c) Which of the three hypotheses can the flow of part (a) be used to test?

Solution

(a) \(\mathrm{Re}_0=0.05\times0.04/10^{-6}=2000\). \(\varepsilon=0.05^3/0.04=3.13\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\). \(\eta=((10^{-6})^3/3.13\times10^{-3})^{1/4}=1.34\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm m\), so \(\ell_0/\eta=299\), and the check \(\mathrm{Re}_0^{3/4}=2000^{3/4}=299\) agrees. Then \(\ell_{EI}=0.04/6=6.7\ \mathrm{mm}\) and \(\ell_{DI}=60\eta=8.0\ \mathrm{mm}\). Since \(\ell_{DI}\gt\ell_{EI}\), there is no inertial subrange; equivalently \(\mathrm{Re}_0=2000\) is below the threshold \(2.56\times10^{3}\) of (10.17).

(b) One decade requires \(\mathrm{Re}_0\gt3600^{4/3}=5.5\times10^{4}\), so \(u_0\gt5.5\times10^{4}\times10^{-6}/0.04=1.4\ \mathrm{m/s}\), 28 times the given velocity. Because \(\mathrm{Re}_0\) enters (10.17) with exponent 1 and \(\ell_0/\eta\) grows only as \(\mathrm{Re}_0^{3/4}\), each decade of inertial subrange costs a factor \(10^{4/3}=21.5\) in Reynolds number.

(c) The flow can test the first similarity hypothesis, which applies to \(\ell\lt\ell_{EI}\) and needs no inertial subrange, and local isotropy, with the reservation that \(\mathrm{Re}_0=2000\) may not be "sufficiently high". It cannot test the second hypothesis, because no range of sizes satisfies \(\ell_{EI}\gt\ell\gt\ell_{DI}\). The conclusion depends on the nominal factors \(\tfrac16\) and 60; with factors twice as permissive on each side, \(\ell_{EI}=\ell_0/3\) and \(\ell_{DI}=30\eta\), the condition becomes \(\ell_0/\eta\gt90\), which this flow satisfies by a factor 3.3. The exercise shows how much the answer depends on those factors.

Exercise 3 (check an assumption). Turbulence in air (\(\nu=1.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\)) behind a grid decays with \(dk/dt=-\varepsilon\). At one instant \(u_0=1\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(\ell_0=0.1\ \mathrm m\), \(k=\tfrac32u_0^2\) and \(\varepsilon=u_0^3/\ell_0\). (a) Use (10.10) to estimate the relative difference between \(\mathcal T_{EI}\) and \(\varepsilon\). (b) Repeat with the demarcation \(\ell_{EI}=\ell_0/20\). What does the comparison show about the choice of \(\ell_{EI}\)? (c) For statistically stationary, homogeneous forced turbulence with the same values, what limits the accuracy of \(\varepsilon\approx\mathcal T_{EI}\)?

Solution

(a) \(\mathrm{Re}_0=1\times0.1/(1.5\times10^{-5})=6.7\times10^{3}\), above the threshold \(2.56\times10^{3}\) of (10.17), so the inertial forms can be used at \(\ell_{EI}\). \(\tau_0=\ell_0/u_0=0.1\ \mathrm s\), \(\varepsilon=10\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\), \(k=1.5\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\), so \(T_s\sim k/\varepsilon=0.15\ \mathrm s=1.5\,\tau_0\). By (10.19) with \(\varepsilon=u_0^3/\ell_0\), \(\tau(\ell_{EI})=\tau_0(1/6)^{2/3}=0.030\ \mathrm s\). The ratio (10.10) is \(0.030/0.15=0.20\). The estimate gives the order of the departure: the small scales may receive energy at a rate that differs from \(\varepsilon\) by some tens of percent. It does not give the sign or the value, because (10.10) uses order-of-magnitude estimates of \(k_\lt\) and of \(dk_\lt/dt\).

(b) With \(\ell_{EI}=\ell_0/20\), \(\tau(\ell_{EI})=\tau_0\,20^{-2/3}=0.136\,\tau_0\) and the ratio is \(0.136/1.5=0.09\). A smaller demarcation length puts the boundary at faster eddies and improves the equilibrium, but it shortens the universal range: \(\ell_{EI}/\ell_{DI}\) falls by a factor \(20/6=3.3\). The choice of \(\ell_{EI}\) trades the accuracy of the equilibrium against the width of the range in which the hypotheses can be applied.

(c) In stationary turbulence the mean of \(dk_\lt/dt\) is zero and (10.9) gives \(\mathcal T_{EI}=\varepsilon_\lt\) exactly in the mean. The remaining error is the dissipation at scales larger than \(\ell_{EI}\), a fraction of order \(\mathrm{Re}(\ell_{EI})^{-1}\) by the argument of (5.17) applied at the size \(\ell_{EI}\): with \(\ell_0/\eta=\mathrm{Re}_0^{3/4}=737\), \(\ell_{EI}/\eta=123\) and \(\mathrm{Re}(\ell_{EI})=123^{4/3}=611\), the fraction is of order \(1.6\times10^{-3}\), about ten times \(\mathrm{Re}_0^{-1}=1.5\times10^{-4}\). The instantaneous balance still fluctuates; the statement concerns the mean.

Exercise 4 (counterexample). A student argues: "The second similarity hypothesis says the eddies are independent of viscosity, and (10.18) gives exactly \(u_\eta\) at \(\ell=\eta\), so \(u(\ell)=(\varepsilon\ell)^{1/3}\) holds for all \(\ell\le\ell_{EI}\), including \(\ell\lt\eta\)." Use the smoothness of the velocity field and Kolmogorov's isotropic relation \(\varepsilon=15\nu\langle(\partial u_1/\partial x_1)^2\rangle\) (chapter 5) to show that the claim is false for \(\ell\ll\eta\), and compute the discrepancy at \(\ell=0.1\,\eta\).

Solution

At a finite Reynolds number the velocity field is taken to be smooth: viscosity acts on every gradient, and the derivatives that enter \(\varepsilon\) in (5.13) are assumed to exist with finite mean square. (Whether three-dimensional solutions stay smooth for all time is an open problem; turbulence theory assumes it. Chapter 8 used differentiability in a weaker sense, that the mean of a derivative is the derivative of the mean.) For a separation \(r\) much smaller than every length over which the gradient changes, the longitudinal velocity difference is \(\Delta u\approx r\,\partial u_1/\partial x_1\), so its root-mean-square value is \(r\,\langle(\partial u_1/\partial x_1)^2\rangle^{1/2}=r\,(\varepsilon/15\nu)^{1/2}=r/(15^{1/2}\tau_\eta)\), using the isotropic relation and (10.12). In Kolmogorov units, \(\Delta u_{\mathrm{rms}}/u_\eta=(r/\eta)/15^{1/2}\), because \(u_\eta\tau_\eta=\eta\). This grows in proportion to \(r\), whereas the student's law grows as \(r^{1/3}\).

At \(r=0.1\,\eta\): the smooth-field value is \(0.1/3.873=0.026\,u_\eta\); the claimed value is \(0.1^{1/3}u_\eta=0.46\,u_\eta\), 18 times larger. The claim is therefore false below \(\eta\). The agreement of (10.18) with \(u_\eta\) at \(\ell=\eta\) is an identity of the definitions, not evidence that the power law holds there; the second hypothesis applies only for \(\ell\gg\eta\), and the first hypothesis allows any universal function \(\Phi\) in (10.11), of which \(\Phi(x)\propto x\) for \(x\ll1\) and \(\Phi(x)\propto x^{1/3}\) for \(x\gg1\) are the two limits. The argument uses local isotropy for the factor 15; without it, the linear growth in \(r\) still follows from differentiability, with a different constant.

Exercise 5 (judge a design). Two groups plan velocity measurements with a sensor that averages the velocity over a length of 1 mm. Group A works at the jet station, group B in the atmospheric layer. Each wants (a) the statistics of the inertial subrange and (b) the dissipation rate from velocity gradients. Judge each plan with the scales of this chapter.

Solution

A sensor that averages over 1 mm cannot represent motions much smaller than 1 mm; it represents sizes from about 1 mm upward.

Group A (jet): \(1\ \mathrm{mm}=12.7\,\eta\). The inertial subrange, 4.7–16.7 mm, lies above the sensor length, so plan (a) is feasible in principle, but the subrange spans only a factor 3.5, which is too narrow to establish a power law such as (10.18) (section "What the hypotheses establish and what they do not"). Plan (b) fails: by (10.13) the rms strain rate is \(1/(\sqrt2\,\tau_\eta)=1.65\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), a velocity change of order \(u_\eta=0.185\ \mathrm{m/s}\) over a distance of order \(\eta=79\ \mu\mathrm m\); a 1 mm sensor averages the velocity over \(12.7\,\eta\), so it smooths that gradient, and the chapter cannot say by how much. Chapter 5 (section "A resolution budget for a direct simulation") cites Yeung, Sreenivasan and Pope (2018): a grid spacing of about \(2\eta\) may be adequate for low-order statistics in a simulation. The 1 mm sensor is about 6 times coarser than that spacing, so by the only resolution evidence available in this book it does not resolve the dissipative gradients, and the error of a gradient-based \(\varepsilon\) cannot be quantified from this chapter.

Group B (atmospheric layer): \(1\ \mathrm{mm}=0.30\,\eta\). The sensor resolves the Kolmogorov scale, so plan (b) is feasible as far as size is concerned, and the inertial subrange (0.20–167 m, 2.9 decades) is far above the sensor length, so plan (a) is feasible and the range is wide enough to fit a power law. Group B's difficulties lie elsewhere: buoyancy, the wall and the non-stationarity of the atmosphere, which affect the large scales and, through \(\varepsilon\) and its fluctuations, the small ones. The judgment is that the same instrument suits the high-Reynolds-number flow for both purposes and the laboratory flow for neither purpose fully.

Exercise 6 (derive and compute). From (10.22), (a) write the exponent \(\zeta_p\) in \(\overline{|\Delta_{dd}(r)|^p}\propto r^{\zeta_p}\) at fixed \(L\), and show that the correction to \(p/3\) vanishes for \(p=3\). (b) For an assumed \(\chi=0.025\), an illustrative value and not one given by Kolmogorov's paper, compute the correction factor \((L/r)^{\chi p(p-3)/2}\) for \(p=2\) and \(p=6\) at \(L/r=10^{2}\) and \(L/r=10^{4}\). (c) Interpret the results for the jet and the atmospheric layer.

Solution

(a) The \(r\)-dependence of (10.22) is \(r^{p/3}\,r^{-\chi p(p-3)/2}\), so \(\zeta_p=p/3-\chi p(p-3)/2\). At \(p=3\) the factor \(p-3\) vanishes and \(\zeta_3=1\) for every \(\chi\). For \(p\gt3\) the correction is negative and grows as \(p^2\); for \(0\lt p\lt3\) it is positive and at most \(9\chi/8\), at \(p=\tfrac32\).

(b) For \(p=2\), the exponent of \(L/r\) is \(\chi\cdot2\cdot(-1)/2=-\chi=-0.025\): the factor is \(100^{-0.025}=0.89\) and \(10^{4\times(-0.025)}=0.79\). For \(p=6\), the exponent is \(\chi\cdot6\cdot3/2=9\chi=0.225\): the factor is \(100^{0.225}=2.8\) and \(10^{0.9}=7.9\).

(c) For the second moment the correction is 11 % over two decades of \(L/r\) and 21 % over four. For the sixth moment the corresponding factors are 2.8 and 7.9. The jet's inertial subrange is too narrow to separate \(r^{2/3}\) from \(r^{0.69}\) (\(\zeta_2=2/3+0.025\)), and high-order moments are where the 1941 predictions would fail first. The atmospheric layer, with a wider range of \(L/r\), is where the departures can be measured. The computation depends on the assumed \(\chi\) and on the log-normal assumption behind (10.22); it demonstrates the structure of the correction, not its measured size.
