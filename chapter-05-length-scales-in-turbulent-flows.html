Length scales in turbulent flows: from boundary-layer depth to the Kolmogorov scales

Suppose you must simulate, or instrument, the lowest kilometre of the atmosphere over flat ground on a windy day. Two numbers decide the plan: the size of the largest eddies, which carry momentum and heat through the layer, and the size of the smallest eddies, at which the kinetic energy of the motion becomes heat. For the case developed in this chapter the first is about \(10^{3}\ \mathrm{m}\) and the second about \(3\ \mathrm{mm}\). A grid that resolves both needs of order \(10^{5}\) intervals in each direction and \(10^{15}\) to \(10^{16}\) points. This chapter derives both numbers from order-of-magnitude arguments, states the assumption behind each step, and shows which factors the arguments leave undetermined.

Grounded in four recordings of the NPTEL course Essentials of Turbulence (Prof. Abhilash J. Chandy, IIT Bombay): Lecture 10, Lecture 11, Lecture 12 and Lecture 13. Lecture material: the atmospheric boundary layer as a diffusion problem with an imposed time scale; the scale analysis of the Navier–Stokes equations and the viscous length; diffusive and convective lengths and laminar and turbulent skin friction; the small-scale argument, the Kolmogorov scales and the inviscid estimate of the dissipation rate; the scale ratios and the distribution of energy and vorticity. The lecture attributes several of its examples and figures to Tennekes and Lumley, A First Course in Turbulence (1972); no statement in this chapter relies on that book. Research extensions: Kolmogorov's 1941 paper (definitions of the scales), Blasius's 1908 boundary-layer paper (the laminar drag constant), McComb and co-authors (2015, the measured dissipation constant), Yeung, Sreenivasan and Pope (2018, grid resolution in units of the Kolmogorov length), Pope (2004, the cost of resolving all scales) and the American Meteorological Society glossary (the Coriolis parameter). Original teaching constructions: the use of one atmospheric case through every section except the flat-plate drag section, whose argument does not transfer to a rotating layer over rough ground, the fetch construction that connects the boundary-layer and rotation estimates, the advection–diffusion model problem, the wall-stress consistency checks for the drag estimates, the analysis of the unit Kolmogorov Reynolds number as an identity, the resolution budget, the code and the six exercises.

The objects of the chapter and the running case

Every result in this chapter is a relation between characteristic scales. A characteristic scale is a number, with units, that measures the typical size of a quantity in a region of the flow: a typical speed, a typical distance over which that speed changes, or a typical time. The symbol \(\sim\) between two quantities means that they have the same order of magnitude: their ratio is a dimensionless number that the argument does not determine and that is assumed to be neither very large nor very small. An equals sign is used only for definitions and exact identities. A relation written with \(\sim\) fixes a power law. It does not fix the coefficient in front of it.

The formal model has three groups of parameters. The mean flow has a velocity scale \(U\) and a length scale \(L\) measured along the flow. The largest turbulent eddies have a velocity scale \(u\), which measures the size of the velocity fluctuations, and a size \(\ell\). The fluid has kinematic viscosity \(\nu\) (units \(\mathrm{m^2/s}\)). Later sections add the dissipation rate \(\varepsilon\) and the three Kolmogorov scales \(\eta\), \(\tau_\eta\) and \(u_\eta\), each defined at first use. Three Reynolds numbers appear in the large-scale arguments, and each is a different ratio: \(\mathrm{Re}=UL/\nu\) for the mean flow, \(\mathrm{Re}_t=uL_t/\nu\) for the rotating boundary layer, and \(\mathrm{Re}_\ell=u\ell/\nu\) for the largest eddies; a fourth, \(\mathrm{Re}_\eta\), belongs to the smallest eddies and is defined in (5.15). The lecture writes \(\mathrm{Re}\) for all of them; the subscripts keep them apart.

The running case is a neutral atmospheric boundary layer: the layer of air, next to the ground, in which the wind is slowed by the surface and in which the motion is turbulent. Its numbers come from the lecture: wind speed \(U\approx10\ \mathrm{m/s}\); turbulence velocity \(u\approx U/30\approx0.3\ \mathrm{m/s}\); kinematic viscosity of air \(\nu=0.15\ \mathrm{cm^2/s}=1.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\); observed layer thickness about \(10^{3}\ \mathrm{m}\). "Neutral" means that the case contains no buoyancy: no heating from the ground and no stable temperature stratification. Each section changes or adds one part of this model.

An imposed time scale fixes the depth of the atmospheric boundary layer L10 0:05

The atmosphere moves in a frame that rotates with the Earth. In such a frame a fluid particle with velocity \(\mathbf V\) relative to the frame has an additional acceleration, the Coriolis acceleration \(-2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf V\), where \(\boldsymbol\Omega\) is the angular velocity of the frame. The Earth's angular velocity has magnitude \(\Omega=7.2921\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{rad/s}\) (AMS Glossary of Meteorology). For horizontal motion the relevant part is the rotation about the local vertical. The Coriolis parameter is defined as twice that component, \(f=2\Omega\sin\vartheta\), where \(\vartheta\) is the latitude (the glossary writes \(\varphi\); this book reserves \(\phi\) for a transported scalar); \(fV\) is then the magnitude of the horizontal Coriolis force per unit mass on a parcel moving horizontally at speed \(V\). (In this chapter \(f\) is the Coriolis parameter and \(V\) a velocity; later chapters use \(f(V)\) for a probability density.) At \(\vartheta=40^\circ\), \(f=2(7.2921\times10^{-5})\sin40^\circ=9.4\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), which the lecture rounds to \(10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\). The frame therefore turns about the local vertical at angular velocity \(f/2\), and any horizontal flow is turned by the Coriolis force on the time scale

\[ T=\frac1f\approx10^{4}\ \mathrm s\approx2.8\ \mathrm h. \tag{5.1} \]

The time \(T\) is set by the rotation of the Earth, not by the boundary layer. This is the sense in which the problem has an imposed time scale. The estimate below treats \(T\) as the time available for momentum to spread upward from the ground, and asks what depth the layer reaches in that time. The previous chapter's room-heating example was the reverse problem, with an imposed length (the room size) and an unknown time.

The laminar estimate. If the layer were laminar, momentum would move away from the ground only by molecular diffusion. A one-dimensional model is \(\partial V/\partial t=\nu\,\partial^2V/\partial z^2\), with \(z\) the height. If the velocity changes by an amount of order \(V\) over a height \(L_m\) and a time \(T\), the left side is of order \(V/T\) and the right side of order \(\nu V/L_m^2\). Equating them gives

\[ L_m^2\sim\nu T,\qquad L_m\sim(\nu T)^{1/2}=\bigl(1.5\times10^{-5}\times10^{4}\bigr)^{1/2}\ \mathrm m=0.39\ \mathrm m. \tag{5.2} \]

The lecture gives \(L_m=40\ \mathrm{cm}\). A laminar atmospheric boundary layer would be about as deep as a table is high.

The turbulent estimate. The real layer is turbulent, and the lecture states a typical thickness of about \(10^{3}\ \mathrm m\). Turbulent transport is modelled here by an eddy diffusivity \(K\): a coefficient that replaces \(\nu\) in the diffusion equation and represents the transport by the eddies. This replacement is a model, not a consequence of the equations of motion. Its order of magnitude is the product of the eddy velocity and the eddy size, \(K\sim u\ell\). The second assumption is that the largest eddies are as large as the layer is deep, \(\ell\sim L_t\); the lecture supports this with the observation that in most turbulent flows the largest eddies have sizes comparable to the width of the flow normal to the mean flow. With \(K\sim uL_t\) in place of \(\nu\) in (5.2),

\[ L_t^2\sim KT\sim uL_tT\quad\Longrightarrow\quad L_t\sim uT=\frac uf. \tag{5.3} \]

The hinge of this step is that \(K\) itself grows with \(L_t\). Molecular diffusion has a fixed diffusivity, so the depth grows as \(T^{1/2}\). Eddy diffusion has a diffusivity proportional to the depth, so the depth grows as \(T\). With \(u=0.3\ \mathrm{m/s}\) and \(T=10^{4}\ \mathrm s\), \(L_t\sim3\times10^{3}\ \mathrm m\). This has the same order of magnitude as the observed thickness. The result can also be read as a dimensional statement: once viscosity is excluded, \(u\) and \(f\) are the only parameters, and \(u/f\) is the only length they can form.

An order-of-magnitude argument cannot supply the coefficient. The lecture quotes the empirical relation \(L_t=\tfrac14\,u/f\) for the atmospheric boundary layer, which gives \(750\ \mathrm m\) for the running case. The lecture does not give the measurements behind the factor \(\tfrac14\), and this chapter has not checked it; treat it as a quoted value. The estimate (5.3) also contains no buoyancy parameter. A layer heated from below, or a stably stratified layer, has additional time scales, and (5.3) makes no claim about its depth.

The ratio of the two depths is the square root of a Reynolds number L10 12:41

Divide (5.3) by (5.2) and use \(T=1/f\):

\[ \frac{L_t}{L_m}\sim\frac uf\Bigl(\frac f\nu\Bigr)^{1/2}=\Bigl(\frac{u^2}{f\nu}\Bigr)^{1/2}=\mathrm{Re}_t^{1/2}, \qquad \mathrm{Re}_t\equiv\frac{uL_t}{\nu}=\frac{u^2}{f\nu}. \tag{5.4} \]

The second form of \(\mathrm{Re}_t\) uses \(L_t=u/f\). For the running case \(\mathrm{Re}_t=(0.3)^2/(10^{-4}\times1.5\times10^{-5})=6\times10^{7}\), and \(\mathrm{Re}_t^{1/2}=7.7\times10^{3}\). The direct ratio of the two estimates is \(3000/0.387=7.7\times10^{3}\), as it must be, because (5.4) is an algebraic consequence of (5.2) and (5.3). The lecture rounds \(\mathrm{Re}_t\) to "of order \(10^{7}\)". The physical content is that the turbulent layer is about \(7.7\times10^{3}\) times deeper than a laminar layer would be after the same time.

The comparison with the imposed-length problem shows why the exponent is \(\tfrac12\) here. In a room of size \(L\) stirred by air movements of speed \(u\), heat spreads by molecular diffusion in a time \(T_m\sim L^2/\Gamma\), where \(\Gamma\) is the molecular diffusivity of heat (the lecture writes \(\gamma\)), and by eddies in a time \(T_t\sim L/u\). The ratio is \(T_m/T_t\sim uL/\Gamma\), the Péclet number \(\mathrm{Pe}\). For air the lecture gives the Prandtl number \(\nu/\Gamma=0.73\), so \(\mathrm{Pe}\) and the Reynolds number \(uL/\nu\) of the room flow differ by that factor. With an imposed length, the Reynolds number is of the order of a ratio of times. With an imposed time, it is of the order of the square of a ratio of lengths. The two statements describe one mechanism: the molecular diffusion distance grows as the square root of time and the eddy diffusion distance grows in proportion to time. Exercise 1 evaluates both forms for one room.

Equation (5.4) also states how the depth ratio changes. At fixed \(f\) and \(\nu\), a stronger wind (larger \(u\)) increases both \(L_t\) and \(\mathrm{Re}_t\). The laminar depth \(L_m\) does not depend on \(u\). The conclusion that the lecture draws, that turbulence is a far more effective diffusion agent than molecular motion in flows with large Reynolds number, is the statement \(L_t/L_m\gg1\) when \(\mathrm{Re}_t\gg1\).

Inertia and viscosity act at different lengths L10 19:25

In a turbulent flow the largest lengths are fixed by the dimensions of the flow: the diameter of a pipe, the distance between two plates, the diameter of a cylinder, the thickness of a boundary layer. The smallest lengths are fixed by the diffusive action of viscosity. They are still very large compared with molecular distances, so the continuum equations hold at all of these scales. Because many lengths are present at once, a description that separates the motion by size, a spectral analysis, becomes necessary; that tool is developed in later chapters.

The steady flow of an incompressible fluid with constant viscosity obeys the Navier–Stokes equations

\[ (\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U=-\frac1\rho\nabla p+\nu\nabla^2\mathbf U, \tag{5.5} \]

where \(\mathbf U(\mathbf x)\) is the velocity field, \(p\) the pressure and \(\rho\) the density. (The lecture writes the velocity components in lower case. In this book upper case denotes the full velocity and lower case the turbulent fluctuation: \(u_i\) for its components and \(u\) for its velocity scale.) If the velocity changes by an amount of order \(U\) over a distance \(L\), the inertial term is of order \(U^2/L\) and the viscous term of order \(\nu U/L^2\). Their ratio is

\[ \frac{U^2/L}{\nu U/L^2}=\frac{UL}\nu=\mathrm{Re}. \tag{5.6} \]

At large \(\mathrm{Re}\) the viscous term appears negligible. It cannot be dropped. Boundary and initial conditions make it necessary: on a solid surface the fluid velocity equals the surface velocity (the no-slip condition), and an equation without the viscous term cannot satisfy that condition in addition to the conditions far from the surface. Mathematically, \(\nu\) multiplies the highest derivative in (5.5). Setting it to zero lowers the order of the equation and removes the possibility of satisfying all boundary conditions. A small parameter with this property is called singular.

A model problem. The effect can be seen in an exactly solvable problem (an original construction for this chapter). A scalar \(\phi(x)\) is carried at speed \(U\) and diffuses with diffusivity \(\Gamma\) on \(0\le x\le L\), with \(U\phi'=\Gamma\phi''\), \(\phi(0)=0\) and \(\phi(L)=1\). The solution is

\[ \phi(x)=\frac{e^{\mathrm{Pe}\,x/L}-1}{e^{\mathrm{Pe}}-1},\qquad \mathrm{Pe}=\frac{UL}\Gamma. \tag{5.7} \]

For \(\mathrm{Pe}=100\), \(\phi\) is below \(e^{-1}=0.37\) everywhere except in the last \(1\,\%\) of the domain. With \(\Gamma=0\) the equation becomes \(U\phi'=0\), whose solutions are constants, and no constant satisfies both boundary conditions. Diffusion survives in a thin layer whose thickness \(\delta\) is fixed by requiring the two terms to balance there: \(U/\delta\sim\Gamma/\delta^2\), so \(\delta\sim\Gamma/U=L/\mathrm{Pe}\). The small parameter does not remove the diffusion. It concentrates the diffusion into a short length.

The viscous length. The same reasoning applies to (5.5). The viscous term can be as large as the inertial term if its derivatives act over a new, shorter length \(\ell\), while the inertial term still varies over \(L\). In a boundary layer \(\ell\) is the layer thickness. The symbol is the one used for the large-eddy size, as in the lecture, because in a turbulent layer the largest eddies are about as large as the layer is thick; in a laminar layer \(\ell\) is the thickness only. Equating the two estimates,

\[ \frac{U^2}{L}\sim\frac{\nu U}{\ell^2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac\ell L\sim\Bigl(\frac\nu{UL}\Bigr)^{1/2}=\mathrm{Re}^{-1/2}. \tag{5.8} \]

The exponent is \(-\tfrac12\) rather than the \(-1\) of the model problem because the two derivatives act in different directions. In a boundary layer, inertia acts along the flow over the distance \(L\), and viscosity acts across the flow over the thickness \(\ell\). In (5.7), advection and diffusion act along the same coordinate. Exercise 2 develops this difference.

Blasius used this argument in 1908 in his boundary-layer paper. In the English translation (NACA TM 1256, p. 3) the friction term "gains influence" when the friction constant is of the order of the square of the boundary-layer thickness, both written in Blasius's scaled variables. That is (5.8) in dimensionless form.

For air that has moved over the ground for a time \(T\), the fetch is \(L=UT\), and (5.8) gives \(\ell^2\sim\nu L/U=\nu T\), the laminar depth \(L_m\) of (5.2).

Diffusive and convective lengths in laminar and turbulent boundary layers L11 7:03

The length \(\ell\) of (5.8) is a transverse length. It measures the thickness of the boundary layer, because it is set by the diffusion of the momentum deficit away from the surface. (Near the surface the fluid moves more slowly than the outer flow, so it carries less momentum; that shortfall is the momentum deficit.) Diffusion along the flow is negligible compared with the transport of momentum downstream by the flow itself. The lecture calls \(\ell\) the diffusive length and \(L\) the convective length. The distinction applies to all shear flows, laminar and turbulent. Many shear flows are slender: their width is much smaller than their length.

The ratio \(\ell/L\to0\) is the basis of simplified equations. In the limit \(\ell/L\to0\) the flow in the thin layer obeys equations that are much simpler than (5.5); Prandtl's boundary-layer equations are the standard example, and the lecture notes that they were formulated for laminar layers. In this limit the shear flow is independent of most of its environment, except through the conditions that the outer flow imposes at its edge.

The turbulent boundary layer. In a turbulent layer, eddies carry the momentum deficit away from the surface. If the velocity fluctuations are of order \(u\), the thickness grows at a rate \(d\ell/dt\sim u\). A fluid particle that has travelled from the leading edge of the surface to a downstream position \(L\) has done so in a time of order \(L/U\). Therefore

\[ \ell\sim u\,\frac LU,\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \frac\ell L\sim\frac uU\quad\text{and}\quad\frac\ell u\sim\frac LU. \tag{5.9} \]

The last form states that the turbulent "diffusion" time \(\ell/u\) equals the convective time \(L/U\). The same procedure for a laminar layer, with the diffusion distance \(\ell\sim(\nu t)^{1/2}\) and \(t\sim L/U\), returns (5.8). The lecture adds a warning that applies to both: these relations connect characteristic lengths and velocities, and they are not formulas for the rate at which a particular turbulent boundary layer spreads.

The two cases differ in what the ratio depends on. For a laminar layer \(\ell/L\sim\mathrm{Re}^{-1/2}\), which decreases as the Reynolds number increases. For a turbulent layer \(\ell/L\sim u/U\). The lecture states that experiments give \(u/U\) of order \(10^{-2}\) over a wide range of Reynolds numbers. If \(u/U\) is independent of \(\mathrm{Re}\), turbulent layers are thicker than laminar ones whenever \(\mathrm{Re}^{-1/2}\lt u/U\), that is for \(\mathrm{Re}\gt(U/u)^2\): \(\mathrm{Re}\gt10^{4}\) for \(u/U=0.01\) and \(\mathrm{Re}\gt1.1\times10^{3}\) for \(u/U=0.03\).

The running case. Apply (5.9) to the atmospheric layer over the time \(T=1/f\) for which it can grow. The fetch is \(L=UT=U/f=10^{5}\ \mathrm m\), and (5.9) gives \(\ell\sim uL/U=u/f=3\times10^{3}\ \mathrm m\), which is the rotation estimate (5.3). This agreement is bookkeeping, not an independent confirmation: both estimates assume that the layer grows at speed \(u\) for a time \(1/f\). The flat-plate argument contains no rotation, so it cannot explain why the growth stops; the Coriolis time scale supplies that limit.

Skin friction: why the turbulent drag coefficient is larger L11 15:19

Consider steady two-dimensional flow at speed \(U\) along a flat plate of length \(L\). Let \(D\) be the friction drag per unit span (force per unit width of plate). The drag equals the rate at which the fluid loses momentum to the plate. The drag coefficient is defined as

\[ C_d\equiv\frac{D}{\tfrac12\rho U^2L}. \tag{5.10} \]

Laminar layer. Across a laminar layer the velocity deficit is of order \(U\), so the mass flow deficit per unit span at the end of the plate is of order \(\rho U\ell\) and the momentum deficit is of order \(\rho U^2\ell\). Hence

\[ D\sim\rho U^2\ell,\qquad C_d\sim2\frac\ell L\sim2\,\mathrm{Re}^{-1/2}, \tag{5.11} \]

using (5.8). An independent route gives the same scaling. The wall shear stress is of order \(\tau_w\sim\mu U/\ell\), where \(\mu=\rho\nu\) is the dynamic viscosity, and \(D\sim\tau_wL\sim\mu UL/\ell\). With \(\ell^2\sim\nu L/U\), \(\mu UL/\ell=\rho U^2\ell\,(\nu L/U\ell^2)\sim\rho U^2\ell\).

Blasius solved the laminar flat-plate problem without free constants. For water flowing on both sides of a plate of length \(L\), he obtained a total drag per unit width of \(1.327\,(\mu\rho LU^3)^{1/2}\) (NACA TM 1256, p. 18, where the viscosity is written \(k\)). Per side, this is \(0.664\,(\mu\rho LU^3)^{1/2}\), and (5.10) gives \(C_d=1.327\,\mathrm{Re}^{-1/2}\). The scale estimate (5.11) has the correct exponent and a coefficient of 2 in place of 1.327, a factor of 1.5. This is the general boundary of a scale argument: it fixes the power law and leaves the constant to an exact solution or to measurement.

Turbulent layer. The lecture states that in a turbulent layer the mass flow deficit at the end of the plate is of order \(\rho u\ell\), so that the rate of loss of momentum is of order \((\rho u\ell)U\):

\[ D\sim\rho uU\ell,\qquad C_d\sim2\,\frac uU\,\frac\ell L\sim2\Bigl(\frac uU\Bigr)^2, \tag{5.12} \]

using (5.9). The first step requires an assumption that should be stated: across most of the turbulent layer the mean velocity deficit is of order \(u\), not of order \(U\). A consistency check identifies what \(u\) must be. The mean wall stress is \(\bar\tau_w=D/L\), and (5.10) gives \(C_d=2\bar\tau_w/(\rho U^2)\). Equation (5.12) is therefore the statement \(\bar\tau_w\sim\rho u^2\). The velocity scale \(u\) plays the role of the friction velocity \((\tau_w/\rho)^{1/2}\).

Table 5.1 shows the consequence. The laminar coefficient decreases as \(\mathrm{Re}^{-1/2}\), by a factor of \(10^{1/2}=3.2\) per decade. If the turbulence level \(u/U\) changes slowly with \(\mathrm{Re}\), as the lecture states experiments show, the turbulent coefficient is almost constant. The turbulent estimate exceeds the Blasius value for \(\mathrm{Re}\gt5.4\times10^{5}\) when \(u/U=0.03\) and for \(\mathrm{Re}\gt4.4\times10^{7}\) when \(u/U=0.01\). Because \(C_d\) depends on the square of \(u/U\), the two quoted turbulence levels, both "of order \(10^{-2}\)", give coefficients that differ by a factor of 9. The scale argument predicts the dependence on \(\mathrm{Re}\). The magnitude of the turbulent drag requires a measured \(u/U\).

These crossover values are not predictions of transition, the change from laminar to turbulent flow. The lecture's figure of flat-plate drag coefficients carries the caption remark that transition is very sensitive to small disturbances, and it shows a family of transitional curves between the laminar and turbulent lines. What the scale argument does explain is why the turbulent line lies above the laminar line and falls much more slowly: a turbulent layer is thicker by the factor in (5.9) and transmits a larger wall stress. The lecture names aircraft wings and automobiles as designs in which this difference matters.

Viscosity cannot be removed: the small scales L12 0:05

Up to this point only the largest eddies have appeared. They are as large as the width of the flow, they interact with the mean flow, and they do most of the transport of momentum and contaminants. Other aspects of the dynamics need other lengths. At very small lengths, viscosity smooths the velocity fluctuations. The nonlinear term of (5.5) generates small-scale fluctuations, and the viscous term prevents the generation of infinitely small scales by converting small-scale kinetic energy into heat.

Section "Inertia and viscosity act at different lengths" found that viscosity is a singular small parameter. Turbulence shows a second form of the same behaviour, and the lecture summarizes it as follows. At large \(\mathrm{Re}\) the relative size of the viscous term over the flow scale \(L\) becomes very small. The nonlinear terms counteract this by generating motions small enough to be affected by viscosity. The smallest scale of motion adjusts itself to the value of \(\nu\). As soon as the flow scale is large enough for viscous effects to be negligible, the flow creates small-scale motion that keeps the viscous effects, in particular the dissipation rate, at a finite level. In the laminar boundary layer the thin viscous region was forced by the no-slip condition at a wall. In a turbulent flow the small scales are created by the flow itself, also far from walls.

Kolmogorov's 1941 paper describes the same picture (Kolmogorov 1941, English translation 1991, p. 10, footnote). At very large Reynolds number the large "pulsations" of the first order are themselves unsteady, and smaller pulsations of higher order are superposed on them, until at some order \(n\) the Reynolds number \(l^{(n)}v^{(n)}/\nu\) of the pulsations, with \(l^{(n)}\) and \(v^{(n)}\) the size and relative velocity of the pulsations of order \(n\) (Kolmogorov's notation), becomes so small that viscosity prevents the formation of the next order. The energy is passed from the first order to the higher orders, and the energy of the finest pulsations is converted into heat by viscosity.

The dissipation rate. The rate at which viscosity converts the kinetic energy of the velocity fluctuations into internal energy, per unit mass of fluid, is the dissipation rate \(\varepsilon\). Its units are energy per unit mass per unit time, \(\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-1}}=\mathrm{m^2/s^3}\). For an incompressible Newtonian fluid,

\[ \varepsilon=2\nu\langle s_{ij}s_{ij}\rangle,\qquad s_{ij}=\frac12\Bigl(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}+\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\Bigr), \tag{5.13} \]

where \(u_i\) is the velocity fluctuation (the velocity minus its mean), \(s_{ij}\) is the rate of strain of the fluctuation, repeated indices are summed over 1, 2, 3, and \(\langle\cdot\rangle\) is the mean. Kolmogorov's equation (14) writes a sum of this form in components (Kolmogorov 1941, p. 12). Equation (5.13) is given here so that \(\varepsilon\) has a definite meaning. The arguments of this section and the next three use only two facts about it: its units, and the fact that it is an energy rate.

The equilibrium argument. For the purpose of the argument, the lecture divides the scales into two groups: large scales, which receive energy from the mean flow, and small scales, which are affected by viscosity. Energy goes from the large group to the small group and from the small group into heat. Real turbulence has a continuous range of intermediate scales, and this two-group picture is a simplification of the lecture.

The argument has three steps.

  1. Small length scales have small time scales. A small eddy turns over quickly and exists only for a short time before its energy is dissipated.
  2. Therefore the small-scale motion can adjust quickly to changes in the supply of energy. The net rate of change of the small-scale energy is related to the time scale of the flow as a whole, which is long. That net rate must therefore be small compared with the rate at which energy is dissipated, and the rate of energy supply to the small scales is approximately equal to the rate of dissipation.
  3. Because the small scales are fast, they may be statistically independent of the relatively slow large-scale turbulence and of the mean flow. The small-scale motion then depends only on the rate at which it receives energy, which equals \(\varepsilon\), and on \(\nu\).

The lecture calls this the basis of Kolmogorov's universal equilibrium theory of the small-scale structure. Steps 2 and 3 are hypotheses. Step 1 is an assumption here; section "The scale ratios widen as the Reynolds number increases" shows that it is consistent with the scales it produces. Chapter 10 states Kolmogorov's hypotheses formally. This chapter uses only their consequence for dimensions.

The Kolmogorov scales follow from the dimensions of the dissipation rate and the viscosity L12 13:58

If the small-scale motion depends only on \(\varepsilon\) and \(\nu\), every length, time and velocity that characterizes it must be a product \(\nu^a\varepsilon^b\) with the right dimensions. With \([\nu]=\mathrm{L^2T^{-1}}\) and \([\varepsilon]=\mathrm{L^2T^{-3}}\), a product \(\nu^a\varepsilon^b\) has dimensions \(\mathrm L^{2a+2b}\mathrm T^{-a-3b}\). A length requires \(2a+2b=1\) and \(a+3b=0\), so \(a=\tfrac34\), \(b=-\tfrac14\). A time requires \(2a+2b=0\) and \(a+3b=-1\), so \(a=\tfrac12\), \(b=-\tfrac12\). A velocity requires \(2a+2b=1\) and \(a+3b=1\), so \(a=b=\tfrac14\). The Kolmogorov length, time and velocity scales are

\[ \eta=\Bigl(\frac{\nu^3}{\varepsilon}\Bigr)^{1/4},\qquad \tau_\eta=\Bigl(\frac{\nu}{\varepsilon}\Bigr)^{1/2},\qquad u_\eta=(\nu\varepsilon)^{1/4}. \tag{5.14} \]

The solutions are unique because each pair of exponent equations has the coefficient matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\1&3\end{pmatrix}\), whose determinant is \(4\neq0\). Equivalently, \(\nu\) and \(\varepsilon\) have independent dimensions, so no dimensionless number can be formed from them alone. This is the Buckingham pi argument the lecture refers to: each of the three sets \(\{\eta,\nu,\varepsilon\}\), \(\{\tau_\eta,\nu,\varepsilon\}\) and \(\{u_\eta,\nu,\varepsilon\}\) has three quantities and two independent dimensions, so each set has exactly one dimensionless group, and that group must be a constant. Its consequence is strong: any length that characterizes the small-scale motion must equal \(\eta\) times a pure number, and the argument cannot supply the number.

The lecture writes the time and velocity scales as \(\tau\) and \(v\). This book writes \(\tau_\eta\) and \(u_\eta\), because an italic \(v\) is hard to distinguish from \(\nu\) and because \(\tau\) without a subscript is needed for other times in later chapters. Kolmogorov's paper (p. 12, equations (17), (18) and (20)) defines the length \(\nu^{3/4}/\varepsilon^{1/4}\) and the time \((\nu/\varepsilon)^{1/2}\), and its velocity-difference moments are made dimensionless by \((\nu\varepsilon)^{1/2}\), the square of \(u_\eta\).

The Kolmogorov Reynolds number is one by construction. The Reynolds number formed with the Kolmogorov scales is

\[ \mathrm{Re}_\eta\equiv\frac{\eta u_\eta}\nu=\frac{\nu^{3/4}\varepsilon^{-1/4}\,\nu^{1/4}\varepsilon^{1/4}}{\nu}=1. \tag{5.15} \]

The lecture describes \(\mathrm{Re}_\eta=1\) as an assumption, chosen because the smallest scales should have the smallest Reynolds number. With the definitions (5.14) it is an algebraic identity, not an assumption. Kolmogorov's own construction shows why: he measures lengths in units of \(\eta\) and times in units of \(\tau_\eta\), and in those units the viscosity and the dissipation rate both become 1 (Kolmogorov 1941, p. 12). The physical content lies elsewhere. It is the claim that motions of size of order \(\eta\) have a Reynolds number of order one, so that inertia and viscosity are comparable there, and that the energy is dissipated at about this size. That claim inherits the undetermined constant of the dimensional argument: the dissipative motions may be several \(\eta\) across. The lecture's interpretation is that the small-scale motion is quite viscous and that the viscous dissipation adjusts itself to the energy supply by adjusting the length scale. Exercise 3 examines what changes if \(\eta\) is defined with a different constant.

The dependence on \(\varepsilon\) is weak. Doubling \(\varepsilon\) multiplies \(\eta\) by \(2^{-1/4}=0.84\) and \(\tau_\eta\) by \(2^{-1/2}=0.71\). The scales are useful only if \(\varepsilon\) can be estimated, and \(\varepsilon\) is difficult to measure in a turbulent flow because (5.13) requires all nine velocity derivatives. The next section estimates it from the large scales.

An inviscid estimate of the dissipation rate L13 4:07

The kinetic energy per unit mass of the large eddies is of order \(u^2\) (more precisely \(\tfrac12u^2\) for a motion of speed \(u\)). The lecture makes a plausible assumption: the rate at which the large eddies supply energy to the small eddies is proportional to the reciprocal of the large-eddy time scale \(\ell/u\). The supply rate is then of order \(u^2\cdot u/\ell\). In equilibrium this supply equals the dissipation rate:

\[ \varepsilon\sim\frac{u^3}{\ell}. \tag{5.16} \]

This estimate contains no viscosity. It states that the viscous dissipation can be estimated from the large-scale dynamics, which are inviscid. Dissipation is a passive process in this picture: it proceeds at the rate set by the inertial behaviour of the large eddies, and viscosity determines only the size \(\eta\) at which it occurs. The lecture calls this one of the cornerstone assumptions of turbulence theory: the large eddies lose a significant fraction of their kinetic energy \(\tfrac12u^2\) within one turnover time \(\ell/u\). The nonlinear mechanism that makes small eddies out of large ones is therefore as dissipative as its characteristic time permits, although the dissipation itself occurs at the small scales.

Direct viscous loss of the large eddies is negligible. Viscosity also acts on the large eddies directly. The time for viscosity to diffuse momentum across a distance \(\ell\) is of order \(\ell^2/\nu\), so the direct viscous energy loss of the large eddies proceeds at a rate of order \(u^2/(\ell^2/\nu)=\nu u^2/\ell^2\). Compared with (5.16),

\[ \frac{\nu u^2/\ell^2}{u^3/\ell}=\frac{\nu}{u\ell}=\mathrm{Re}_\ell^{-1}. \tag{5.17} \]

For the running case take \(\ell=10^{3}\ \mathrm m\), the observed depth, and \(u=0.3\ \mathrm{m/s}\). Then \(\mathrm{Re}_\ell=0.3\times10^{3}/(1.5\times10^{-5})=2\times10^{7}\). The turnover time is \(\ell/u=3.3\times10^{3}\ \mathrm s\), about 56 minutes. The viscous time is \(\ell^2/\nu=6.7\times10^{10}\ \mathrm s\), about 2100 years. Their ratio, \(2\times10^{7}\), is \(\mathrm{Re}_\ell\), as (5.17) requires. The estimate (5.16) gives \(\varepsilon\sim(0.3)^3/10^{3}=2.7\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\). This is a value for the layer as a whole. Close to the ground the eddies are smaller and the local dissipation rate differs; this chapter makes no claim about measured profiles of \(\varepsilon\) in the atmosphere.

The constant. Write the estimate with its undetermined factor as \(\varepsilon=C_\varepsilon u^3/\ell\). McComb, Berera, Yoffe and Linkmann (2015) state that this form was given by Taylor in 1935 and that many authors call it the Taylor dissipation surrogate (accepted manuscript, p. 2; the attribution is theirs). They measured \(C_\varepsilon\) in direct numerical simulations of forced isotropic turbulence, with \(u\) the root-mean-square velocity and \(\ell\) the integral length scale (pp. 1–2). The integral length scale is a statistical measure of the size of the largest eddies. Its standard definition is the integral, over the separation between two points, of the correlation between the velocities at those points, divided by the velocity variance. Chapter 8 defines it as \(L_{11}\); here it is the statistical counterpart of the characteristic size \(\ell\). Their data, for integral-scale Reynolds numbers \(R_L\) up to 5875, fit \(C_\varepsilon=C_{\varepsilon,\infty}+C'/R_L\) with \(C_{\varepsilon,\infty}=0.468\pm0.006\) and \(C'=18.9\pm1.3\) (p. 1; they write \(C\) for \(C'\)). Two other simulation studies that they review report asymptotes of about 0.4 (Donzis and co-authors) and about 0.5 (Bos and co-authors) (p. 3). Their energy balance has the form \(\varepsilon=A_3u^3/\ell+A_2\nu u^2/\ell^2\), with dimensionless functions \(A_3\) and \(A_2\) of the two-point separation, obtained from the second- and third-order structure functions (p. 7): an inertial term and a viscous term with the same forms as (5.16) and the numerator of (5.17), so the viscous correction to \(C_\varepsilon\) decreases as \(1/R_L\). Two boundaries apply. The value 0.468 belongs to their definitions of \(u\) and \(\ell\) and to isotropic turbulence in a periodic box; it is not a measured constant for the atmospheric boundary layer. The form \(C_\varepsilon=C_{\varepsilon,\infty}+C'/R_L\) is derived in the paper from the Kármán–Howarth equation; the existence of a finite, nonzero limit \(C_{\varepsilon,\infty}\) is taken there from Kolmogorov's inertial-range phenomenology (p. 7), so (5.16) with a constant coefficient remains an assumption that the simulations support at the Reynolds numbers reached.

The scale ratios widen as the Reynolds number increases L13 16:26

Substitute (5.16) into (5.14). For the length, \(\eta=(\nu^3\ell/u^3)^{1/4}\), so \(\eta/\ell=(\nu^3/(u^3\ell^3))^{1/4}\). Proceeding the same way for the time and the velocity,

\[ \frac\eta\ell\sim\mathrm{Re}_\ell^{-3/4},\qquad \frac{\tau_\eta}{\ell/u}\sim\mathrm{Re}_\ell^{-1/2},\qquad \frac{u_\eta}{u}\sim\mathrm{Re}_\ell^{-1/4}. \tag{5.18} \]

With the constant kept, \(\eta/\ell=C_\varepsilon^{-1/4}\mathrm{Re}_\ell^{-3/4}\), \(\tau_\eta u/\ell=C_\varepsilon^{-1/2}\mathrm{Re}_\ell^{-1/2}\) and \(u_\eta/u=C_\varepsilon^{1/4}\mathrm{Re}_\ell^{-1/4}\). The relations (5.18) depend on two inputs: the dimensional result (5.14), which rests on the equilibrium hypothesis, and the inviscid estimate (5.16), which rests on the energy-supply assumption.

For the running case, with \(C_\varepsilon=1\) and \(\mathrm{Re}_\ell=2\times10^{7}\): \(\eta=3.3\ \mathrm{mm}\), \(\tau_\eta=0.75\ \mathrm s\) and \(u_\eta=4.5\ \mathrm{mm/s}\). The largest eddy is \(\ell/\eta=3.0\times10^{5}\) times larger than the smallest, and it lives \((\ell/u)/\tau_\eta=4.5\times10^{3}\) times longer. With \(C_\varepsilon=0.47\) instead of 1, \(\eta\) is larger by the factor \(0.47^{-1/4}=1.21\), that is \(4.0\ \mathrm{mm}\). The exponent changes \(\eta\) by orders of magnitude across the range of \(\mathrm{Re}_\ell\); the constant changes it by about 20 %.

Figure 5.1 places the results of the chapter on one axis. The separation between the largest and smallest scales widens as \(\mathrm{Re}_\ell\) increases. At fixed \(\ell\), doubling \(\mathrm{Re}_\ell\) multiplies \(\eta\) by \(2^{-3/4}=0.59\). This widening supports the time-scale assumption of step 1 in the equilibrium argument: \(\tau_\eta/(\ell/u)\sim\mathrm{Re}_\ell^{-1/2}\) becomes small at large \(\mathrm{Re}_\ell\). It is a consistency check, not a proof, because (5.18) was derived with the help of that assumption. The lecture draws the corresponding conclusion: one may suspect that the statistical independence and the dynamical equilibrium of the small-scale structure are most evident at very large Reynolds number.

The lecture illustrates the effect with a sketch of two turbulent jets from the same nozzle, so that the largest scale is the same in both. The caption attributes the sketch to a film sequence by R. W. Stewart (1969). The jet at the higher Reynolds number has the finer small-scale structure. Two turbulent flows with the same largest scale and different Reynolds numbers differ mainly in the size of their smallest eddies.

Energy is carried by large scales and vorticity by small scales L13 23:45

The vorticity \(\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf U\) is the local rate of rotation of the fluid (twice the local angular velocity). It is built from velocity gradients, so it has units of \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\), and the vorticity of eddies of a given size is of the order of the reciprocal of their time scale. The small eddies have vorticity of order \(u_\eta/\eta=1/\tau_\eta\) and the large eddies of order \(u/\ell\). By (5.18),

\[ \frac{1/\tau_\eta}{u/\ell}\sim\mathrm{Re}_\ell^{1/2},\qquad \frac{u_\eta^2}{u^2}\sim\mathrm{Re}_\ell^{-1/2}. \tag{5.19} \]

The first ratio is large and the second is small. The vorticity of the small-scale eddies is much larger than that of the large-scale motion, while their kinetic energy per unit mass is much smaller. For the running case, \(1/\tau_\eta=1.34\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) and \(u/\ell=3\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), a ratio of \(4.5\times10^{3}\); \(u_\eta^2/u^2=2.2\times10^{-4}\). The lecture states this as typical of all turbulence: most of the energy is associated with the large-scale motions, and most of the vorticity with the small-scale motions.

Kolmogorov's paper gives an exact version of the gradient statement for locally isotropic turbulence (turbulence whose velocity differences over small separations have statistics that do not change under rotation or reflection of the axes; Kolmogorov 1941, Definition 2, p. 10). His equations (11) and (14) state that the mean dissipation equals \(15\nu\langle(\partial u_1/\partial x_1)^2\rangle\), where \(\partial u_1/\partial x_1\) is a velocity derivative taken along the direction of that velocity component (Kolmogorov 1941, pp. 11–12). Hence the root-mean-square of that derivative is \((\varepsilon/15\nu)^{1/2}=1/(15^{1/2}\tau_\eta)\): the root-mean-square velocity gradient is fixed by \(\varepsilon\) and \(\nu\) and is of order \(1/\tau_\eta\), not of order \(u/\ell\). This is consistent with (5.19), under the isotropy assumption that Kolmogorov's equation requires.

A resolution budget for a direct simulation L12 24:36

A direct numerical simulation (DNS) solves (5.5), without a turbulence model, on a grid fine enough to represent every scale of motion. The lecture describes the Kolmogorov length as the first check of such a simulation: the grid spacing must be comparable to \(\eta\). Because \(\varepsilon\) is usually unknown before the simulation and the Reynolds number is known, (5.18) is the practical way to estimate \(\eta\) in advance.

Published practice quantifies "comparable". Yeung, Sreenivasan and Pope (2018) express resolution through \(k_{\max}\eta\), where \(k_{\max}\) is the largest wavenumber the grid represents (a wavenumber is \(2\pi\) divided by a wavelength; the source writes \(k\), later chapters of this book write \(\kappa\)), and relate it to the grid spacing \(\Delta x\) by \(\Delta x/\eta\approx2.96/(k_{\max}\eta)\) for their numerical method. They state that \(k_{\max}\eta\) of order 1.5 may be adequate for low-order statistics, and that larger values are required for higher-order statistics because of intermittency, the occurrence of rare, very intense small-scale events (p. 064603-3). The value 1.5 corresponds to \(\Delta x\approx2.0\,\eta\).

Grid size. A simulation domain must contain at least one large eddy, so it must be at least of size \(\ell\); real domains are several times larger. The number of grid points in each direction is then at least \(N\sim\ell/\Delta x\propto\ell/\eta\sim\mathrm{Re}_\ell^{3/4}\), and the number of points in three dimensions is \(N^3\propto\mathrm{Re}_\ell^{9/4}\). For the running case, with \(\Delta x=1.97\,\eta=6.6\ \mathrm{mm}\), \(N=1.5\times10^{5}\) and \(N^3=3.5\times10^{15}\). With \(\Delta x=\eta\), \(N^3=2.7\times10^{16}\).

Time steps and cost. With the explicit time-stepping used in such codes, the time step is limited by a Courant number \(C\) (not the Kolmogorov constant of later chapters), which Yeung, Sreenivasan and Pope estimate as \(C\approx\beta u'\Delta t/\Delta x\), with \(u'\) the root-mean-square velocity and \(\beta\approx12\) in their simulations (p. 064603-3). At fixed \(C\) the time step is proportional to \(\Delta x/u'\), and with \(u'\sim u\) a simulation of one large-eddy turnover time \(\ell/u\) needs a number of steps proportional to \(\ell/\Delta x=N\). The work is therefore of order \(N^3\times N=N^4\propto\mathrm{Re}_\ell^{3}\).

Pope (2004) reports the factor that \(N^4\) implies for one halving of the grid spacing: with the numerical methods usually employed, halving the grid spacing increases the computational cost by about \(2^4=16\) (p. 6). He also separates the two resources: halving the grid spacing typically increases the required CPU time by a factor of 16 and the required memory by a factor of \(2^3=8\) (p. 5). The memory factor is the growth of \(N^3\); the CPU factor includes the additional time steps.

Pope also notes that DNS requirements increase as a power of the Reynolds number, and that near a wall the structures that carry the transfer have lengths that scale with the viscous length; he quotes Bradshaw: "in the viscous near-wall region there are no large eddies" (p. 4). The running-case estimate treats the interior of the layer only. Resolving the region next to the ground adds a further requirement that this estimate does not include.

    if nu <= 0 or eps <= 0:
        raise ValueError("nu and eps must be positive")
    return (nu**3/eps)**0.25, (nu/eps)**0.5, (nu*eps)**0.25

What the estimates establish and what they do not

Every result in this chapter is an order-of-magnitude relation. Each one fixes a power law under stated assumptions and leaves a coefficient undetermined: the factor \(\tfrac14\) in the boundary-layer depth, the factor 1.327 in place of 2 in the laminar drag, the constant \(C_\varepsilon\) in the dissipation estimate, and the constant in front of \(\eta\). Where a coefficient is known, it comes from an exact solution (Blasius) or from measurement or simulation (McComb and co-authors), not from the scale argument.

The assumptions are of four kinds. The eddy diffusivity \(K\sim u\ell\) is a model. The statement that the largest eddies are as large as the flow width is an observation. The equilibrium of the small scales and their dependence on \(\varepsilon\) and \(\nu\) alone is a hypothesis. The inviscid estimate \(\varepsilon\sim u^3/\ell\) is an assumption with empirical support. A conclusion is no stronger than the weakest assumption it uses: the scale ratios (5.18) use the hypothesis and the assumption together.

The model uses one velocity and one length for the large eddies, although a real flow has a range of large scales that vary with position; the region next to a wall is the clearest exception (Pope 2004, p. 4). The equilibrium argument requires the small scales to be fast compared with changes in the large scales; when the large-scale flow changes on a time comparable to \(\tau_\eta\), the argument loses its basis. The scales (5.14) are built from the mean dissipation rate. Because of intermittency, higher-order statistics of the small scales need finer resolution in units of \(\eta\) than low-order statistics (Yeung, Sreenivasan and Pope 2018, p. 064603-3); a single mean length \(\eta\) does not describe the most intense small-scale events. The running case omits buoyancy, surface roughness and the details of rotation.

The quantities \(u\), \(\ell\) and \(\varepsilon\) have been used as characteristic scales without statistical definitions. The following chapters supply them: the equations of motion in chapter 6, the statistical description of random velocity fields in chapters 7 and 8, and the energy cascade and Kolmogorov's hypotheses in chapter 10, where \(\eta\) reappears as part of a formal theory.

Exercise 1 (derive and compute). A room of side \(L=4\ \mathrm m\) contains air (\(\nu=1.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), Prandtl number \(\nu/\Gamma=0.73\)) stirred by air movements of speed \(u=0.05\ \mathrm{m/s}\). (a) Estimate the time for heat to spread across the room by molecular diffusion and by eddies, and show that their ratio is the Péclet number. (b) Now impose the time \(T=L/u\) instead and estimate the molecular and turbulent spreading distances. Show that their ratio is \(\mathrm{Pe}^{1/2}\).

Solution

(a) The heat diffusivity is \(\Gamma=\nu/0.73=2.05\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). Molecular diffusion over a distance \(L\) needs \(T_m\sim L^2/\Gamma=16/(2.05\times10^{-5})=7.8\times10^{5}\ \mathrm s\), about 9 days. Eddies of speed \(u\) cross the room in \(T_t\sim L/u=80\ \mathrm s\). The ratio is \(T_m/T_t\sim uL/\Gamma=\mathrm{Pe}=0.05\times4/(2.05\times10^{-5})=9.7\times10^{3}\). The algebra is exact: \((L^2/\Gamma)/(L/u)=uL/\Gamma\).

(b) With \(T=80\ \mathrm s\), the molecular distance is \((\Gamma T)^{1/2}=(2.05\times10^{-5}\times80)^{1/2}=0.041\ \mathrm m\) and the turbulent distance is \(uT=4\ \mathrm m\). The ratio is \(99\), and \(\mathrm{Pe}^{1/2}=(9.7\times10^{3})^{1/2}=99\). In general \(uT/(\Gamma T)^{1/2}=(u^2T/\Gamma)^{1/2}\), and with \(T=L/u\) this is \((uL/\Gamma)^{1/2}\). The same physical comparison gives the first power of \(\mathrm{Pe}\) when a length is imposed and its square root when a time is imposed, because the molecular distance grows as \(T^{1/2}\) and the turbulent distance as \(T\). The eddy speed \(u=0.05\ \mathrm{m/s}\) is an assumed value for this exercise.

Exercise 2 (counterexample). A student claims that "a thin diffusive layer always has thickness \(L\,\mathrm{Re}^{-1/2}\)". Use the model problem (5.7) with \(\mathrm{Pe}=100\) to show that the claim is false, and identify the property of the boundary layer that produces the exponent \(-\tfrac12\).

Solution

Write \(s=L-x\) for the distance from the right boundary. From (5.7), \(\phi=(e^{\mathrm{Pe}(1-s/L)}-1)/(e^{\mathrm{Pe}}-1)\approx e^{-\mathrm{Pe}\,s/L}\) when \(\mathrm{Pe}\gg1\) and \(\mathrm{Pe}(1-s/L)\gg1\). So \(\phi\) falls from 1 to \(e^{-1}\) over \(s=L/\mathrm{Pe}\). At \(\mathrm{Pe}=100\), the exact value at \(s=L/100\) is \((e^{99}-1)/(e^{100}-1)=0.368=e^{-1}\) to three digits. The layer thickness is \(L/\mathrm{Pe}=0.01L\), whereas the claim gives \(L\,\mathrm{Pe}^{-1/2}=0.1L\), ten times too thick. The claim is false.

The exponent follows from which derivatives balance. In (5.7) advection and diffusion act along the same coordinate, so the balance in the layer is \(U\phi/\delta\sim\Gamma\phi/\delta^2\), giving \(\delta\sim\Gamma/U\), exponent \(-1\). In a boundary layer, advection acts along the flow over the distance \(L\) and diffusion acts across it over \(\ell\), so the balance is \(U/L\sim\nu/\ell^2\), giving exponent \(-\tfrac12\). The exponent \(-\tfrac12\) is a property of a slender layer that grows along the flow, not of thin layers in general.

Exercise 3 (check an assumption). The lecture describes \(\eta u_\eta/\nu=1\) as an assumption. (a) Suppose a textbook defines the dissipative length as \(\eta'=2(\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}\) and keeps \(u_\eta=(\nu\varepsilon)^{1/4}\). What is \(\eta'u_\eta/\nu\), and what physical prediction changes? (b) State a claim about the dissipative scales that can be tested, and name the kind of evidence that tests it.

Solution

(a) \(\eta'u_\eta/\nu=2\). No physical prediction changes. The dimensional argument determines the small-scale length only up to a pure number, so \(\eta\) and \(\eta'\) are equally valid choices of unit. With the convention (5.14), the Reynolds number is 1 by algebra (5.15); with \(\eta'\), it is 2 by algebra. The value of \(\mathrm{Re}_\eta\) is a property of the definition, not of the flow.

(b) A testable claim is that the motions in which most of the dissipation occurs have sizes of a few \(\eta\), so that their Reynolds number is of order one, independently of the large-scale Reynolds number. Evidence comes from experiments or simulations that measure where dissipation occurs in units of \(\eta\). The resolution studies cited in the chapter are indirect evidence of this kind: Yeung, Sreenivasan and Pope (2018) report that a grid spacing of about \(2\eta\) (\(k_{\max}\eta\approx1.5\)) may be adequate for low-order statistics, which would not be true if the dissipative motions were much smaller than \(\eta\).

Exercise 4 (compute). A laboratory air jet has large eddies with \(u=2\ \mathrm{m/s}\) and \(\ell=0.1\ \mathrm m\). Using \(\nu=1.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\) and \(C_\varepsilon=1\), compute \(\mathrm{Re}_\ell\), \(\varepsilon\), \(\eta\), \(\tau_\eta\) and \(u_\eta\). Check \(\mathrm{Re}_\eta\) and \(\ell/\eta\) against (5.15) and (5.18). Estimate the number of grid points for a DNS of a cube of side \(\ell\) with \(\Delta x=1.97\,\eta\), and compare it with the running case.

Solution

\(\mathrm{Re}_\ell=2\times0.1/(1.5\times10^{-5})=1.33\times10^{4}\). \(\varepsilon\sim u^3/\ell=8/0.1=80\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\), about \(3\times10^{6}\) times the atmospheric value, because \(u^3\) is about 300 times larger and \(\ell\) is \(10^{4}\) times smaller. Then \(\eta=((1.5\times10^{-5})^3/80)^{1/4}=8.1\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm m=81\ \mu\mathrm m\), \(\tau_\eta=(1.5\times10^{-5}/80)^{1/2}=4.3\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm s\) and \(u_\eta=(1.5\times10^{-5}\times80)^{1/4}=0.19\ \mathrm{m/s}\).

Checks: \(\eta u_\eta/\nu=8.06\times10^{-5}\times0.186/(1.5\times10^{-5})=1.00\). \(\ell/\eta=0.1/(8.06\times10^{-5})=1.24\times10^{3}\), and \(\mathrm{Re}_\ell^{3/4}=(1.33\times10^{4})^{3/4}=1.24\times10^{3}\).

Grid: \(\Delta x=1.97\times81\ \mu\mathrm m=0.16\ \mathrm{mm}\), \(N=0.1/(1.59\times10^{-4})=630\) per direction, \(N^3=2.5\times10^{8}\). The running case needs \(3.5\times10^{15}\) points, \(1.4\times10^{7}\) times more. The ratio follows from (5.18): \((2\times10^{7}/1.33\times10^{4})^{9/4}=1.4\times10^{7}\). The jet is within reach of a single simulation; the atmospheric layer is not.

Exercise 5 (derive). (a) Show that the turbulent drag estimate \(C_d\sim2(u/U)^2\) is equivalent to the statement that the mean wall stress is of order \(\rho u^2\). (b) Show that the laminar estimate \(D\sim\rho U^2\ell\) follows from the wall stress \(\tau_w\sim\mu U/\ell\). (c) At \(\mathrm{Re}=10^{7}\), compare the turbulent estimate with the Blasius value for \(u/U=0.03\) and for \(u/U=0.01\). What does the comparison show about the claim "turbulent skin friction exceeds laminar skin friction"?

Solution

(a) The drag per unit span is the wall stress integrated along the plate, \(D=\bar\tau_wL\), where \(\bar\tau_w\) is the mean over the plate. By (5.10), \(C_d=\bar\tau_wL/(\tfrac12\rho U^2L)=2\bar\tau_w/(\rho U^2)\). Setting this equal to \(2(u/U)^2\) gives \(\bar\tau_w=\rho u^2\). Each step is reversible, so the statements are equivalent, and \(u\) is a friction velocity.

(b) \(D\sim\tau_wL\sim\mu UL/\ell=\rho U^2\ell\cdot\nu L/(U\ell^2)\). By (5.8), \(\ell^2\sim\nu L/U\), so the last factor is of order one and \(D\sim\rho U^2\ell\).

(c) Blasius gives \(1.327\times10^{-7/2}=4.2\times10^{-4}\). The turbulent estimate is \(2(0.03)^2=1.8\times10^{-3}\), 4.3 times larger, for \(u/U=0.03\), and \(2(0.01)^2=2.0\times10^{-4}\), 0.48 times the laminar value, for \(u/U=0.01\). The scale argument alone does not decide the claim at this Reynolds number; the decision depends on the measured value of \(u/U\). What the argument does establish is the trend: at fixed \(u/U\) the ratio of turbulent to laminar coefficient grows as \(\mathrm{Re}^{1/2}\), so above some Reynolds number the turbulent coefficient is larger for any fixed \(u/U\).

Exercise 6 (judge a design). A group proposes to simulate the running-case boundary layer with grid spacing \(\Delta=1\ \mathrm m\) and molecular viscosity only, arguing that "the eddies that carry momentum are hundreds of metres across". (a) Which part of the scale range does the grid leave out? (b) The smallest resolved motions have size \(\Delta\) and some velocity \(u_\Delta\lt u\). Estimate the viscous dissipation rate they can produce, and show that it cannot equal \(\varepsilon\sim2.7\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\). What happens to the energy? (c) By what factor would the cost rise if the grid were refined to \(\Delta x=6.6\ \mathrm{mm}\)?

Solution

(a) The grid represents motions from the domain size down to about 1 m. It leaves out the range from 1 m to \(\eta=3.3\ \mathrm{mm}\), a factor of 300 in length, about 2.5 decades of the 5.5 decades between \(\ell\) and \(\eta\).

(b) Velocity gradients at the grid scale are of order \(u_\Delta/\Delta\), so viscosity dissipates at a rate of order \(\nu(u_\Delta/\Delta)^2\). To dissipate \(\varepsilon\), the gradient would have to be \((\varepsilon/\nu)^{1/2}=(2.7\times10^{-5}/1.5\times10^{-5})^{1/2}=1.34\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), that is \(1/\tau_\eta\). At \(\Delta=1\ \mathrm m\) this requires \(u_\Delta=1.34\ \mathrm{m/s}\), more than four times the velocity \(u=0.3\ \mathrm{m/s}\) of the largest eddies, which contradicts \(u_\Delta\lt u\). The resolved motions therefore cannot dissipate energy at the rate at which the large eddies supply it. The energy accumulates at the smallest resolved scales, and the simulation does not represent the energy balance of the real layer. A coarse simulation needs an added model that removes energy at the grid scale; models of this kind are the subject of the chapters on large-eddy simulation.

(c) The ratio of spacings is \(1/0.0066=152\), that is \(\log_2152=7.25\) halvings. With a cost factor of about 16 per halving (Pope 2004), the cost rises by \(16^{7.25}=2^{29}\approx5\times10^{8}\). The proposal's argument is correct about where momentum is carried and incorrect about where energy is dissipated, and the second point decides whether the simulation can omit a model.
