Direct numerical simulation: pseudo-spectral methods and cost

A direct numerical simulation of isotropic turbulence at a Taylor-scale Reynolds number of 800 needs about \(9.2\times10^{3}\) grid points in each direction, \(7.8\times10^{11}\) in total, and \(2.1\times10^{5}\) time steps; on a computer that performs \(10^{9}\) floating-point operations per second the run takes about 5000 years. At a Taylor-scale Reynolds number of 25 the slide's table gives 104 points per direction and 20 minutes (the recomputation below: 105 and 22 minutes). The cost grows as the sixth power of the Taylor-scale Reynolds number, or the cube of the large-scale Reynolds number; the lecture presents this growth as the motivation for modelling the small scales instead of resolving them. This chapter derives each factor of that estimate from four stated choices: the size of the periodic box, the smallest resolved length, the time step and the duration of the run. It shows how the nonlinear term is evaluated so that one time step costs of order \(N^{3}\log N\) operations instead of \(N^{6}\), why that evaluation creates aliasing errors and how codes remove them, and it recomputes the lecture's table of costs from the model spectrum. The recomputation reproduces the table to within 1 % in the number of points, finds one misprinted entry, and shows where the lecture's estimate and the grids of published simulations differ by a factor of about 3.

The objects of the chapter and the running case

The formal model is the periodic box of chapter 12. The velocity \(\mathbf u(\mathbf x,t)\) of an incompressible fluid of constant density \(\rho\) and constant kinematic viscosity \(\nu\) is periodic with period \(\mathcal L\) in each of the three Cartesian directions, divergence-free, and has zero average over the box; as in chapter 12, \(\mathbf u\) is the whole field, because the mean velocity is zero, and it obeys the Navier–Stokes equations of chapter 6. The field is statistically homogeneous and, where a formula needs it, isotropic. The box side is \(\mathcal L\) (the slides write a script L); the plain letter \(L\) is reserved, as in chapter 11, for the large-scale length

\[ L\equiv\frac{k^{3/2}}{\varepsilon},\qquad \mathrm{Re}_L\equiv\frac{k^{1/2}L}{\nu}=\frac{k^{2}}{\varepsilon\nu}, \]

where \(k=\tfrac12\langle u_iu_i\rangle\) is the turbulent kinetic energy per unit mass and \(\varepsilon\) the dissipation rate (chapter 11, (11.25)). The other statistical scales are the longitudinal integral length \(L_{11}\) of chapter 8, (8.28), the Kolmogorov length \(\eta=(\nu^{3}/\varepsilon)^{1/4}\) and time \(\tau_\eta=(\nu/\varepsilon)^{1/2}\), the root-mean-square velocity \(u'=(2k/3)^{1/2}\), and the Taylor-scale Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) of chapter 11, which the slides write \(R_\lambda\). The numerical objects are the number of grid points (or Fourier modes) per direction \(N\), the lowest wavenumber \(\kappa_0=2\pi/\mathcal L\), the highest retained wavenumber \(\kappa_{\max}\), the grid spacing \(\Delta x\), the time step \(\Delta t\), the number of time steps \(M\) and the time \(T_G\), in days, that a computer performing \(10^{9}\) floating-point operations per second (one gigaflop) needs for the run. Each is defined again where it enters.

Two exact relations of chapter 11 carry the Reynolds-number dependence of every cost below. For isotropic turbulence, (11.26) gives

\[ \frac{L}{\eta}=\mathrm{Re}_L^{3/4},\qquad \mathrm{Re}_\lambda=\Bigl(\frac{20}{3}\mathrm{Re}_L\Bigr)^{1/2}. \tag{17.1} \]

The first is an identity: substituting the definitions, \((L/\eta)^{4/3}=k^{2}\varepsilon^{-4/3}\varepsilon^{1/3}\nu^{-1}=\mathrm{Re}_L\). It is not the order-of-magnitude relation \(\eta/\ell\sim\mathrm{Re}_\ell^{-3/4}\) of chapter 5, (5.18), whose \(\sim\) hides the undetermined constant \(C_\varepsilon\) of the estimate \(\varepsilon=C_\varepsilon u^{3}/\ell\) (chapter 5 writes \(\eta/\ell=C_\varepsilon^{-1/4}\mathrm{Re}_\ell^{-3/4}\) with the constant kept). The lecture refers to the earlier relation (L47 23:51); the cost estimate uses the identity, and that is why its prefactors are definite numbers. The second relation uses the isotropic dissipation formula (11.24) and holds only under isotropy.

The running case. The air jet of chapters 6–8 and 11 at its downstream station: \(\nu=1.4607\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), \(k=6\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\), \(\varepsilon=80\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\), so that \(L=0.1837\ \mathrm m\), \(\eta=79.0\ \mu\mathrm m\), \(\mathrm{Re}_L=3.08\times10^{4}\) and \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=453\). The jet is neither homogeneous nor isotropic; the question is what a simulation of homogeneous isotropic turbulence with these scales would cost, which is the closest a periodic-box simulation can come to the jet station. Chapter 12 chose a box of side \(\mathcal L=1\ \mathrm m\) for the same scales and found \(N=6.4\times10^{3}\), or \(2.6\times10^{11}\) points, at \(\kappa_{\max}\eta=1.5\) with the truncation \(\kappa_{\max}=\sqrt2N\kappa_0/3\). The section "What the truncation rule does to the count" shows that the lecture's recipe gives a smaller box and a different truncation rule, and reconciles the two numbers.

The model spectrum. Several criteria below need the energy spectrum \(E(\kappa)\) of chapter 8, (8.32), at a given Reynolds number. The lecture uses the model spectrum of chapter 14, (14.9), with the factors \(f_L\) and \(f_\eta\) defined there; it is restated here because the program solves its constants at each Reynolds number:

\[ E(\kappa)=C\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3}f_L(\kappa L)f_\eta(\kappa\eta),\qquad f_L(x)=\Bigl[\frac{x}{(x^{2}+c_L)^{1/2}}\Bigr]^{5/3+p_0},\qquad f_\eta(x)=\exp\bigl\{-\beta\bigl[(x^{4}+c_\eta^{4})^{1/4}-c_\eta\bigr]\bigr\}, \tag{17.2} \]

with \(C=1.5\), \(p_0=2\) and \(\beta=5.2\) as on the slides. The two remaining constants are fixed at each Reynolds number by the integral conditions of chapter 14, (14.11)–(14.12): the spectrum must carry the energy and the dissipation. \(L_{11}\) then follows from the isotropic relation (14.15), \(\int_0^\infty E/\kappa\,d\kappa=\tfrac{4}{3\pi}kL_{11}\), which chapter 14 derives from the one-dimensional spectrum at \(\kappa_1=0\):

\[ \int_0^\infty E\,d\kappa=k,\qquad \int_0^\infty2\nu\kappa^{2}E\,d\kappa=\varepsilon,\qquad L_{11}=\frac{3\pi}{4k}\int_0^\infty\frac{E}{\kappa}\,d\kappa. \tag{17.3} \]

The program of this chapter solves (17.3) for \(c_L\) and \(c_\eta\) at finite Reynolds number (chapter 14 solved them in the infinite-Reynolds-number limit, where they are 6.783 and 0.402). At \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=10^{5}\) it finds \(c_L=6.781\), \(c_\eta=0.402\) and \(L_{11}/L=0.4302\), in agreement with chapter 14's 0.4301; at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=25\) it finds \(L_{11}/L=0.909\). The model is a curve fitted to measured spectra, not a solution of the equations of motion; every number below that uses it inherits its form.

Four sources of disagreement, and how numerical error trades against cost L46 0:05

Lecture 46 opens by completing the appraisal criteria of the chapter "Modeling approaches and criteria for appraising models" with the last one, accuracy. The slide defines accuracy as the attribute of a model that is determined by comparing its calculations with experimental measurements. It lists four sources of a discrepancy between a measured and a calculated property: (1) the inaccuracy of the model, for example a mixing length taken from boundary layers and applied to a jet; (2) the numerical error, such as the truncation error of a discretization; (3) the measurement error; and (4) discrepancies between the boundary conditions of the calculation and those of the experiment. The lecture's examples for (4) are a flow that is approximately two-dimensional in the experiment and exactly two-dimensional in the calculation, and the inflow of a jet, which is taken from measurements because the long upstream pipe is too expensive to compute; the calculation must then choose between the measured mean profile alone and the mean with fluctuations of the measured root-mean-square amplitude. The lecture judges measurement error to be the least important source when the experiment is done properly; that is the speaker's assessment, and this chapter has no data on it.

The slide ends with a remark that connects accuracy to cost: the truncation error scales as a positive power of \(\Delta x\), while the computational cost increases with \(1/\Delta x\), "possibly as a positive power". The exponent can be made definite for a time-dependent calculation in three dimensions with explicit time stepping. The number of grid points is proportional to \(\Delta x^{-3}\). If the time step is limited by a Courant number (the distance a fluid particle moves in one step, in units of \(\Delta x\); defined in the section "The time step and the number of steps"), then \(\Delta t\propto\Delta x\) and the number of steps for a fixed physical time is proportional to \(\Delta x^{-1}\). The work \(W\) is therefore proportional to \(\Delta x^{-4}\). For a method whose truncation error is proportional to \(\Delta x^{n}\),

\[ \text{error}\propto\Delta x^{n}\propto W^{-n/4}. \tag{17.4} \]

For a second-order method (\(n=2\)), ten times the work reduces the error by \(10^{1/2}=3.2\); halving \(\Delta x\) costs \(2^{4}=16\) times the work and reduces the error by 4. Pope (2004, p. 5) gives the same two factors for one halving of the grid spacing: the required memory and CPU time increase by about 8 and 16. Relation (17.4) assumes that the solution is smooth enough for the truncation error to follow its asymptotic power, and that the Courant number, not accuracy at the smallest time scale, sets \(\Delta t\). It does not apply to the Fourier derivative of chapter 12, whose error for a represented mode is zero to rounding; for that method the numerical error comes from the modes that are not represented and from aliasing, which the next sections treat.

Direct numerical simulation, and why homogeneous turbulence is the test case L46 7:34

The slide defines direct numerical simulation (DNS) as solving the Navier–Stokes equations, resolving all scales of motion, with initial and boundary conditions appropriate to the flow considered; each simulation produces a single realization of the flow. "Single realization" has the meaning of chapter 7: one sample from the ensemble, as one run of an experiment is one sample, so that statistics require averaging over the box, over time or over several runs. The slide states three appraisals. DNS was infeasible until the 1970s, when computers of sufficient power became available. Conceptually it is the simplest approach, and where it can be applied it is unrivalled in accuracy and in the level of description. The lecture gives the reason in speech: no additional constants and "no tuning factors" are specified (L46 8:40); the fluid properties, the geometry and the initial and boundary conditions are the only inputs. The cost is extremely high and grows so rapidly with the Reynolds number that the approach is limited to flows of low to moderate Reynolds number. The lecture qualifies the accuracy claim at once: it holds "if you do it properly" (L46 8:51), meaning on a grid fine enough to resolve the Kolmogorov scale.

"No adjustable constants" needs a qualification. A simulation has numerical parameters that the user chooses: the box size, the resolution, the time step, the duration and the treatment of aliasing. Each decides what the simulation omits, and the section "Resolution requirements: the box and the grid spacing" computes what the lecture's choices leave out. Their effect can be reduced by refinement; the effect of a model constant cannot. That is what "no tuning factors" correctly means. For the date, Rogallo (1981, p. 44) states that fast-Fourier-transform techniques in the simulation of isotropic turbulence were pioneered by Orszag and Patterson (1972), whose paper was not checked for this chapter.

The lecture then restricts the discussion to homogeneous turbulence (L46 9:41), and gives two reasons. Homogeneous turbulence is the simplest turbulent flow, because no statistic depends on position. And for it, pseudo-spectral methods, the preferred numerical approach because of their accuracy, are applicable, so that the cost can be examined with the accuracy of the derivatives set aside. The lecture's conclusion is an inequality: if a simulation of homogeneous turbulence at a given Reynolds number costs \(x\), a jet, pipe or channel flow at a comparable Reynolds number costs more than \(x\) (L46 10:32). The section "What the homogeneous estimate says about other flows" examines what this inequality does and does not establish.

The lecture repeats here, and again in Lectures 47 and 48, that spectral methods have "infinite accuracy" and "zero truncation error". Chapter 12, in its section "What the representation establishes and what it does not", showed that this holds for the derivative of a represented mode and not for the computed solution; the cost estimate needs only that the error of a well-resolved simulation is controlled by \(\kappa_{\max}\eta\), the time step and the treatment of aliasing.

The velocity field on a lattice of N cubed wavenumbers L46 12:24

In a DNS of homogeneous turbulence the solution domain is a cube of side \(\mathcal L\), and the velocity is represented by a finite Fourier series, the truncation of the series (12.2) of chapter 12:

\[ \mathbf u(\mathbf x,t)=\sum_{\boldsymbol\kappa}e^{i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf x}\,\hat{\mathbf u}(\boldsymbol\kappa,t),\qquad \boldsymbol\kappa=\kappa_0\mathbf n=\kappa_0(\mathbf e_1n_1+\mathbf e_2n_2+\mathbf e_3n_3),\qquad -\tfrac12N+1\le n_i\le\tfrac12N. \tag{17.5} \]

Here \(\kappa_0=2\pi/\mathcal L\) is the lowest non-zero wavenumber magnitude, \(\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2,\mathbf e_3\) are the unit vectors along the axes (the lecture calls them direction cosines; chapter 12 made the same correction), and the integers \(n_i\) take \(N\) values in each direction, so that \(N^{3}\) wavenumber vectors are represented. The slide states that the even number \(N\) determines the size of the simulation and therefore the Reynolds number that can be reached, and that \(N\) is typically a power of 2, with the examples 128 and 192. The second example is not a power of 2 (\(192=3\times2^{6}\)); fast-Fourier-transform algorithms also exist for lengths with small prime factors, and the slide's example reflects that, although the lecture's statement does not.

The largest wavenumber in each direction is (L46 15:05)

\[ \kappa_{\max}=\tfrac12N\kappa_0=\frac{\pi N}{\mathcal L},\qquad \Delta x=\frac{\mathcal L}{N}=\frac{\pi}{\kappa_{\max}}. \tag{17.6} \]

The second relation is the slide's statement that the spectral representation is equivalent to representing \(\mathbf u\) on a uniform grid of \(N^{3}\) points. The equivalence is exact in the following sense: \(N\) coefficients per direction and \(N\) values per direction carry the same information, and the discrete Fourier transform maps one set onto the other one-to-one. The relation \(\Delta x=\pi/\kappa_{\max}\) says that the shortest represented wave has two grid points per wavelength, \(2\pi/\kappa_{\max}=2\Delta x\). This wavenumber is the Nyquist wavenumber of the grid. Equation (17.6) assumes that every one of the \(N\) modes per direction is kept; the section "Aliasing, the two-thirds rule and phase shifting" shows that codes which remove aliasing discard some of them, so that their \(\kappa_{\max}\) is smaller than \(\tfrac12N\kappa_0\).

The lecture describes the periodic boundary conditions as a consequence of the Fourier representation (L46 26:06). The model assumes periodicity so that the series can be used; chapter 12, section "A periodic velocity field is a sum of Fourier modes", stated what the assumption removes: motions larger than \(\mathcal L\), and the decay of the correlation at separations comparable to \(\mathcal L\).

Spectral and pseudo-spectral evaluation of the nonlinear term L46 17:41

The discrete Fourier transform (DFT) gives the one-to-one mapping between the coefficients \(\hat{\mathbf u}(\boldsymbol\kappa,t)\) and the velocities \(\mathbf u(\mathbf x,t)\) at the \(N^{3}\) grid nodes. The fast Fourier transform (FFT) is an algorithm that computes the same DFT with fewer operations; it is not a different transform. A spectral method advances the Fourier modes in small time steps \(\Delta t\) according to the Navier–Stokes equations written in wavenumber space, the system of ordinary differential equations (12.19) of chapter 12, one equation for each retained wavevector.

The operation count of the fully spectral method. The slide states that advancing the modes in wavenumber space requires \(O(N^{6})\) operations per step. The count follows from the nonlinear term. In chapter 12, (12.20), the coefficient of the nonlinear term at the wavevector \(\boldsymbol\kappa\) is a sum over all wavevectors \(\boldsymbol\kappa'\) of products of the coefficients at \(\boldsymbol\kappa'\) and \(\boldsymbol\kappa-\boldsymbol\kappa'\). For each of the \(N^{3}\) output wavevectors the sum has up to \(N^{3}\) terms, so one evaluation needs of order \(N^{3}\times N^{3}=N^{6}\) multiplications. (The lecture describes the sum as a double summation over two wavevectors; with the constraint that the two add to \(\boldsymbol\kappa\), only one of them is free, which is the form (12.20).) The direct sum in Part 5 of this chapter's program is such an evaluation, for \(16^{3}\) modes.

The pseudo-spectral evaluation. To avoid this cost, pseudo-spectral methods evaluate the nonlinear terms differently. The velocity coefficients are transformed to physical space; there the products \(u_ju_k\) are formed, one multiplication per grid point; and the products are transformed back to wavenumber space, where they are differentiated by multiplication with \(i\kappa_k\) (chapter 12, (12.9)) and projected (12.19). The name records that the method is spectral in its derivatives and in its time advance, but not in its evaluation of the products. Each transform of an \(N^{3}\) array by the FFT costs of order \(N^{3}\log N\) operations, so the slide's count for one evaluation is \(O(N^{3}\log N)\). The saving is large: the ratio \(N^{6}/(N^{3}\log_2N)\) is \(2.1\times10^{6}\) at \(N=256\) and \(1.1\times10^{8}\) at \(N=1024\). The number of transforms per step is a fixed number, independent of \(N\): with three inverse transforms for the velocity components and six forward transforms for the products \(u_ju_k\) per stage, it is 9 per stage and 18 to 36 for a scheme of two to four stages. Rogallo (1981, p. 44) describes how his code forms the nonlinear terms in physical space and reduces the storage to four words per grid node with a modified pressure.

The slide then states the price: an aliasing error that must be removed or controlled is introduced. The lecture attributes it to "going back and forth between physical space and Fourier space". The transforms themselves are exact; the error arises from the product, as the next section shows.

The main numerical and computational issues of pseudo-spectral methods, according to the next slide (L46 21:50), are the time-stepping strategy, the control of aliasing errors and the implementation on distributed-memory parallel computers, in which the arrays are divided among many processors. The slide names Rogallo's report of 1981 as the treatment of each of these issues and as the basis of many later codes for homogeneous turbulence. Yeung, Sreenivasan and Pope (2018, p. 064603-2) describe their code as a massively parallel implementation of "a well-known Fourier pseudospectral algorithm" and cite Rogallo's report for it.

Aliasing, the two-thirds rule and phase shifting L46 21:24

The lecture states that aliasing errors must be controlled, and does not show how. This section supplies the mechanism and the two standard remedies from Rogallo's report (1981, pp. 45–50), and checks both with the program. Consider one direction, with grid points \(x_j=j\mathcal L/N\), \(j=0,\dots,N-1\). Two waves whose wavenumbers differ by \(N\kappa_0\) take the same values at every grid point:

\[ e^{i(\kappa+N\kappa_0)x_j}=e^{i\kappa x_j}\,e^{i2\pi j}=e^{i\kappa x_j}. \tag{17.7} \]

The grid cannot distinguish them; the wave of wavenumber \(\kappa+N\kappa_0\) is said to be aliased to \(\kappa\). In this section \(n\), \(m\) and \(k\) are integer wavenumbers in units of \(\kappa_0\), the letters of Rogallo's report; \(k\) is not the kinetic energy. Rogallo (p. 45) states this in the form that a wave of integer wavenumber \(k\) in physical space is transformed into the Fourier coefficient at \(k\bmod N\), where \(N\) is the length of the transform, and that the aliasing problem occurs when operations in physical space produce a wavenumber span greater than \(M\). A product does that. If \(a\) and \(b\) contain integer wavenumbers \(n\) and \(m\) up to \(\pm\tfrac12N\), their product contains \(n+m\) up to \(\pm N\). Computing the product at the grid points and transforming it gives, for the coefficient at \(k\),

\[ \tilde c_k=\sum_{n+m=k}\hat a_n\hat b_m+\sum_{n+m=k\pm N}\hat a_n\hat b_m, \tag{17.8} \]

(Rogallo, p. 47, whose transform length \(M\) is written \(N\) here). The first sum is the correct coefficient of the product, the convolution (12.20) of chapter 12. The second sum is the alias error: contributions of pairs whose sum lies outside the represented range and has been folded back into it. Chapter 12's program showed one instance on 16 points: the square of \(\sin5x\) contains the wavenumber 10, which a 16-point grid folds onto \(-6\).

Truncation: the two-thirds rule. Suppose only the coefficients with \(|n|\le K\) are kept (\(K\) is chapter-local here: the largest retained integer wavenumber, not the eddy diffusivity of chapters 4 and 5), and all others are set to zero before the product is formed and after it is transformed back. A pair of retained modes has \(|n+m|\le2K\). If \(|n+m|\gt\tfrac12N\), its contribution appears at the folded wavenumber \(n+m\mp N\), whose magnitude is at least \(N-2K\). The alias error lands only on discarded wavenumbers if \(N-2K\gt K\), that is if

\[ K\lt\frac N3. \tag{17.9} \]

With this truncation the retained coefficients of the product are exact. Rogallo (p. 47) derives the same bound and states that the wavenumbers carried are reduced from \(1-\tfrac12N\le k\le\tfrac12N\) to \(-\tfrac13N\le k\le\tfrac13N\), "popularly referred to as the 2/3 rule"; he obtains it also as the shortest transform length, \(\tfrac32M\), that does not alias a product into the retained modes. In three dimensions the rule applied to each component keeps the cube \(|n_i|\lt N/3\), a fraction \((2/3)^{3}=0.30\) of the \(N^{3}\) coefficients. The highest wavenumber retained in every direction is then \(\kappa_{\max}=\tfrac13N\kappa_0\), not \(\tfrac12N\kappa_0\).

Phase shifting. The alias sum in (17.8) can be made to change sign. Evaluate the product on a grid shifted by half a cell, \(x_j+\tfrac12\Delta x\). In wavenumber space the shift multiplies each coefficient \(\hat a_n\) by \(e^{in\kappa_0\Delta x/2}\). After the product is formed and transformed, the result is shifted back by multiplication with \(e^{-ik\kappa_0\Delta x/2}\). The correct terms, with \(n+m=k\), are unchanged; the alias terms, with \(n+m=k\pm N\), are multiplied by \(e^{\pm iN\kappa_0\Delta x/2}=e^{\pm i\pi}=-1\). The average of the evaluations on the two grids is therefore the alias-free sum (Rogallo, p. 48). This removes the error while keeping all \(N\) modes, at the price of two evaluations of the nonlinear term.

Three dimensions. In three dimensions a product can be aliased in one, two or three directions independently, giving eight possible outcomes (Rogallo, p. 48). A shift of half a cell along all three axes multiplies a single alias by \(-1\), a double alias by \((-1)^{2}=+1\) and a triple alias by \(-1\), so the two-grid average removes the single and triple aliases and keeps the double ones. Rogallo (p. 49) reports that Patterson and Orszag truncated the wavevectors with \(|\mathbf n|^{2}\gt2(N/3)^{2}\), that is, they kept the sphere

\[ |\mathbf n|\le\frac{\sqrt2}{3}N,\qquad \kappa_{\max}=\frac{\sqrt2}{3}N\kappa_0, \tag{17.10} \]

and combined this truncation with shifts; his own code truncates the wavevectors with two or more components greater than \(N/3\), a less severe cut, and also uses shifts. Why the sphere (17.10) eliminates the double and triple aliases can be seen from the lengths. A double alias requires \(\mathbf n+\mathbf m-\mathbf k=N(\pm\mathbf e_i\pm\mathbf e_j)\), a vector of length \(\sqrt2N\); a triple alias requires length \(\sqrt3N\). For three wavevectors inside the sphere, \(|\mathbf n+\mathbf m-\mathbf k|\le3\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt2}{3}N=\sqrt2N\), with equality only if all three have length \(\tfrac{\sqrt2}{3}N\) and are parallel, which requires integer components \(\pm N/3\) and is impossible when \(N\) is a power of 2. So only single aliases remain, and the two-grid shift removes them. Rogallo (p. 50) adds a cheaper variant: instead of two evaluations at each step, a random shift is drawn at each step, so that the single-alias error of one step is nearly cancelled at the next; in his Runge–Kutta scheme the residual alias error is of the order of the square of the time step.

The truncation (17.10) is the one Yeung, Sreenivasan and Pope (2018, p. 064603-3) state for their code: on an \(N^{3}\) domain of side \(2\pi\) the highest resolvable wavenumber is \(\kappa_{\max}=\sqrt2N/3\), with both phase shifting and truncation used for the control of aliasing errors (p. 064603-2). They state (p. 064603-4) that residual aliasing errors of order \(\Delta t^{2}\) remain in Rogallo's algorithm, and (p. 064603-11) that the error can be removed entirely, at a cost, by a simulation on \((2N)^{3}\) points from which all content at \(|\boldsymbol\kappa|\ge\sqrt2N/3\) is removed at every Runge–Kutta substage; at a fixed Courant number this alias-free procedure requires 16 times the resources (p. 064603-12). The factor 16 is the cost model of this chapter: \((2N)^{3}\) is 8 times the points, and halving \(\Delta x\) at a fixed Courant number doubles the number of steps.

Part 5 of the program tests these statements on a \(16^{3}\) grid. It draws two random fields with coefficients on a given set of wavevectors, computes their product by the pseudo-spectral route on one grid and as the average over two grids shifted by half a cell, and compares both with the direct sum over pairs. Without truncation (the program's untruncated set is \(|n_i|\le7\), \(15^{3}=3375\) modes; the Nyquist plane \(n_i=8\) of (17.5) is left out) the error is \(2.3\times10^{2}\) on one grid and \(1.5\times10^{2}\) after the shift, which removes only the single and triple aliases. With the cube \(|n_i|\le5\) (the two-thirds rule, \(5\lt16/3\)) the error is \(2.2\times10^{-13}\), rounding error, without any shift. With the sphere \(|\mathbf n|\le\sqrt2\cdot16/3\) the error is \(9.3\times10^{1}\) on one grid and \(3.7\times10^{-13}\) after the shift. The sphere keeps 1791 coefficients against 1331 for the cube, 35 % more, for the price of the second evaluation.

Resolution requirements: the box and the grid spacing L46 23:20

The slide states that the computational cost is largely determined by the resolution requirements. The box must be large enough to represent the energy-containing motions; the grid spacing must be small enough to resolve the dissipative scales; and the time step must be small enough for numerical accuracy. The lecture emphasizes that the box cannot be made small to reduce the cost: each requirement is a lower bound on the work.

The box. For isotropic turbulence with a given spectrum, the slide takes eight integral length scales as a reasonable lower limit on the box size (L47 2:36):

\[ \mathcal L=8L_{11}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\kappa_0L_{11}=\frac{2\pi}{8}=\frac\pi4\approx0.8. \tag{17.11} \]

The justification on the slide uses the spectrum: the peak of the energy spectrum is at \(\kappa L_{11}\approx1.3\), 10 % of the energy is at wavenumbers below \(\kappa L_{11}\approx1.0\), and with \(\kappa_0L_{11}\approx0.8\) approximately 5 % of the energy at the low-wavenumber end of the spectrum is not resolved. The lecture connects the factor 8 to the energy-containing range estimated in chapter 14, section "The energy-containing range". The program checks the three spectral statements on the model spectrum (17.2) at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=800\): the peak is at \(\kappa L_{11}=1.24\), the energy below \(\kappa L_{11}=1\) is 9.6 % of \(k\), and the energy below \(\kappa L_{11}=\pi/4\) is 5.7 %. The slide's three numbers agree with the model to within 5 %, 4 % and 0.7 percentage points.

The 5 % figure is a heuristic, and its assumption should be stated. It integrates the spectrum of unbounded isotropic turbulence below \(\kappa_0\). A periodic box has no wavenumbers between 0 and \(\kappa_0\), and its lowest shells contain only a few modes (6 wavevectors have \(|\mathbf n|=1\)); the turbulence that develops in the box is not the unbounded turbulence with its lowest 5 % removed, because the energy of the large scales and their forcing are set by the box. The criterion (17.11) therefore states what part of an assumed spectrum the box could hold, not how the statistics of the box differ from those of an unbounded flow. Chapter 12 stated the corresponding condition, \(\mathcal L\gg L_{11}\), and the 1 m box of its running case is \(12L_{11}\) by the model value of \(L_{11}\) computed below.

The grid spacing. The smallest, dissipative motions are characterized by the Kolmogorov length \(\eta\), and resolving them requires a small enough \(\Delta x/\eta\) or, equivalently, a large enough \(\kappa_{\max}\eta\) (L47 5:13). The slide states that the dissipation spectrum is extremely small beyond \(\kappa\eta=1.5\) and that experience shows \(\kappa_{\max}\eta\ge1.5\) to be the criterion for good resolution of the smallest scales. With (17.6),

\[ \kappa_{\max}\eta\ge1.5,\qquad \frac{\Delta x}{\eta}=\frac{\pi}{\kappa_{\max}\eta}=\frac{\pi}{1.5}\approx2.1. \tag{17.12} \]

The program finds that 0.22 % of the dissipation of the model spectrum lies above \(\kappa\eta=1.5\) at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=800\). That supports "extremely small" for the mean dissipation, under the model's exponential dissipation range. The lecture states the consequence correctly: the grid misses some dissipative motions, but very little of the dissipation. Two qualifications apply. First, \(\Delta x\approx2\eta\) means that the Kolmogorov length is not itself resolved by several grid points; the criterion rests on where the dissipation spectrum lies, not on the size of \(\eta\). Second, Yeung, Sreenivasan and Pope (2018, p. 064603-3) state that \(\kappa_{\max}\eta\) of order 1.5 "(without truly resolving the smallest scales) may be adequate" for low-order statistics, and that larger values are required for higher-order statistics, because of intermittency, especially at higher Reynolds number. Their tests at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\approx650\) use \(\kappa_{\max}\eta\approx1.4\), 2.8 and 5.6 (p. 064603-6). With their truncation (17.10), \(\Delta x=\mathcal L/N=2\sqrt2\pi/(3\kappa_{\max})\), so \(\kappa_{\max}\eta=1.5\) corresponds to \(\Delta x/\eta=2.96/1.5=1.97\) (their equation (2), p. 064603-3) instead of 2.09: the same \(\kappa_{\max}\) needs a finer grid when the modes above it are discarded.

The number of modes as a function of the Reynolds number L47 9:00

The two spatial requirements (17.11) and (17.12) determine the number of modes \(N^{3}\) as a function of the Reynolds number. From (17.6), \(N=2\kappa_{\max}/\kappa_0\). Multiplying and dividing by \(\eta\), \(L_{11}\) and \(L\) separates the factors that the criteria fix from those that the turbulence fixes:

\[ N=2\frac{\kappa_{\max}}{\kappa_0}=2\,\frac{\kappa_{\max}\eta}{\kappa_0L_{11}}\Bigl(\frac{L_{11}}{L}\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac L\eta\Bigr)=\frac{12}{\pi}\Bigl(\frac{L_{11}}{L}\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac L\eta\Bigr). \tag{17.13} \]

The factor \(12/\pi=2\times1.5/(\pi/4)\) contains the two criteria. The ratio \(L/\eta\) is \(\mathrm{Re}_L^{3/4}\) exactly, by (17.1). The ratio \(L_{11}/L\) depends on the shape of the spectrum; the slide obtains it from the model spectrum, in which it depends on the Reynolds number and tends to 0.43 at high Reynolds number. With that asymptote, \(12/\pi\times0.43=1.64\), which the slide rounds to 1.6, and (17.1) gives \(\mathrm{Re}_L^{3/4}=(3/20)^{3/4}\mathrm{Re}_\lambda^{3/2}=0.241\,\mathrm{Re}_\lambda^{3/2}\):

\[ N\approx1.6\frac L\eta=1.6\,\mathrm{Re}_L^{3/4}\approx0.4\,\mathrm{Re}_\lambda^{3/2},\qquad N^{3}\approx4.4\,\mathrm{Re}_L^{9/4}\approx0.06\,\mathrm{Re}_\lambda^{9/2}. \tag{17.14} \]

The arithmetic checks: \(1.64\times0.241=0.395\); \(1.64^{3}=4.4\); \(0.395^{3}=0.062\). The exponent \(\tfrac94\) is the one of chapter 5, section "A resolution budget for a direct simulation", which found \(N^{3}\propto\mathrm{Re}_\ell^{9/4}\) for a cube of the size of the largest eddy. What (17.14) adds is a definite prefactor, obtained from stated criteria and a model spectrum instead of an order-of-magnitude estimate.

What the truncation rule does to the count

Equation (17.13) uses \(\kappa_{\max}=\tfrac12N\kappa_0\) from (17.6): every mode up to the Nyquist wavenumber is kept. A code that removes aliasing keeps fewer, and needs a larger \(N\) to reach the same \(\kappa_{\max}\eta\). Write the retained maximum as \(\kappa_{\max}=fN\kappa_0\) (\(f\) is a chapter-local fraction, distinct from \(f_L\), \(f_\eta\) and from \(f(r)\), \(f(V)\)), with \(f=\tfrac12\) for (17.6), \(f=\sqrt2/3=0.471\) for the sphere (17.10) and \(f=\tfrac13\) for the two-thirds rule (17.9). At fixed \(\kappa_{\max}\eta\) and fixed box, \(N\propto1/f\), and the sphere needs \(\tfrac12/0.471=1.061\) times the \(N\) of (17.13), the cube 1.5 times. The work scales as \(N^{4}\), because the number of points is \(N^{3}\) and, at a fixed Courant number, the number of steps is proportional to \(1/\Delta x=N/\mathcal L\) (the next section; the argument is that of (17.4)). The work therefore rises by \(1.061^{4}=1.27\) for the sphere and \(1.5^{4}=5.06\) for the cube, before the second evaluation that exact phase shifting needs.

For the running case the program gives the following. With the model's \(L_{11}/L=0.454\) at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=453\), \(L_{11}=83.4\ \mathrm{mm}\) and the box (17.11) is \(\mathcal L=8L_{11}=0.667\ \mathrm m\). The time step and the number of steps L47 13:56

For the advance in time to be accurate, a fluid particle should move only a fraction of the grid spacing in one time step. Measuring the particle speed by \(k^{1/2}\), the slide states that in practice the Courant number so imposed is found to be approximately

\[ \frac{k^{1/2}\Delta t}{\Delta x}=\frac1{20}. \tag{17.15} \]

The duration of a simulation is typically about four times the turbulence time scale \(\tau=k/\varepsilon\). The number of time steps is therefore

\[ M=\frac{4\tau}{\Delta t}=4\,\frac k\varepsilon\cdot\frac{20\,k^{1/2}}{\Delta x}=80\,\frac L{\Delta x}=\frac{120}{\pi}\,\frac L\eta\approx9.2\,\mathrm{Re}_\lambda^{3/2}. \tag{17.16} \]

The hinge steps are \(k^{3/2}/\varepsilon=L\) and \(\Delta x=\pi\eta/1.5\) from (17.12), so \(80\times1.5/\pi=120/\pi=38.2\); then \(38.2\times0.241=9.2\) by (17.1). The \(L\) in (17.16) is the large-scale length \(k^{3/2}/\varepsilon\), not the box side: the number of steps is set by how far the energy-containing motions move, in grid spacings, during the run. Part 1 of the program reproduces the slide's \(M\) column for \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=25\) to 400 exactly to two figures.

The time step against the Kolmogorov time. The criterion (17.15) is set by advection across a grid cell, not by the time scale of the smallest eddies. The ratio of the two follows from (17.15), (17.12) and the identity \(u_\eta/k^{1/2}=\mathrm{Re}_L^{-1/4}\) (with \(u_\eta=\eta/\tau_\eta=(\varepsilon\nu)^{1/4}\), \((u_\eta/k^{1/2})^{4}=\varepsilon\nu/k^{2}=1/\mathrm{Re}_L\)):

\[ \frac{\Delta t}{\tau_\eta}=\frac1{20}\,\frac{\Delta x}\eta\,\frac{u_\eta}{k^{1/2}}=\frac{\pi}{30}\,\mathrm{Re}_L^{-1/4}. \tag{17.17} \]

For the running case, \(\mathrm{Re}_L=3.08\times10^{4}\) gives \(\Delta t/\tau_\eta=0.0079\): the time step is less than 1 % of the Kolmogorov time, and the ratio decreases as the Reynolds number increases. Yeung, Sreenivasan and Pope (2018, p. 064603-4) report the same order, "order 1% or less" at high \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\), and give the reason that the relevant small time scales are \(\eta/u'\), the time for the large scales to sweep a Kolmogorov-sized structure past a point, and \(\Delta x/u'\), not \(\tau_\eta\). The time step is limited by the sweeping of the small scales by the large ones, which is why \(M\) is proportional to \(L/\Delta x\) and not to \(4\tau/\tau_\eta\).

The Courant number of a production code. Yeung, Sreenivasan and Pope (2018, p. 064603-3, eq. (4)) define the Courant number as \(\Delta t\,(|u|/\Delta x+|v|/\Delta y+|w|/\Delta z)\) maximized over the grid, which they write as approximately \(b\,u'\Delta t/\Delta x\) with \(b\approx12\) in their simulations (they write \(\beta\); this chapter writes \(b\) because \(\beta\) is the constant of (17.2)). The criterion (17.15), with \(k^{1/2}=(3/2)^{1/2}u'\), is \(u'\Delta t/\Delta x=0.0408\), or a Courant number of \(12\times0.0408=0.49\) in their definition. Their tests use 0.6, 0.3 and 0.15 (Table I, p. 064603-5). At \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\approx390\), the peak values of the dissipation and the enstrophy over the grid are considerably higher at 0.6 than at 0.3 and 0.15, which agree very closely, except at the highest spatial resolution (\(\kappa_{\max}\eta\approx5.6\)), and they conclude that the peaks at 0.6 are overpredicted (pp. 064603-8, -9). Because the residual aliasing error of Rogallo's scheme varies as \(\Delta t^{2}\), reducing the Courant number from 0.6 to 0.15 reduces it by a factor of 16 and brings the solution close to the alias-free one (p. 064603-11). The lecture's criterion, 0.49 in their definition, lies between the value they find inadequate for extreme events and the values they find adequate; reducing it to 0.15 multiplies \(M\) by 3.3. The value \(\tfrac1{20}\) is therefore a statement about adequate accuracy for low-order statistics, as \(\kappa_{\max}\eta=1.5\) is.

Where the modes lie: the dissipation range L48 3:38

Lecture 48 shows a diagram of wavenumber space, drawn to scale, and explains it on the next slide. The represented modes lie in a cube of side \(2\kappa_{\max}\), because each component runs from \(-\kappa_{\max}\) to \(\kappa_{\max}\). In a well-resolved simulation only the modes inside the sphere of radius \(\kappa_{\max}\) are dynamically significant; the slide labels the corners of the cube outside the sphere "insignificant modes". The dissipation range is the spherical shell \(\kappa_{DI}\lt|\boldsymbol\kappa|\lt\kappa_{\max}\). Inside the sphere of radius \(\kappa_{DI}\) lie the energy-containing and inertial-range motions, and a still smaller sphere of radius \(\kappa_E\) corresponds to the peak of the energy spectrum of isotropic turbulence at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=70\); at higher Reynolds number this sphere is smaller. The slide's conclusion is that 99.98 % of the represented modes have wavenumbers \(|\boldsymbol\kappa|\) greater than \(\kappa_{DI}\), and fewer than 0.02 % represent motions in the energy-containing range or the inertial subrange.

The share is independent of the Reynolds number. The fraction of the cube inside the sphere of radius \(\kappa_{DI}\) is a ratio of volumes:

\[ \frac{\tfrac43\pi\kappa_{DI}^{3}}{(2\kappa_{\max})^{3}}=\frac\pi6\Bigl(\frac{\kappa_{DI}}{\kappa_{\max}}\Bigr)^{3}=\frac\pi6\Bigl(\frac{2\pi/60}{1.5}\Bigr)^{3}=1.78\times10^{-4}. \tag{17.20} \]

Here \(\kappa_{DI}=2\pi/\ell_{DI}\) with the nominal \(\ell_{DI}=60\eta\) (chapter 11, (11.1) and its section on the objects of the chapter), so \(\kappa_{DI}\eta=2\pi/60\), and \(\kappa_{\max}\eta=1.5\) from (17.12). The program counts the lattice points of an \(N=256\) cube inside the sphere and finds \(1.76\times10^{-4}\). The slide's "less than 0.02 %" is correct. The result (17.20) contains no Reynolds number: both radii are fixed multiples of \(1/\eta\). The slide's remark that the inner sphere shrinks at higher Reynolds number applies to the energy-containing sphere \(\kappa_E\), whose share the program finds to be \(7.1\times10^{-7}\) at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=70\) and which falls as \(\mathrm{Re}_L^{-9/4}\); it does not apply to the inertial-range sphere of radius \(\kappa_{DI}\). Two conventions decide the number: the factor 60 in \(\ell_{DI}\) and the factor \(2\pi\) in \(\kappa=2\pi/\ell\). Of the \(N^{3}\) represented modes, \(1-\pi/6=47.6\) % lie in the corners outside the sphere of radius \(\kappa_{\max}\) and 52.4 % in the dissipation-range shell.

What the modes in the dissipation range carry. The lecture concludes that 99.98 % of the cost is associated with resolving the dissipation range, and asks whether that is worth it, given that these scales "only account for very little of the energy". The first part follows from (17.20), because a pseudo-spectral step costs the same per mode. The second part is true of the energy and false of the dissipation. Exercise 6 computes, from the model spectrum at the running case, that the wavenumbers above \(\kappa_{DI}\) hold 3.6 % of \(k\) and 89 % of \(\varepsilon\); chapter 11 reached the complementary result that an inertial range between the nominal limits holds about 9 % of the dissipation of the jet. A simulation that omits these modes must model the conversion of kinetic energy to heat. The slide on the following page states the motivation correctly (L48 18:37): the vast majority of modes is in the dissipative range, which is a strong motivation for approaches that reduce the resolution requirements in the dissipation range, "especially if energy-containing motions are of primary interest". In large-eddy simulation only the energy-containing motions are resolved and the effects of the unresolved modes are modelled; that approach is the subject of the chapters "Large-eddy simulation: conceptual steps and filtering" and "Filtered equations, the Smagorinsky model, and LES in wavenumber space". The lecture's statement that these approaches give the means and variances "for a fraction of the cost", with some error, is the speaker's appraisal.

Pope (2004, p. 5) describes a different response to the same count: sparse-mode methods, which represent only a fraction of the Fourier modes in each wavenumber shell so that the number of modes increases only as \(\ln\mathrm{Re}\), and which meet the obstacle of the interactions across the continuous range of length scales.

What the homogeneous estimate says about other flows L47 20:27

The lecture lists what made the estimate possible: a periodic box with equal numbers of points in each direction, so that the number of modes is \(N^{3}\); a single ratio of the largest to the smallest length, available because the turbulence is homogeneous; and the ratio \(L_{11}/L\) from a model spectrum. All of these change in an inhomogeneous flow, and the lecture states that the cost there is "definitely going to be higher". Lecture 48 adds the example of a wall-bounded flow, in which the Kolmogorov length near the wall differs from that away from it, so that the smallest scale is not a single number.

The direction of the inequality is plausible but is not a theorem, and it depends on what is compared. A channel flow at a given Reynolds number does not have the same \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) everywhere, and a code for it cannot use Fourier modes in the wall-normal direction; its grid is set by the wall region, where Pope (2004, p. 4), quoting Bradshaw, notes that "in the viscous near-wall region there are no large eddies": the structures that transfer momentum to the wall scale with the viscous length, and resolving them gives "computational requirements that increase as a power of Reynolds number, as in DNS". The homogeneous estimate (17.18) is a lower bound for flows whose smallest scales are as small as \(\eta\) of the homogeneous case and whose domain is at least as large, both in units of the same \(\eta\). For any other comparison the inequality has to be re-derived for that flow.

# Part 1. Model spectrum (17.2)-(17.3) in units k = eps = L = 1.
C, P0, BETA = 1.5, 2.0, 5.2


def spectrum(cL, ceta, eta, x):
    fL = (x/np.sqrt(x**2 + cL))**(5/3 + P0)
    feta = np.exp(-BETA*(((x*eta)**4 + ceta**4)**0.25 - ceta))
    return C*x**(-5/3)*fL*feta


def bisect(f, lo, hi, n=40):
    """Root of an increasing function f on [lo, hi]."""
    for _ in range(n):
        mid = 0.5*(lo + hi)
        lo, hi = (mid, hi) if f(mid) < 0 else (lo, mid)
    return 0.5*(lo + hi)


def model(eta):
    """Constants c_L, c_eta with int E = k and int 2 nu kappa^2 E = eps."""
    if not 0 < eta < 0.2:
        raise ValueError("eta/L must lie in (0, 0.2)")
    x = np.logspace(-6, np.log10(60/eta), 8000)
    nu = eta**(4/3)

    def diss(ceta, cL):
        return np.trapezoid(2*nu*x**2*spectrum(cL, ceta, eta, x), x) - 1

    def ceta_of(cL):
        return bisect(lambda c: diss(c, cL), 0.0, 3.0)

    def energy_deficit(logcL):    # increasing in c_L
        cL = np.exp(logcL)
        return 1 - np.trapezoid(spectrum(cL, ceta_of(cL), eta, x), x)

    cL = np.exp(bisect(energy_deficit, np.log(1e-3), np.log(1e3)))
    ceta = ceta_of(cL)
    E = spectrum(cL, ceta, eta, x)
    L11 = 0.75*np.pi*np.trapezoid(E/x, x)        # (17.3) with k = 1
    return x, E, cL, ceta, L11

    M = 120/np.pi/eta                             # (17.16)
    return ReL, N, M, N**3*M, 1e3*N**3*M/(1e9*86400), (x, E, L11, eta)

What the estimates establish and what they do not

What they establish. Under four stated choices (box \(8L_{11}\), \(\kappa_{\max}\eta=1.5\), Courant criterion \(\tfrac1{20}\), duration \(4k/\varepsilon\)) and the model spectrum, the number of modes, the number of steps and the work of a DNS of isotropic turbulence are definite functions of \(\mathrm{Re}_L\): \(N^{3}\approx4.4\,\mathrm{Re}_L^{9/4}\), \(M\approx38\,\mathrm{Re}_L^{3/4}\), \(N^{3}M\approx169\,\mathrm{Re}_L^{3}\). The exponents follow from the identity \(L/\eta=\mathrm{Re}_L^{3/4}\) and the Courant limit \(\Delta t\propto\Delta x\); the model spectrum enters only the prefactor, through \(L_{11}/L\). The pseudo-spectral step costs \(O(N^{3}\log N)\) operations, and its aliasing error can be removed exactly by truncation (17.9), or by the sphere (17.10) with phase shifting.

What they do not establish. First, the criteria are choices with stated consequences, not requirements derived from the equations: \(\kappa_{\max}\eta=1.5\) leaves 0.22 % of the model's dissipation unresolved and is adequate for low-order statistics only (Yeung, Sreenivasan and Pope 2018, p. 064603-3); the Courant criterion corresponds to 0.49 in their definition, between values they find adequate and inadequate for extreme events; the box criterion rests on a spectrum of unbounded turbulence. Second, the prefactor is uncertain by a factor of about 3 in \(N\) when compared with published grids, which is 81 in work, and the comparison cannot locate the factor. Third, the conversion to time assumes a constant number of operations per mode per step, which grows as \(\log N\), and a sustained computing rate that this chapter has no source for. Fourth, the truncation rule, which (17.13) omits, changes the work by 1.27 (sphere) to 5.06 (cube). Fifth, the estimate is for homogeneous isotropic turbulence; its relation to an inhomogeneous flow is the inequality of the previous section, with the conditions stated there.

The reader can now (i) state the four sources of disagreement between a calculation and a measurement and relate the order of a method to the exponent of its cost (17.4); (ii) write the lattice of a periodic DNS, its largest wavenumber and its equivalent grid spacing; (iii) explain why a pseudo-spectral step costs \(O(N^{3}\log N)\) operations, where the aliasing error comes from (17.8), and remove it by truncation or phase shifting; (iv) derive \(N\), \(M\), \(N^{3}M\), \(T_G\) and the memory from the box, resolution, time-step and duration criteria, with every prefactor; (v) recompute them for a stated truncation rule and Reynolds number, and find a misprint in a published table by its internal consistency; and (vi) say what share of the modes, the energy and the dissipation lies in the dissipation range, and what that implies for large-eddy simulation.

Exercise 2 (derive). (a) For a one-dimensional transform of length \(N\), show that a padded transform of length \(N_p\) evaluates the product of two fields with \(|n|,|m|\le\tfrac12N\) without aliasing onto the retained wavenumbers if \(N_p\gt\tfrac32N-1\). (b) What fraction of the \(N^{3}\) coefficients does the two-thirds rule keep in three dimensions, and what fraction does the sphere (17.10) keep? (c) Why does the two-grid shift alone fail in three dimensions?

Solution

(a) The extreme product of two retained modes is \(n=m=\tfrac12N\), giving \(N\). On a grid of length \(N_p\) it is aliased to \(N-N_p\). It must not land in the retained range \(|k|\le\tfrac12N\) (more precisely the range \(1-\tfrac12N\le k\le\tfrac12N\)): \(N-N_p\lt1-\tfrac12N\), so \(N_p\gt\tfrac32N-1\). This is Rogallo's condition \(\tfrac32M-1\lt L\) in his letters \(M\) and \(L\), here \(N\) and \(N_p\) (1981, p. 47). After the padded product is transformed, the modes outside the original range are discarded. (b) The cube \(|n_i|\lt N/3\) keeps \((2/3)^{3}=0.296\) of the coefficients. The sphere keeps \(\tfrac43\pi(\sqrt2N/3)^{3}/N^{3}=\tfrac43\pi\cdot2\sqrt2/27=0.439\). On \(16^{3}\) the program counts 1331 (0.325, the cube \(|n_i|\le5\)) and 1791 (0.437). (c) A shift of half a cell in every direction multiplies an alias in \(d\) directions by \((-1)^{d}\). Double aliases, \(d=2\), are unchanged, so the average of two grids keeps them; the program's first aliasing line shows an error of \(1.5\times10^{2}\) after the shift. Truncation to the sphere is what makes double and triple aliases impossible (section "Aliasing").

Exercise 3 (check an assumption). Chapter 5 wrote \(\eta/\ell\sim\mathrm{Re}_\ell^{-3/4}\) with \(\mathrm{Re}_\ell=u\ell/\nu\), while this chapter writes \(L/\eta=\mathrm{Re}_L^{3/4}\) with an equals sign. (a) Show that the second is an identity, and state which definitions it needs. (b) Write \(\eta/\ell\) exactly in terms of \(\mathrm{Re}_\ell\) and \(C_\varepsilon\) when \(\varepsilon=C_\varepsilon u^{3}/\ell\), and say which statement is a hypothesis. (c) Which assumption does the relation \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=(20\mathrm{Re}_L/3)^{1/2}\) need that \(L/\eta=\mathrm{Re}_L^{3/4}\) does not?

Solution

(a) With \(L=k^{3/2}/\varepsilon\), \(\eta=\nu^{3/4}\varepsilon^{-1/4}\) and \(\mathrm{Re}_L=k^{2}/(\varepsilon\nu)\): \(L/\eta=k^{3/2}\varepsilon^{-3/4}\nu^{-3/4}=(k^{2}/(\varepsilon\nu))^{3/4}\). It needs only the three definitions; no statistical assumption. (b) \(\eta/\ell=(\nu^{3}/\varepsilon)^{1/4}/\ell=(\nu^{3}\ell/(C_\varepsilon u^{3}))^{1/4}/\ell=C_\varepsilon^{-1/4}\mathrm{Re}_\ell^{-3/4}\). The hypothesis is that \(C_\varepsilon\) tends to a constant at high Reynolds number (chapter 5 cites McComb and co-authors for 0.468); the large-scale length \(L\) of this chapter absorbs that question into its definition, because it is built from \(\varepsilon\) itself. (c) \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) uses \(\lambda_g\) and the isotropic formula \(\varepsilon=15\nu u'^{2}/\lambda_g^{2}\) (11.24); the relation holds for isotropic turbulence only. Exercise 4 (counterexample). A student claims that "a DNS with \(\kappa_{\max}\eta=1.5\) resolves every statistic of the small scales, because 99.8 % of the dissipation lies below \(\kappa\eta=1.5\)". Give a counterexample from the literature cited in this chapter, and compute the factor by which the work grows when \(\kappa_{\max}\eta\) is raised from 1.5 to 2.8 and to 5.6 at fixed \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) and fixed Courant number.

Solution

The statement about the mean dissipation is true for the model spectrum (0.22 % above \(\kappa\eta=1.5\) at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=800\)). The conclusion does not follow, because the mean is a low-order statistic. Yeung, Sreenivasan and Pope (2018, pp. 064603-3, -5) state that \(\kappa_{\max}\eta\) of order 1.5 may be adequate for low-order statistics only. When they remove the highest wavenumbers of a field computed at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\approx650\) with \(\kappa_{\max}\eta\approx2.8\), the tails of the probability density functions of the dissipation and the enstrophy become systematically narrower, and they conclude that the contributions to the wide tails reside mostly in wavenumbers close to \(\kappa_{\max}\) (p. 064603-5, Figure 1); at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\approx390\) and Courant numbers 0.3 and 0.15, the peak values of the dissipation over the grid increase with \(\kappa_{\max}\eta\) and still differ between 2.8 and 5.6 (p. 064603-9). At fixed box and Reynolds number, \(N\propto\kappa_{\max}\eta\), and at fixed Courant number \(M\propto N\), so the work scales as \((\kappa_{\max}\eta)^{4}\): \((2.8/1.5)^{4}=12.1\) and \((5.6/1.5)^{4}=194\).

Exercise 5 (compute). Apply the lecture's recipe to the atmospheric boundary layer of chapter 5: \(u=0.3\ \mathrm{m/s}\), \(\ell=10^{3}\ \mathrm m\), \(\nu=1.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), with \(k=\tfrac32u^{2}\) and \(\varepsilon=u^{3}/\ell\), treated as homogeneous isotropic turbulence. Compute \(\mathrm{Re}_L\), \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\), \(N\), \(N^{3}\), \(M\) and \(T_G\) with the asymptote \(L_{11}/L=0.43\), and compare \(N^{3}\) with chapter 5's estimate of \(3.5\times10^{15}\) points.

Solution

\(k=0.135\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\), \(\varepsilon=2.7\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\), \(\mathrm{Re}_L=k^{2}/(\varepsilon\nu)=0.018225/(4.05\times10^{-10})=4.50\times10^{7}\), which is \(2.25\,\mathrm{Re}_\ell\) with \(\mathrm{Re}_\ell=2\times10^{7}\); \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=(20/3\times4.5\times10^{7})^{1/2}=1.73\times10^{4}\). \(L=k^{3/2}/\varepsilon=1837\ \mathrm m\), \(\eta=3.34\ \mathrm{mm}\), \(L/\eta=\mathrm{Re}_L^{3/4}=5.5\times10^{5}\). \(N=1.64\times5.5\times10^{5}=9.0\times10^{5}\), \(N^{3}=7.4\times10^{17}\), \(M=38.2\times5.5\times10^{5}=2.1\times10^{7}\), \(N^{3}M=1.5\times10^{25}\), and \(T_G=1.8\times10^{14}\) days, \(4.9\times10^{11}\) years. At a sustained \(10^{18}\) operations per second the time would be \(1.8\times10^{5}\) days, about 490 years. Chapter 5's \(3.5\times10^{15}\) used a cube of side \(\ell=10^{3}\ \mathrm m\) and \(\Delta x=1.97\eta\); the recipe here uses a box of \(8L_{11}=8\times0.43\times1837=6.3\times10^{3}\ \mathrm m\) and \(\Delta x=2.09\eta\). The ratio of the volumes in grid cells is \((6.3)^{3}\times(1.97/2.09)^{3}=210\), and \(3.5\times10^{15}\times210=7.4\times10^{17}\): the two estimates agree once their boxes and spacings are stated. Neither describes the atmospheric layer, whose ground, rotation and stratification the periodic box omits.

Exercise 6 (judge a claim). "In a DNS, 99.98 % of the modes are in the dissipation range, and these scales carry very little of the energy, so 99.98 % of the computation is spent on scales that do not matter." Using the model spectrum at the running case (\(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=453\)), compute the shares of the energy and of the dissipation above \(\kappa_{DI}\) and judge the claim.

Solution

A short addition to the program (the integrals of \(E\) and of \(2\nu\kappa^{2}E\) above \(\kappa_{DI}L=2\pi/(60\eta/L)\) with the model at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=453\)) gives 3.6 % of \(k\) and 89 % of \(\varepsilon\) above \(\kappa_{DI}\). The first half of the claim is correct: by (17.20), 99.98 % of the represented modes lie outside the sphere of radius \(\kappa_{DI}\), and since every mode costs the same in a pseudo-spectral step, so does 99.98 % of the work (about 48 % of the modes are corner modes outside \(\kappa_{\max}\), which a code may discard). "Carry very little of the energy" is also correct. "Do not matter" is false: the same modes carry 89 % of the dissipation, the process that removes energy from the flow at the rate the large scales supply it. A simulation without them must replace them by a model of the dissipation, which is what large-eddy simulation does, at the price of the model's error. Whether the work is well spent depends on the statistic sought: for the statistics of the small scales themselves (velocity-gradient moments, the dissipation field) there is no substitute.

Exercise 7 (design). A group has a machine on which its pseudo-spectral code sustains \(10^{15}\) operations per second, and seven days of time. Using (17.18) with 1000 operations per mode per step, find the largest \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda\) it can simulate. Then include the sphere truncation and, separately, a Courant number of 0.15 in the definition of Yeung, Sreenivasan and Pope, and state which of the two choices costs more Reynolds number.

Solution

The budget is \(10^{15}\times7\times86\,400/10^{3}=6.05\times10^{17}\) mode-steps. With \(N^{3}M=0.571\,\mathrm{Re}_\lambda^{6}\), \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=(6.05\times10^{17}/0.571)^{1/6}=1010\). The sphere truncation multiplies the work by 1.27, which gives \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=1010/1.27^{1/6}=970\). Courant number 0.15 instead of 0.49 multiplies \(M\) by 3.27, and with the sphere the work by \(1.27\times3.27=4.15\): \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=1010/4.15^{1/6}=796\). Because the work grows as \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda^{6}\), a factor \(F\) in work costs a factor \(F^{1/6}\) in Reynolds number: the truncation costs 4 %, the smaller Courant number 18 %. The asymptote 0.43 for \(L_{11}/L\) is adequate at these Reynolds numbers (0.444 at 800 in Table 17.1: the work is \((0.444/0.43)^{3}=1.10\) times larger, which lowers the Reynolds number by the factor \(1.10^{1/6}=1.016\)).
