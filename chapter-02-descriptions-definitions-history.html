Descriptions, definitions, and a short history of turbulence

A glass tube about 12.7 mm in diameter (Reynolds's middle tube was nearly half an inch) carries water, and a thread of dye enters at its centre. At one flow speed the thread runs straight for the whole tube; at a higher speed it breaks up at a point downstream and fills the tube with coloured water. Reynolds described exactly this in 1883, and his own numbers contain a second observation that the usual summary omits: the speed at which a smooth flow first breaks down was 6.35 times the speed below which a disturbed flow settles back into order. Two questions follow. What single quantity decides which behaviour a given tube, fluid and speed will show? And what, after more than a century of definitions, is the flow in the second picture called, and how much of that name can be tested? This chapter derives the quantity from Reynolds's own argument, converts his constants into modern Reynolds numbers (about \(2.0\times10^{3}\) and \(1.3\times10^{4}\), not the often quoted 2300), examines the lecture's four attempts at a definition and its list of attributes with a measurable version of one of them, and checks each historical attribution against a source that has been opened.

Grounded in three recordings of the NPTEL course Essentials of Turbulence (Prof. Abhilash J. Chandy, IIT Bombay): Lecture 3, Lecture 4 and Lecture 5. The "Sources" slide of this module was shown in Lecture 1 and is not part of these three recordings (chapter 1 records that it names lecture notes by McDonough, 2007, and by Bakker, 2008, Reynolds, 1883, and Mellado and Ansorge, 2012); this chapter therefore attributes every slide sentence to the lecture. Lecture material: the nonlinearity of the Navier–Stokes equations and the few exact solutions; Reynolds's dye experiment and the Reynolds number as a ratio of inertia to viscous forces; convective transport as "enhanced diffusion"; the quotations of Richardson, von Kármán (quoting Taylor) and Hinze; the "modern definition" of turbulence and its three required elements; six attributes of turbulence; a timeline from da Vinci to large-eddy simulation; the three "movements" of turbulence research. Research extensions: Reynolds (1883) read on the page; Chapman and Tobak (1985); Schmitt (2007) on Boussinesq; Darrigol (2002); Richardson (1922); Kolmogorov (1962); Corrsin and Kistler (1955); Roshko (1954); Boffetta and Ecke (2012). Original teaching constructions: the derivation of the Reynolds number from the ratio of two terms; the conversion of Reynolds's constants and its program; the plane-channel check that the nonlinear term vanishes; the synthetic-record intermittency test; the grading of each lecture claim; the exercises.

The objects of the chapter and the running case

The running case is Reynolds's colour-band experiment: water drawn through a straight glass tube from a large tank, with a streak of strongly coloured water entering with it. Reynolds used three glass tubes about 4 ft 6 in long with trumpet-shaped mouthpieces, of diameters near 1 inch, 1/2 inch and 1/4 inch, in water between 5 °C and 22 °C (Reynolds 1883, pp. 941–943). This chapter computes with a tube of \(D=12.7\ \mathrm{mm}\); the diameter is an illustrative choice, because the Reynolds numbers that result do not depend on it.

The formal model. The flow of water at constant density \(\rho\) and constant viscosity \(\mu\) (kinematic viscosity \(\nu=\mu/\rho\)) obeys the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations, written for the full velocity \(\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)\) and the pressure \(p\) with the slide's body force \(\mathbf F_B\) per unit mass:

\[ \nabla\cdot\mathbf U=0,\qquad \frac{\partial\mathbf U}{\partial t}+(\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U=-\frac1\rho\nabla p+\nu\nabla^{2}\mathbf U+\mathbf F_B. \tag{2.1} \]

Chapter 1 (section "The Navier–Stokes equations and their assumptions") states the continuum and incompressibility assumptions behind (2.1) and checks them for an air jet; for water at 5–22 °C in a tube of millimetre size the same checks hold with a wide margin, and chapter 6 derives (2.1). The lecture writes the slide pressure as \(P\) with the transient term \(\mathbf U_t\); this chapter writes \(p/\rho\) and \(\partial\mathbf U/\partial t\), as chapter 1 does. A tube of diameter \(D\) with bulk (mean) speed \(U\) has Reynolds number

\[ \mathrm{Re}_D=\frac{UD}{\nu}=\frac{\rho UD}{\mu}. \tag{2.2} \]

The lecture writes \(\mathrm{Re}=\rho UL/\mu\) with a characteristic length \(L\); (2.2) is that quantity with \(L=D\) (chapter 4, section "Turbulence begins as an instability of a laminar flow", writes \(\mathrm{Re}_D\) in the same way). A critical Reynolds number belongs to one flow, one choice of \(L\), and one level of disturbance.

The nonlinear term and the few exact solutions L3 1:02

The lecture opens by naming the term that makes (2.1) hard to solve, \((\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U\), which is quadratic in the unknown: a sum of two solutions of (2.1) is not a solution. The slide says that there are few exact solutions, that all of them were obtained at the expense of simplifying, often physically unrealistic, assumptions, and that little progress in understanding turbulence can therefore be made from analytical solutions; early descriptions of turbulence, it adds, were based mainly on experimental observation. The lecture's two examples are laminar flow between two parallel plates, with both plates at rest or with the upper plate moving (Couette flow).

The lecture says that in these examples the nonlinear term "can be neglected". The statement can be made exact. Take plates at \(y=\pm h\), a flow in the \(x\) direction with \(\mathbf U=(u(y),0,0)\) that does not change with \(x\) or \(t\), and a constant pressure gradient \(-G=\partial p/\partial x\), with \(G\gt0\). The convective term is then \(u\,\partial u/\partial x=0\), exactly and not approximately, so (2.1) reduces to \(\mu\,u''=-G\), and with \(u(\pm h)=0\)

\[ u(y)=\frac{G}{2\mu}\,(h^{2}-y^{2}),\qquad U=\frac1{2h}\int_{-h}^{h}u\,dy=\frac{Gh^{2}}{3\mu}. \tag{2.3} \]

The profile (2.3) satisfies (2.1) at every Reynolds number \(Uh/\nu\). The nonlinear term does not vanish because the Reynolds number is small, but because the assumed form of the flow has no streamwise variation. For Couette flow with the upper plate at \(y=h\) moving at speed \(U_w\) and no pressure gradient, \(u=U_wy/h\) with \(y\) measured from the lower plate; again \(u\,\partial u/\partial x=0\). Whether (2.3) is the flow that occurs is a different question, the stability of the solution, which chapter 4 treats (section "Turbulence begins as an instability of a laminar flow").

Reynolds's tube is round, and its laminar solution is the analogue of (2.3). For a tube of radius \(a\) with axial pressure gradient \(-G\) and no slip at \(r=a\), the equation \(\mu\,(u''+u'/r)=-G\) gives

\[ u(r)=\frac{G}{4\mu}\,(a^{2}-r^{2}),\qquad U=\frac{2}{a^{2}}\int_0^a u\,r\,dr=\frac{Ga^{2}}{8\mu}=\frac{GD^{2}}{32\mu}, \]

the flow of Hagen and Poiseuille. It also has \(\mathbf U\cdot\nabla\mathbf U=\mathbf 0\), and it exists at every Reynolds number. Reynolds's experiment shows the range of \(\mathrm{Re}\) in which it is observed.

The lecture draws two conclusions that are judgments rather than results: that analytical solutions of turbulent flow are impossible, and that the lack of analytical solutions is the reason for the little progress made. The first is not proved by anything in the lecture. Chapter 1 (section "What is unsolved") separates what is open: whether three-dimensional solutions of (2.1) stay smooth, the prediction of an individual realization, and the computation of statistics.

Reynolds's colour bands and his two critical velocities L3 4:22

The lecture describes Reynolds's apparatus as a pipe with smooth transparent walls and a dye streak, shows a drawing of him beside the apparatus and three drawings of the streak, and says that as the flow rate increases the streak first runs undisturbed with sharp edges, then fluctuates and begins to mix, and finally mixes completely; the fluid, the dye and the tube are the same, so only the velocity changes. The slide's handwritten annotation labels the three drawings laminar, transitional and turbulent and writes \(\mathrm{Re}<2300\), \(2300<\mathrm{Re}<10000\) and \(\mathrm{Re}>10000\) beside them. Reynolds's own account of the three states is in his section 11 (1883, p. 942):

  1. At low velocity the colour streak "extended in a beautiful straight line through the tube". If the tank water was not quite at rest the streak shifted about the tube, "but there was no appearance of sinuosity".
  2. As the velocity was raised in small steps, at some point in the tube, always a considerable distance from the mouthpiece, "the colour band would all at once mix up with the surrounding water", filling the rest of the tube with coloured water. A further increase moved this point toward the mouthpiece, but with no velocity tried did it reach it.
  3. Viewed by the light of an electric spark, the mass of colour "resolved itself into a mass of more or less distinct curls, showing eddies".

The slide's three tube drawings are Reynolds's figures 3, 4 and 5 (p. 942). Figures 4 and 5 show the same break-down region, the second lit by an electric spark, so the lecture's separate labels "transition" and "turbulent, completely mixed" apply to one observed state. Reynolds does not describe a state between the first and second; the lecture's middle category, "transitional", describes the position of the break-down point along the tube, which moves with velocity. The fluctuating, partly mixed streak that the lecture describes is Reynolds's break-down region seen before the point reaches the mouthpiece.

Reynolds's own critical values. Two sets of experiments give two critical velocities. The colour-band tests used three glass tubes (Nos. 1 to 3, p. 941). The resistance tests used two lead pipes (Nos. 4 and 5, of a quarter and a half inch in diameter, p. 946). The two velocities are different quantities. The first, \(v_s\), is the velocity at which a flow that entered quietly first broke down into eddies; the tank was "as still as could by any means be brought about" (p. 942), and the break-down was seen as mixing of the colour band. The second, \(v_c\), is the velocity below which eddies that are present in the entering water die out along the pipe, found from the departure of the resistance from Poiseuille's law (pp. 946, 948). Both are inversely proportional to the diameter and, through the viscosity of water, depend on temperature (pp. 943, 946). In his notation, with \(P(T)=(1+0.0336\,T+0.000221\,T^{2})^{-1}\) and \(T\) in degrees centigrade, a metre and a second as units, and \(D\) the diameter of the tube,

\[ v_s=\frac{1}{B_s}\frac{P}{D},\quad B_s=43.79\ \ (\text{p. 943});\qquad v_c=\frac{1}{B_c}\frac{P}{D},\quad B_c=278\ \ (\text{p. 948}). \tag{2.4} \]

The second law came from experiments on the resistance of long pipes (a lead pipe of diameter 0.00615 m and one of 0.0127 m) with water that was "in a high state of disturbance" at entry (pp. 946–948). It is the velocity below which the disturbance dies out along the pipe and the resistance follows Poiseuille's law: the velocities "at which a deviation from the law first occurred were in exact inverse ratio of the diameters of the pipes" (p. 946). The constant \(B_c\) is 279.7 in pipe 4 and 272.0 in pipe 5 (p. 971); Reynolds's 278 is his summary value, and the two pipes differ by 2.8 percent. Reynolds remarks that this value "is much less than the critical velocity at which steady motion broke down; the ratio being 43.7 to 278" (p. 948); 43.7 is Reynolds's rounding of 43.79, and the ratio is 6.36 with 43.7 and 6.35 with 43.79. The two numbers say that steady motion in undisturbed water, in glass tubes, persisted to \(278/43.79=6.35\) times the velocity below which a disturbed flow in lead pipes becomes orderly. They are two different thresholds in two pipe materials, and not one threshold measured at two disturbance levels. His comment on the first value is that "the critical velocity was very sensitive to disturbance in the water before entering the tubes" and that "the steady motion was unstable for large disturbances long before the critical velocity was reached" (p. 943). Chapter 4 (section "Turbulence begins as an instability of a laminar flow") and its comparison with the linearized pipe-flow computations of Meseguer and Trefethen (2003) develop the dependence on disturbance; here the numbers are converted.

Conversion. Reynolds's resistance law is Poiseuille's, for which \(G=\rho g\,i\) when \(i\) is the head loss per unit length, so that \(U=GD^{2}/(32\mu)\) gives \(i=32\,\nu U/(gD^{2})\), with \(U\) written \(v\) as Reynolds does (Reynolds's \(i\) is the resistance per unit length measured in weight of water; this identification and \(g=9.81\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\) are assumptions of the conversion). At the critical velocity \(v_c\) of (2.4) he gives \(i_c=P^{2}/(A_cD^{3})\) with \(A_c=4.77\times10^{7}\) (pp. 948, 972). Equating the two expressions for \(i_c\) gives the kinematic viscosity of water at 0 °C, where \(P=1\):

\[ \nu_0=\frac{B_c\,g}{32\,A_c}=1.787\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2/s},\qquad \mathrm{Re}_c=\frac{v_cD}{\nu_0P}=\frac{1}{B_c\nu_0}=2.01\times10^{3},\qquad \mathrm{Re}_s=\frac{1}{B_s\nu_0}=1.28\times10^{4}. \tag{2.5} \]

The factor \(P\) cancels because Reynolds took the temperature dependence of the critical velocity from the temperature dependence of the viscosity of water; the diameter cancels because \(v\propto1/D\). The two laws (2.4) therefore state that the pipe flow is orderly below a Reynolds number of about 2000 for the entry disturbances of his experiments, and that in undisturbed water it stays orderly up to about 12 800. The value \(\nu_0\) can be compared with a table: the CRC Handbook gives the dynamic viscosity of water at 0 °C as 1.7921 mPa s and the density as 0.99984 g/cm\(^3\) (Lide 2004, pp. 6-201 and 6-15 respectively, as cited by the Wikipedia data page for water; the handbook itself was not opened), so \(\nu=1.792\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), and the value in (2.5) is 0.3 percent lower. This is an independent test of the two assumptions of the conversion. The program in section "Executable checks" evaluates (2.5) at four temperatures.

Comparison with the lecture. The values 2300 and 10 000 on the slide are the lecturer's handwritten boundaries, "experimental observations have shown that to us" in the lecture's words; neither number occurs in Reynolds's paper, whose critical constants give about \(2.0\times10^{3}\) and \(1.3\times10^{4}\). The lower of Reynolds's values is of the same size as the transition value of about 2000 that Meseguer and Trefethen place for practical pipe flow (chapter 4, same section). The lecture's three-way division is a convention for pipes under ordinary laboratory disturbance, not a property of water: the flow in Reynolds's tube was laminar at Reynolds numbers above 2300 whenever the entry was quiet.

One parameter from two terms L3 5:43

The lecture says that Reynolds's observations "led to identification of a single dimensionless parameter" and that this parameter is the ratio of inertia to viscous forces. Reynolds's paper derives this from the equations of motion without integrating them (p. 938, section 6). Suppose the motion depends on one velocity \(U\), the mean velocity along the tube, and one length \(c\), the radius. After the pressure is eliminated, Reynolds says, the accelerations are of two types, one with the factor \(U^{2}/c\) and the other with the factor \(\mu U/(\rho c^{2})\) (the viscous term), so that "the relative values of these terms vary respectively as \(U\) and \(\mu/(\rho c)\)". The same scaling in present notation: the convective term \((\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U\) is of order \(U^{2}/c\) and the viscous term \(\nu\nabla^{2}\mathbf U\) of order \(\nu U/c^{2}\), so

\[ \frac{|(\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U|}{|\nu\nabla^{2}\mathbf U|}\sim\frac{U^{2}/c}{\nu U/c^{2}}=\frac{Uc}{\nu}. \tag{2.6} \]

The ratio (2.6) is Reynolds's \(c\rho U/\mu\) with the radius as length; it equals \(\tfrac12\mathrm{Re}_D\). Reynolds concludes that "if the eddies were owing to one particular cause", integration would show their birth to depend on a definite value of this ratio. The lecture's statement that the Reynolds number is the only physical parameter in transition therefore holds under Reynolds's own assumptions: one velocity, one length, and a fluid of fixed viscosity. The assumption excludes the disturbance level at the entry. Reynolds found that \(v_s\) was "very sensitive to disturbance in the water before entering the tubes" (p. 943). His two critical velocities differ by the factor 6.35, but the factor compares two different thresholds (break-down of a quiet flow, decay of a large disturbance) measured in glass tubes and in lead pipes. It is not the change of one critical velocity with the disturbance level, and Reynolds did not measure that change. For a tube, "Re alone decides" is true of the existence of orderly motion at low \(\mathrm{Re}\), and false of the value at which a given tube breaks down. In Lecture 5 the lecturer adds that the Reynolds number is the "bifurcation parameter" of transition. That is the vocabulary of the definition in the section "The lecture's "modern definition" and its three elements"; its content is the statement that the character of the flow changes as \(\mathrm{Re}\) passes through values that depend on the disturbance.

Reynolds adds an observation that is not in the lecture: "the more viscous a fluid is, the less prone is it to eddying" (p. 937). In (2.6) a larger \(\nu\) at fixed \(U\) and \(c\) lowers the ratio.

Convective transport and "enhanced diffusion" L3 16:57

The lecture says that in laminar flow the dye is not seen to mix because molecular diffusion is slow, and that in turbulent flow convective transport mixes the dye much faster; the end state (uniform dye) is the same in both. It calls the faster process "enhanced diffusion" and compares it with stirring milk into coffee: slowly, mixing happens by diffusion; vigorously, it is faster. The statement can be written for a conserved passive scalar \(\phi\) (concentration of dye) with molecular diffusivity \(\Gamma\) carried by the velocity field of (2.1):

\[ \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\mathbf U\cdot\nabla\phi=\Gamma\nabla^{2}\phi. \tag{2.7} \]

The transport term \(\mathbf U\cdot\nabla\phi\) is present in every flow, laminar or turbulent. In Reynolds's straight streak the velocity is parallel to the tube axis, so the term carries the dye along the tube without moving it across the stream, and the thread spreads across only by the diffusion term. The term does not by itself mix. Mixing across the tube needs a velocity with a cross-stream component; in Reynolds's tube the break-down at the point in his description supplies one that varies irregularly in space and time. Two statements of the lecture are therefore conditional. "If \(\nu\) is small the advective behaviour dominates" (slide bullet above the jet image) holds at fixed \(U\) and \(c\), through (2.6), and not for small \(\nu\) alone. And "the final outcome is the same" holds for a closed vessel in which the scalar is conserved: in both cases \(\phi\) becomes uniform; what differs is the time, and chapter 4 (section "The ratio of the two times is a Péclet number") computes the ratio of the two times for heat in a room, days against minutes.

The lecture also says that the nonlinear first-derivative term and the linear second-derivative term are "two different terms mathematically", and so have different physical mechanisms. This is correct and is the content of (2.6): one term is quadratic in \(\mathbf U\) and of first order in derivatives; the other is linear and of second order. The lecture adds that in turbulent flow the viscous diffusion "tends to be negligible compared to the convective transport". That holds for the exchange of momentum and scalar between large eddies. The lecture itself states in the next lecture that viscosity acts at the smallest scales to dissipate energy; chapter 5 treats the scale at which the two terms balance.

The lecture shows two numerical pictures: a vertical cross-section of a turbulent boundary layer under the upper troposphere (a simulation at the Jülich Supercomputing Centre with \(5120\times5120\times840\) grid points, that is \(2.2\times10^{10}\) points; copyright Max Planck Institute for Meteorology, Hamburg), which the lecture uses to explain the bumpiness a passenger feels shortly after take-off, and an exhaust jet from a compressible large-eddy simulation. Both are computations, and the second does not resolve every scale (chapter 19, Large-eddy simulation: conceptual steps and filtering). The lecture's statement that the aircraft meets turbulence because convective transport dominates viscous diffusion in the boundary layer concerns the Reynolds number of the atmospheric flow; the lecture gives no value, and this chapter computes none.

The two statistical definitions. The Taylor–von Kármán sentence names one attribute, irregular motion, and two places where it occurs: at solid surfaces and where neighbouring streams of the same fluid flow past one another. The Hinze sentence adds that the irregular quantities vary "randomly" and that averages can nevertheless be defined. The lecture asks whether the motion is really random, and defers the answer. The answer this book gives is in chapter 7 (section "Deterministic equations, incomplete conditions and sensitivity"): the equations are deterministic, the initial and boundary data of a real flow are never known exactly, and a probabilistic description is then the appropriate one. In this sense Hinze's "random" and the lecture's later "seemingly random" are statements about knowledge of the data, not about the equations.

The lecture's criticism. The slide states that none of these definitions offers a precise characterization in the sense of predicting a priori, from specific flow conditions, when turbulence will or will not occur, or what its extent and intensity will be; that this lack of precision has "at least to some extent" contributed to the inability to solve the turbulence problem; and that if one does not know what turbulence is, it is unlikely that one can say much about it quantitatively. The first sentence sets a demand that no definition of a physical regime meets by itself: a definition says what a state is, and a criterion for when it occurs is a separate result (Reynolds's \(B_s\) and \(B_c\) are such criteria, and each depends on the entry disturbance). The second and third sentences are the lecture's judgment. The record of the quantitative work in this book, from Reynolds's constants to the scaling laws of chapters 5 and 10, does not depend on a formal definition.

The lecture's "modern definition" and its three elements L4 11:54

The slide reads: "Chapman and Tobak (1985) have described the evolution of our understanding of turbulence in terms of three overlapping eras: a) statistical, b) structural and c) deterministic. A more precise definition of turbulence is now possible within the context of ideas from the deterministic era. Namely: Turbulence is any chaotic solution to the 3-D Navier–Stokes equations that is sensitive to initial data and which occurs as a result of successive instabilities of laminar flows as a bifurcation parameter is increased through a succession of values. While this definition is still somewhat vague, it contains specific elements that permit detailed examination of flow situations relating to turbulence."

Attribution. The italic sentence is not in the version of Chapman and Tobak that was read for this chapter (NASA TM 86679, May 1985). The words "chaotic solution", "sensitive to initial data", "successive instabilities" and "bifurcation parameter" do not occur in it. The slide itself says only that the definition is possible "within the context of ideas from the deterministic era", and does not say that Chapman and Tobak wrote it. This book therefore writes "the lecture's definition" and does not attribute the sentence to Chapman and Tobak. The paper does state that the problem of turbulence "is so difficult that it has even defied the formulation of a consistent and rigorous definition" (p. 1). The slide shows the sentence in quotation marks and names no source for it. In Lecture 5 (16:15 to 16:20) the lecturer says of Ruelle and Takens (1971) that "the modern definition of turbulence sort of came from their paper". Their paper was not accessible, so whether the sentence is in it was not checked; Chapman and Tobak describe the paper's argument without this sentence (pp. 8, 12). The source of the wording remains unknown. Chapter 1 records that the module's "Sources" slide names lecture notes by McDonough (2007) and by Bakker (2008) among others; those notes were not obtained, and whether the sentence comes from them was not checked.

The three elements, as the lecture states them. (1) It names equations, the Navier–Stokes equations, whose solutions are to be associated with turbulence; the slide adds that it is now well known that they may exhibit turbulent solutions, which earlier definitions did not acknowledge. (2) It requires the behaviour to be chaotic, "erratic, irregular, as required in earlier definitions, but deterministic and not random (because the N–S equations are deterministic)". The slide calls this "in strong contrast to, especially, Hinze's definition, but it is overwhelmingly supported by experimental data". (3) It requires turbulence to be three-dimensional. A fourth requirement, sensitivity to initial data, "allows one to distinguish highly irregular laminar motion (such as arises in a quasiperiodic flow regime) from actual turbulence", and the slide notes that it provides a direct link to modern mathematical theories of the equations.

What each element covers. Chapter 1 (sections "What is unsolved" and "The Navier–Stokes equations and their assumptions") gives the status of the first element: solutions of (2.1) are a model of a fluid inside the continuum hypothesis, and in three dimensions the existence of smooth solutions for all time is an open problem. Chapman and Tobak state the same premise with its reservations: turbulence "can be understood within the framework of the continuum assumption", which implies accepting that the equations are a complete description; they name an experimental fact that may cast doubt on it (small amounts of long-chain polymers in water change turbulent properties although the polymers are smaller than the dissipation scales), and the mathematical gap that existence has not been proved (pp. 1–2). The second element, determinism with sensitivity to the data, is quantified for a model by the growth rate of (1.5) of chapter 1, which for Lorenz's three-variable system is 0.906 in the system's time units (chapter 1, section "What is unsolved"). The sensitive-dependence criterion is a property of a system and of a solution; for a flow in a bounded domain it is stated as in chapter 1 and the lecture gives no value for any turbulent flow.

The definition has three limits that the lecture does not state. First, it gives a route by which turbulence arises from a laminar flow, successive instabilities, and so is silent about flows that are turbulent without having passed through that sequence at that flow: grid turbulence behind a wind-tunnel grid, or the flow in a tube fed by a turbulent inflow, is produced by the inlet and not by an instability of a laminar flow in the observed region. Reynolds's own remark that the steady motion was unstable to large disturbances before the critical velocity (1883, p. 943) describes a second path, a finite disturbance acting on a stable laminar flow, that the clause "as a result of successive instabilities" does not cover. Second, "chaotic" is a property of a model's long-time behaviour: for a flow in a long channel it needs a statement of which quantity, over what time, and with what tolerance is chaotic, and the lecture gives none. Third, the definition contains no threshold. Whether a given tube at a given speed is turbulent is answered by an experiment or a computation, not by the definition, which is the lecturer's own remark about "somewhat vague".

The lecture's phrase for the status of the definition, "Is this complete? No. But this is the definition that is used in the modern era", is a judgment about usage; The slide's claim that the deterministic element is "overwhelmingly supported by experimental data" refers to the observation that the same experiment, repeated, gives different detailed records but reproducible statistics; chapter 7 develops it with the Lorenz system. The lecture does not exhibit the data, and this book does not use the statement as a finding.

Why the definition says three-dimensional L4 17:43

The lecture gives a physical reason for the third element: vortex stretching, the mechanism by which vortices are stretched and large vortices are broken into smaller ones, is absent from the one- and two-dimensional vorticity equations and present only in three dimensions. The argument takes two lines. For incompressible constant-property flow, the vorticity \(\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf U\) obeys \(\partial\boldsymbol\omega/\partial t+(\mathbf U\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol\omega=(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U+\nu\nabla^{2}\boldsymbol\omega\), and the term \((\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U\) is the stretching term. In a two-dimensional flow \(\mathbf U=(U_1,U_2,0)\) that does not depend on \(x_3\), the vorticity is \(\boldsymbol\omega=(0,0,\omega_3)\), and \((\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf U=\omega_3\,\partial\mathbf U/\partial x_3=\mathbf 0\). The slide attributes the classical viewpoint to Tennekes and Lumley (1972), a book that was not available; chapter 6 derives the vorticity equation (section "The vorticity equation and vortex stretching"). The lecture adds that chaotic solutions have been obtained from one- and two-dimensional versions of the equations, "suggesting that chaos is not necessarily turbulence". A source for that statement was not found; two remarks on it can be made. In one dimension the incompressibility condition \(\partial U_1/\partial x_1=0\) forces the velocity to be uniform in \(x_1\), so a one-dimensional incompressible flow has no spatial dynamics; one-dimensional chaotic solutions must come from a different equation (a compressible or Burgers-type model), which is not (2.1). In two dimensions the equation is (2.1), and the review of Boffetta and Ecke (2012) treats these flows as turbulence.

The statement of the difference in that review is that two-dimensional turbulence lacks vortex stretching (p. 430) and conserves, besides energy, the mean square vorticity, which leads to an inverse cascade of energy to larger scales and a direct cascade of mean square vorticity to smaller scales (p. 427); they also state that large-scale atmospheric and oceanic motions are to a first approximation two-dimensional (p. 428). The lecture's third element therefore excludes two-dimensional flows from the word by stipulation, and not because two-dimensional flows are not irregular, mixing and multi-scale. The stipulation separates a family of flows with a direct energy cascade from one with an inverse cascade; it does not say that the second is regular. The book follows the lecture's usage for the rest of the course, "turbulence" meaning three-dimensional turbulence, and states when two-dimensional flows are meant.

Intermittency: the fraction of time a point sees irregular motion L4 23:46

The lecture explains intermittency by two sketches of the velocity at a fixed point. In the first the signal is irregular for a short time and otherwise regular. In the second it is irregular for most of the time. It describes the second as typical of turbulence and the first as more like transitional flow, and says that a flow irregular only for a short time "wouldn't be classified as turbulent". The standard measure is the intermittency factor of Townsend, which Corrsin and Kistler (1955) define as "the fractional time spent by the (fixed) probe in turbulent fluid" (p. 5):

\[ \gamma_{\mathrm i}=\frac{\text{time during which the probe is in turbulent fluid}}{\text{total time}},\qquad 0\le\gamma_{\mathrm i}\le1. \tag{2.8} \]

This chapter writes \(\gamma_{\mathrm i}\). Corrsin and Kistler write \(\gamma\); the lecture gives intermittency no symbol. The symbol \(\gamma\) is also used in the course for the heat diffusivity (chapter 4 writes \(\Gamma\)) and, as \(\dot\gamma\), for the strain rate in Newton's law of viscosity (Lecture 5), so the subscript keeps three meanings apart. Corrsin and Kistler measured \(\gamma_{\mathrm i}\) in a boundary layer and a round jet, and compared a wake of Townsend's with them. Their Figure 32 (p. 25) plots \(\gamma_{\mathrm i}\) against position on a Gaussian probability scale, and all three distributions are, in their words, "Gaussian within the experimental precision except in the tail regions". This is the statement that \(\gamma_{\mathrm i}(y)\) is close to a Gaussian distribution function of the position of the wandering edge: it falls from 1 inside the turbulent region to 0 outside, because a probe near the edge is sometimes in turbulent fluid and sometimes in irrotational fluid. A point with \(0\lt\gamma_{\mathrm i}\lt1\) at the edge of a jet therefore lies in a flow that is turbulent. The lecture's statement that a record irregular only briefly is not turbulent is right when the briefly irregular record belongs to a transitional flow. It is wrong as a statement about a point at the edge of a turbulent flow: the same flow gives an intermittent record near its edge and a continuously irregular one towards its centre. The lecture's term also has a second meaning in the literature: Kolmogorov (1962, p. 82) writes of the variation of the dissipation of energy, noted by Landau soon after 1941, which "should increase without limit" with the ratio of the large to the small scale. Chapman and Tobak (p. 4) connect intermittency with the "nonspace-filling nature" of turbulence. The lecture's slide uses only the first meaning, the fraction of time; chapter 10 (The energy cascade and Kolmogorov's hypotheses) treats the refinement.

The definition (2.8) needs a criterion for "in turbulent fluid". Corrsin and Kistler obtained it from a hot-wire signal passed through a gate that gave an on-off signal (p. 5). Whether such a criterion gives a number that is meaningful depends on how well the two states are separated, which can be tested on a synthetic record whose true fraction is known. The test in section "Executable checks" builds a record of \(4\times10^{5}\) samples in which the state switches at random between turbulent bursts, of mean length 200 samples, and quiet intervals, with the target fraction 0.30; the realized fraction of the record is 0.300. In the turbulent state the increment of a signal has standard deviation 1; in the quiet state it has standard deviation \(q\). The detector declares "turbulent" where a 20-sample moving average of the absolute increment exceeds a threshold \(\theta\) times the mean absolute increment of the turbulent state. For \(q=0.05\) the estimates are 0.313, 0.300 and 0.279 for \(\theta=0.3\), 0.5 and 0.7: a plateau around the true value 0.300. For \(q=0.3\) the estimates are 0.657, 0.311 and 0.285 for the same three thresholds, and for \(q=0.6\) they are 1.000, 0.888 and 0.407: no plateau, and the number is set by the threshold. The synthetic record shows that "the percentage of time the flow is irregular" is a measurement with a criterion, and that its value is reliable only when the irregular and the regular states differ clearly in the measured statistic. The reference level uses the known state of each sample. A real record has no such label, so the reference level must be estimated, and the error of a real measurement is larger. The test does not measure a real flow, and it assumes a two-state switching model with Gaussian increments.

Ruelle–Takens and the cylinder wake L5 16:09

The slide for 1971 says that Ruelle and Takens describe the "modern view" of turbulence: the equations can produce chaotic solutions with sensitivity to initial conditions, associated with a "strange attractor", and the sequence of transitions as \(\mathrm{Re}\) increases is steady, periodic, quasiperiodic, turbulent. Chapman and Tobak give the content of the argument more precisely than the slide. The Landau theory of turbulence (Landau 1944) "supposed that tori of increasing dimension" would follow one another "in an indefinite sequence of supercritical bifurcations", whereas Ruelle and Takens "argued that beyond a 2-torus, a 'strange attractor' would appear on the next bifurcation" (p. 12); they proposed strange-attractor behaviour as a model for turbulence and argued that the turbulent state would be reached after a finite and small number of bifurcations (p. 8). The attractor has three properties that Chapman and Tobak list: phase mixing, with an autocorrelation function that decays rapidly in time, which they note is shared by all observations of turbulent flows; sensitive dependence on initial conditions; and zero volume in state space, with non-integer dimension (pp. 12–13). The slide's statement that "the Navier–Stokes equations can produce chaotic solutions" is therefore the lecture's compression of an argument made at the level of dynamical systems theory; Chapman and Tobak add that, as of 1985, the effort had focused on simple systems and, in fluid dynamics, "principally on the mechanisms of transition", and that application to fully developed turbulent flows "has not yet been seriously undertaken" (p. 9). The lecture's later statement that the Navier–Stokes equations describe turbulence "exactly" is the premise that Chapman and Tobak accept with the reservations quoted in the previous section.

def estimate(quiet_ratio, thetas, window=20):
    """Fraction of time a smoothed |increment| exceeds theta * (turbulent rms)."""
    if not 0.0 < quiet_ratio <= 1.0:
        raise ValueError("quiet_ratio must lie in (0, 1]")
    sigma = np.where(state, 1.0, quiet_ratio)
    inc = rng.normal(0.0, 1.0, N)*sigma
    smooth = np.convolve(np.abs(inc), np.ones(window)/window, mode="same")
    return [float(np.mean(smooth > th*np.mean(np.abs(inc[state])))) for th in thetas]

What the chapter establishes and what it does not

Five statements are established, each with its boundary. The Reynolds number is the ratio (2.6) of the convective and viscous terms, derived under Reynolds's assumption of one velocity and one length; it is not the only parameter that decides transition in a tube, because Reynolds's two critical velocities, for a quiet entry and for a large entry disturbance, differ by the factor 6.35 (they were measured in different pipes). Reynolds's constants give critical Reynolds numbers of about \(2.0\times10^{3}\) and \(1.28\times10^{4}\) for a tube, under the assumption that his head loss per length is a Poiseuille head loss; neither equals 2300 or 10 000. The nonlinear term vanishes identically in fully developed parallel flow, so (2.3) is an exact solution at every Reynolds number, and its stability is a separate question. The intermittency factor (2.8) is a measurement whose value depends on the criterion unless the two states are well separated, shown on a synthetic record. Each historical statement of the lecture is graded in Tables 2.1, 2.3 and 2.4 by the source opened for it.

Four things are not established. The lecture's "modern definition" has no source in the paper cited for it; its elements are quoted, graded, and not adopted as a necessary-and-sufficient definition. Taylor (1935, 1938), Prandtl (1925), Hinze (1959), von Kármán (1938), Tsinober (2001), Tennekes and Lumley (1972) and Ruelle and Takens (1971) were not opened; their statements are given as the lecture and Chapman and Tobak give them. The lecture's "fully turbulent at 5000 to 10 000" and its "quasi-periodic above 150" differ from the sources opened (irregular range above 300; turbulent boundary layers above about \(3.5\times10^{5}\)), and no source for those two statements was found. The attributions to Boussinesq of a warning and to a 300-year interval are not supported. Speaker judgments that this book does not adopt: that the Reynolds number is the only parameter, that the equations are complete, and that the future lies with deterministic simulation.

After this chapter the reader can do four things. They can compute Reynolds's two critical Reynolds numbers from his constants and name the assumptions that enter. They can test a claimed critical Reynolds number against its flow, its entry and its criterion. They can say which lecture statements are results, which are judgments and which are unsupported. They can compute an intermittency factor from a record and state how far it depends on the criterion.

Exercises

Exercise 1 (compute). Use the program's constants. A tube of diameter 6.35 mm (1/4 inch) carries water at 15 °C. Find (a) the critical velocities \(v_s\) and \(v_c\) of (2.4); (b) the Reynolds numbers of the lecture's boundaries 2300 and 10 000 in velocity; (c) which of the lecture's three classes the speed \(v_s\) falls in.

Solution

(a) \(P(15)=1/(1+0.504+0.0497)=0.6436\). \(v_s=0.6436/(43.79\times0.00635)=2.31\ \mathrm{m/s}\); \(v_c=0.6436/(278\times0.00635)=0.365\ \mathrm{m/s}\); ratio 6.35.

(b) \(\nu(15)=1.787\times10^{-6}\times0.6436=1.150\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\). \(U=\mathrm{Re}\,\nu/D\): 2300 gives 0.417 m/s and 10 000 gives 1.81 m/s.

(c) \(v_s=2.31\) m/s gives \(\mathrm{Re}=2.31\times0.00635/1.150\times10^{-6}=1.28\times10^{4}\), above 10 000, so the lecture's class is "turbulent", and \(v_c\) gives \(\mathrm{Re}=2.0\times10^{3}\), below 2300, "laminar". Reynolds's tube with quiet entry would still have been laminar at 1.81 m/s, the speed at which the lecture's table begins to call the flow turbulent: the table describes pipes with ordinary entry disturbance, not Reynolds's quiet tank.

Exercise 2 (derive). (a) For plane Poiseuille flow (2.3), compute the volume flow per unit width and the wall shear stress, and express the pressure gradient through the mean speed \(U\). (b) Show that the convective term is zero, and say why the same argument fails for flow in a channel whose walls converge.

Solution

(a) \(Q=\int_{-h}^{h}u\,dy=\tfrac{2Gh^{3}}{3\mu}=2hU\). The wall shear stress is \(\mu|u'(h)|=Gh\), and from \(U=Gh^{2}/(3\mu)\), \(G=3\mu U/h^{2}\), so \(\tau_w=3\mu U/h\).

(b) \(u=u(y)\) only, so \(\mathbf U\cdot\nabla\mathbf U=(u\,\partial u/\partial x,0,0)=0\). In a converging channel the mean speed grows with \(x\) and \(\partial u/\partial x\ne0\), so \(u\,\partial u/\partial x\) is nonzero and (2.3) is not a solution. The form of the flow, not the Reynolds number, removes the term.

Exercise 3 (counterexample). A student writes: "By the lecture's definition, turbulence is a chaotic solution that arises from successive instabilities of a laminar flow. Therefore the air behind a grid in a wind tunnel is not turbulent." Give the error, and a case in this chapter's sources that shows the definition's limit.

Solution

The definition is the lecture's and is introduced as "somewhat vague"; it describes how turbulence may arise and is silent about flows maintained or created by a disturbance at the inlet. The grid flow is a flow with irregular, three-dimensional, sensitive motion by its attributes (Table 2.2), whatever its history. The chapter's case is Reynolds's remark (1883, p. 943) that steady motion was unstable to large disturbances before the critical velocity: a finite disturbance of a stable laminar flow also produces eddies, and "successive instabilities of laminar flows" does not describe it. The correct sentence: the definition covers the transition scenario it names; the attributes of Table 2.2 are what a grid flow has.

Exercise 4 (judge a claim). An article states: "Reynolds showed in 1883 that pipe flow is laminar below \(\mathrm{Re}=2300\) and turbulent above 10 000." Using (2.4)–(2.5), say which part is supported, which is not, and give the passage that decides it.

Solution

Not supported: neither number is in the paper. The constants give \(\mathrm{Re}_c\approx2.0\times10^{3}\) (below it a disturbed flow becomes orderly, pp. 946–948) and \(\mathrm{Re}_s\approx1.28\times10^{4}\) (above it eddies appeared in undisturbed water, p. 943). Reynolds gives the two velocities in the ratio 278 to 43.7, that is 6.36 (6.35 with 43.79), and writes that "the critical velocity was very sensitive to disturbance in the water before entering the tubes" (p. 943). A tube at \(\mathrm{Re}=5000\) lies between \(\mathrm{Re}_c\) and \(\mathrm{Re}_s\). The colour-band law predicts steady flow for a quiet entry, because the glass tubes stayed steady up to \(\mathrm{Re}_s\). The resistance law predicts that eddies present at entry do not die out. Reynolds did not run both tests on one tube, so "no single threshold exists" is an inference from the two laws. The supported part: below about 2000 the flow was orderly in all of his experiments.

Exercise 5 (design). You must report the intermittency factor at a point near the edge of a jet from a hot-wire record whose quiet-state increment is 0.3 of the turbulent-state value. Using the program's table, choose a threshold, state the error you expect for the realized fraction 0.300, and say what change in the measurement would let you report with more confidence.

Solution

The row for \(q=0.3\) gives 0.657, 0.311, 0.285 and 0.202 for \(\theta=0.3,0.5,0.7,0.9\). The values at \(\theta=0.5\) and 0.7 bracket 0.300 to within 0.015, and \(\theta=0.5\) is the better of the two; \(\theta=0.3\) is unusable. The error holds for this synthetic model (Gaussian increments, two states, 20-sample averaging) and not for a real record. Reporting with more confidence needs a statistic in which the states are more widely separated, or a longer averaging window to reduce the overlap of the two distributions, and a check that the estimate is flat over a range of thresholds, as it is for \(q=0.05\) between 0.3 and 0.7.

Exercise 6 (check an assumption). The lecture's relation is \(\tau^t_{ij}=\mu_t\bar S_{ij}\). A homogeneous turbulent flow in a box has no mean strain and velocity variances \(\langle u_1^2\rangle=\langle u_2^2\rangle=\langle u_3^2\rangle=1\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\). (a) What stress does the lecture's relation give? (b) What is the turbulent kinetic energy per unit mass \(k=\tfrac12\langle u_iu_i\rangle\), and what normal Reynolds stress \(-\rho\langle u_1^2\rangle\) does the flow have? (c) What term in Boussinesq's expression (2.10) accounts for the difference?

Solution

(a) The mean strain is zero, so the relation gives \(\tau^t_{ij}=0\).

(b) \(k=\tfrac12(1+1+1)=1.5\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\). The normal Reynolds stress is \(-\rho\langle u_1^2\rangle=-\rho\) (in N/m² for \(\rho\) in kg/m³): not zero.

(c) The isotropic term \(-\tfrac23K\delta_{ij}\) of (2.10). With \(K=\rho k\), since \(K\) has the units of the stresses, the term equals \(-\tfrac23\rho k=-\rho\times(1\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2})\), that is \(-\rho\) in N/m\(^2\) for \(\rho\) in kg/m\(^3\) on each normal component, which is the normal Reynolds stress \(-\rho\langle u_1^2\rangle\). The strain-proportional term cannot represent a stress that exists without strain; the isotropic part carries it, and chapter 9 treats it with the sign conventions.
