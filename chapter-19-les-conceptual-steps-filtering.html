Large-eddy simulation: conceptual steps and filtering

A direct numerical simulation of the air-jet station used throughout this book, with Kolmogorov length \(79.0\ \mu\mathrm m\), needs about \(6\times10^{3}\) grid points in each direction of a 1 m periodic box, about \(2\times10^{11}\) in total. A large-eddy simulation of the same turbulence that resolves 80 % of the kinetic energy needs about 56 points in each direction, about \(1.8\times10^{5}\) in total, a factor of \(1.3\times10^{6}\) fewer. The saving comes from a single decision: the simulation computes a smoothed velocity field and models the effect of the motions removed by the smoothing. This chapter defines that smoothing, the filtering operation, exactly; derives what it does to a Fourier mode, to the energy spectrum and to the rate of strain; shows where it differs from the Reynolds average; and derives the filter width at which a stated fraction of the energy is resolved, with the assumptions on which each number depends.

The objects of the chapter and the running case

The formal model is the one of the preceding chapters: an incompressible fluid with constant density \(\rho\) and constant kinematic viscosity \(\nu\), with velocity field \(\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)\) at position \(\mathbf x=(x_1,x_2,x_3)\) and time \(t\), governed by the Navier–Stokes equations. The velocity is a random field in the sense of chapter 8: one experiment, or one simulation started from slightly different initial conditions, gives one realization, and statistics are expectations over the ensemble of realizations, written \(\langle\cdot\rangle\). The lecture often says "time average" for \(\langle\cdot\rangle\); for a statistically stationary flow the two agree when the process is ergodic and the record long (chapter 8, section "Time averages, ergodicity and the length of a record"), and this chapter writes the expectation throughout.

Several statements need a field that is statistically homogeneous (its statistics do not change under a translation of the origin) and, where stated, isotropic (they do not change under rotations and reflections of the axes either). For such a field, chapter 8 defines the velocity-spectrum tensor \(\Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)\) as the Fourier transform of the two-point correlation \(R_{ij}(\mathbf r)=\langle u_i(\mathbf x)u_j(\mathbf x+\mathbf r)\rangle\) (8.29)–(8.30), and the energy-spectrum function \(E(\kappa)\) as its shell integral (8.32), with \(\int_0^\infty E\,d\kappa=k\), the turbulent kinetic energy per unit mass. Here \(\mathbf u=\mathbf U-\langle\mathbf U\rangle\) is the fluctuation (9.1). The argument of \(E\) is always written.

The running case is the air-jet station of chapters 6 to 13, treated as homogeneous isotropic turbulence with the scales chosen there: \(\nu=1.4607\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}\), \(k=6\ \mathrm{m^2/s^2}\), dissipation rate \(\varepsilon=80\ \mathrm{m^2/s^3}\), Kolmogorov length \(\eta=(\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}=79.0\ \mu\mathrm m\) and Taylor-scale Reynolds number \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=453\). The large-scale length is \(L=k^{3/2}/\varepsilon=183.7\ \mathrm{mm}\). A real jet station is neither homogeneous nor known to be isotropic; the numbers illustrate the relations and are not predictions for a particular jet. Where a statement needs a spectrum of definite shape, the chapter uses a model spectrum stated in full in the section "The filtered one-dimensional spectrum".

Two symbols need a remark at first use. The residual velocity of large-eddy simulation is written \(\mathbf u'\), as on the slides; in earlier chapters \(u'\) was the root-mean-square velocity \((2k/3)^{1/2}\). In this chapter \(\mathbf u'\) and \(u'\) always mean the residual field. The grid spacing is written \(h\), as on the slides, because \(\Delta\) is reserved for the filter width.

What a large-eddy simulation resolves and what it models L50 2:31

The slide states the idea in one sentence: in large-eddy simulation (LES) the larger three-dimensional unsteady turbulent motions are directly represented, whereas the effects of the smaller-scale motions are modelled. "Directly represented", or resolved, means that the simulation computes these motions from their equations of motion on a grid fine enough to carry them. The lecture draws three grids over a sketch of one large eddy containing one small eddy. The direct-simulation grid is fine enough to show both. The LES grid shows the large eddy, while the small eddy falls inside a grid cell and is invisible to it; its effect on the large eddy must be supplied by a model, the subgrid-scale model. The grid of a Reynolds-averaged (RANS) calculation resolves neither: it carries only the mean flow, and all turbulent motions are represented by a model, such as the turbulent-viscosity models of chapter 9 and of the chapter "Turbulent viscosity models". The slide calls this group "Reynolds-stress models", in the sense of models for the Reynolds stresses, and the speaker uses the two names interchangeably.

The slide places LES between the other two in computational expense and says that it is motivated by the limitations of each. The limitation of direct numerical simulation (DNS) is its cost. Chapter 5, section "A resolution budget for a direct simulation", derives that the number of grid points grows as \(\mathrm{Re}^{9/4}\) and the work as \(\mathrm{Re}^{3}\), with \(\mathrm{Re}\) the Reynolds number of the largest eddies; the slide states the same \(\mathrm{Re}^3\) and concludes that DNS is inapplicable to high-Reynolds-number flows. The slide adds that nearly all of the computational effort in DNS is spent on the smallest, dissipative motions, whereas the energy and the anisotropy are contained predominantly in the larger scales. The speaker illustrates this with a rule of thumb, "90 % of the effort on resolving 10 % of the energy" in the dissipation range. The slide's statement is "nearly all"; the 90/10 split is an illustration, not a result, and this chapter uses neither number.

The limitation of a RANS model is its accuracy. The speaker's example is the turbulent-viscosity hypothesis, which makes the Reynolds stress anisotropy proportional to the local mean rate of strain: downstream of a contraction, where the mean strain rate has returned to zero, the hypothesis gives zero anisotropy, whereas the measured stresses retain the history of the strain. The slide's third point follows: because the large-scale unsteady motions are represented explicitly, LES "can be expected to be more accurate and reliable than Reynolds-stress models" in flows where the large-scale unsteadiness is significant. The lecture's further statement that accuracy and cost both increase from RANS to LES to DNS is a trend, not a theorem; chapter 16 grades it, and a LES with a poor model or an inadequate grid can be less accurate than a well-calibrated RANS calculation of a simple flow.

The physical argument for the division between resolved and modelled motions rests on chapter 10. The large motions are affected by the flow geometry and are not universal; they carry the directional bias, the anisotropy, from which one can tell a jet from a channel. Below the demarcation length \(\ell_{EI}\), Kolmogorov's hypothesis of local isotropy (chapter 10, section "The hypothesis of local isotropy") states that the small-scale motions are statistically isotropic, and the first similarity hypothesis states that their statistics depend only on \(\nu\) and \(\varepsilon\). Motions with these properties are the ones the slide expects to be "represented by simple models", because a model of them need not know the geometry of the flow. The hypotheses concern high-Reynolds-number local structure; they do not guarantee that a particular model is accurate, and near a wall the energy-containing motions themselves become small (the limits section returns to this). The speaker draws the length-scale axis of chapter 10, with the energy-containing range above \(\ell_{EI}\), the inertial subrange between \(\ell_{EI}\) and \(\ell_{DI}\), and the dissipation range below \(\ell_{DI}\), and places the resolved motions of LES above a length "slightly below" \(\ell_{EI}\). The vast computational cost of explicitly representing the small-scale motions, the slide concludes, is avoided.

The speaker quantifies the premise twice: "80 % of the energy is contained within the large scales", and an LES captures "80 percent of the energy, 90 percent of the energy, not 100 percent", only a DNS capturing all of it. These are statements about a spectrum, and they can be checked for one. For the model spectrum of the jet introduced below, a sharp spectral filter of width \(\ell_{EI}\) keeps 83.5 % of the kinetic energy, and the width that keeps exactly 80 % is \(1.29\ell_{EI}\) (section "Filtering in three dimensions and the resolved fraction of energy"). The large-scale unsteadiness that the speaker says is "captured to a certain extent" is therefore the unsteadiness of motions carrying about four fifths of the energy; the remaining fifth, and nearly all of the strain rate and dissipation, belong to the modelled motions (section "The filtered rate of strain and Lilly's estimate"). The figures depend on the spectrum and on the definition of \(\ell_{EI}\), and the chapter derives them under stated assumptions rather than adopting them.

Four conceptual steps L50 19:25

The slide lists the four conceptual steps of LES. They organize the whole module, and each is named here with the place in the book where it is developed.

Step 1, filtering. A filtering operation is defined to decompose the velocity \(\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)\) into the sum of a filtered, or resolved, component \(\overline{\mathbf U}(\mathbf x,t)\) and a residual, or subgrid-scale (SGS), component \(\mathbf u'(\mathbf x,t)\):

\[ \mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)=\overline{\mathbf U}(\mathbf x,t)+\mathbf u'(\mathbf x,t). \tag{19.1} \]

The filtered velocity field \(\overline{\mathbf U}(\mathbf x,t)\) is three-dimensional and time-dependent, and it represents the motion of the large eddies. This step is the subject of the rest of this chapter.

Step 2, the filtered equations. The equations for the evolution of \(\overline{\mathbf U}\) are derived from the Navier–Stokes equations. They have the standard form, except that the momentum equation contains the residual-stress tensor (or SGS stress tensor), which arises from the residual motions. The derivation belongs to the chapter "Filtered equations, the Smagorinsky model, and LES in wavenumber space".

Step 3, closure. Closure is obtained by modelling the residual-stress tensor, most simply by an eddy-viscosity model, of which the Smagorinsky model is the example the lecture names. This is also the subject of that chapter.

Step 4, solution. The model filtered equations are solved numerically for \(\overline{\mathbf U}(\mathbf x,t)\), which provides an approximation to the large-scale motions in one realization of the turbulent flow.

The speaker writes the Reynolds decomposition (9.1), \(\mathbf U=\langle\mathbf U\rangle+\mathbf u\), above (19.1) and compares them. The forms are the same; the meanings differ. The mean \(\langle\mathbf U\rangle\) is a statistic: it is not random, and in a statistically stationary flow it does not depend on time, so the Reynolds-averaged equations are steady. The filtered velocity \(\overline{\mathbf U}\) is a smoothed version of one realization: it is random, three-dimensional and unsteady, and so are the equations it obeys. This is the sense of the speaker's "beauty of LES": the large-scale unsteadiness is computed, not modelled. The section "A filter is not an average: the filtered residual" derives the mathematical difference.

Step 4 needs a qualification that the lecture's description does not state. The lecture describes the result as the filtered velocity of one realization: take a realization from a DNS or an experiment, filter it, and that is what the LES produces. Pope (2004) argues that this view "is not sustainable" (p. 9). He writes \(\mathbf W(\mathbf x,t)\) for the resolved field and defines it as the solution of the LES equations, not as the filtered value of \(\mathbf U\). Even a perfect model cannot make \(\mathbf W\) equal \(\overline{\mathbf U}\): the filtered field \(\overline{\mathbf U}\) is random, "whose future evolution is not determined by its current state", so even with \(\mathbf W=\overline{\mathbf U}\) imposed at \(t=0\), for \(t\gt0\) the filtered field has a statistical distribution and no value of \(\mathbf W\) equals it (p. 9). The residual motions, which the filtered field does not contain, are part of what its future depends on. The relationship between \(\mathbf W\) and \(\mathbf U\) "can only be statistical" (pp. 9–10): an LES is judged by whether the statistics it predicts equal the statistics of the turbulent flow. The four steps remain the correct description of how the equations are built; the claim that their solution is the filtered realization is an idealization.

Filtering is a convolution with a normalized kernel L51 3:13

In DNS the velocity field must be resolved down to lengths of the order of the Kolmogorov scale \(\eta\). In LES, the slide states, a low-pass filtering operation is performed so that the filtered velocity field can be adequately resolved on a relatively coarse grid. "Low-pass" is a term from signal processing: the operation lets the low wavenumbers (the long wavelengths, the large scales) through and removes or attenuates the high wavenumbers (the small scales). The speaker draws a fluctuating signal and its smooth filtered version, with the filter width \(\Delta\) marked as the length over which the fluctuations are smoothed away.

The slide then states the design rule. The required grid spacing \(h\) is proportional to the specified filter width \(\Delta\). In the ideal case the filter width is somewhat smaller than \(\ell_{EI}\), the size of the smallest energy-containing motions, and the grid spacing is as large as possible subject to the condition that the energy-containing motions are resolved. The speaker gives the reasons for both halves of the rule: a width larger than \(\ell_{EI}\) leaves part of the energy-containing motions unresolved, which removes the premise of LES, and a width much smaller than \(\ell_{EI}\) increases the cost and approaches DNS. Because \(\ell_{EI}\) is a nominal boundary, not a sharp one, the speaker recommends a width somewhat below it, in the inertial subrange. The section "Filtering in three dimensions and the resolved fraction of energy" makes the rule quantitative.

The definition. The general filtering operation, which the slide attributes to Leonard (1974), is

\[ \overline{\mathbf U}(\mathbf x,t)=\int G(\mathbf r,\mathbf x)\,\mathbf U(\mathbf x-\mathbf r,t)\,d\mathbf r, \tag{19.2} \]

where the integration is over the entire flow domain and the specified filter function \(G\), also called the kernel, satisfies the normalization condition

\[ \int G(\mathbf r,\mathbf x)\,d\mathbf r=1. \tag{19.3} \]

The filtered value at \(\mathbf x\) is a weighted average of the velocity at the points \(\mathbf x-\mathbf r\) around it, and \(G(\mathbf r,\mathbf x)\) is the weight given to the point at separation \(\mathbf r\). The normalization makes the average a true average: by (19.3), a uniform velocity field is unchanged by the filter. The speaker's reason, "to recover the velocity", is this property. In the simplest case the filter is homogeneous: \(G\) does not depend on \(\mathbf x\), and (19.2) is a convolution. Leonard's 1974 paper was not available and is cited here only as the slide's attribution.

The residual field is defined by (19.1) as \(\mathbf u'(\mathbf x,t)=\mathbf U(\mathbf x,t)-\overline{\mathbf U}(\mathbf x,t)\). Two properties of a homogeneous filter are used repeatedly. It is linear: the filtered value of a sum is the sum of the filtered values. It commutes with differentiation: since \(G\) does not depend on \(\mathbf x\), \(\partial\overline{\mathbf U}/\partial x_j=\int G(\mathbf r)\,\partial\mathbf U(\mathbf x-\mathbf r,t)/\partial x_j\,d\mathbf r=\overline{\partial\mathbf U/\partial x_j}\). The second property implies that the filtered field of an incompressible flow is itself divergence-free. Linearity holds for every filter of the form (19.2); commutation with differentiation fails, in general, for a filter whose width varies with position.

A filter is not an average: the filtered residual L51 13:29

The slide states that (19.1) appears analogous to the Reynolds decomposition, and names two important differences: \(\overline{\mathbf U}(\mathbf x,t)\) is a random field, and, in general, the filtered residual is not zero, \(\overline{\mathbf u'}(\mathbf x,t)\ne0\). The second difference has an exact mathematical form.

Taking the expectation twice gives the same result as taking it once, \(\langle\langle\mathbf U\rangle\rangle=\langle\mathbf U\rangle\), because \(\langle\mathbf U\rangle\) is not random. Applying \(\langle\cdot\rangle\) to (9.1) therefore gives \(\langle\mathbf u\rangle=\langle\mathbf U\rangle-\langle\langle\mathbf U\rangle\rangle=0\). An operation \(P\) with the property \(P(P(f))=P(f)\) for every \(f\) is called a projection; the expectation is a projection, and the zero mean of the fluctuation is a consequence of that property alone. Filtering (19.1), by linearity,

\[ \overline{\mathbf u'}=\overline{\mathbf U}-\overline{\overline{\mathbf U}}. \tag{19.4} \]

The filtered residual vanishes for every field if and only if the doubly filtered field equals the filtered field, that is, if and only if the filter is a projection. The speaker's reason that it does not vanish, that \(\overline{\mathbf U}\) is a random variable and "one random variable minus another random variable" is not zero, does not decide the question: \(\mathbf U-\langle\mathbf U\rangle\) is also a difference of random quantities, yet its expectation is zero. What decides it is whether filtering twice smooths more than filtering once. For most filters it does: each application averages again over a neighbourhood. The section "The transfer function multiplies each Fourier mode" proves that among the filters of the course exactly one, the sharp spectral filter, is a projection, so the slide's "in general" is a necessary qualification and the lecture's own list of filters contains the exception. The program confirms it on a random periodic signal of unit root-mean-square value, filtered with \(\kappa_c=32\) times the lowest wavenumber: the root-mean-square filtered residual is \(6.22\times10^{-2}\) for the box filter and \(5.72\times10^{-2}\) for the Gaussian filter, against root-mean-square residuals \(u'\) of 0.2551 and 0.2279, and \(1.83\times10^{-16}\), zero to rounding, for the sharp spectral filter.

The randomness of \(\overline{\mathbf U}\) is the first difference the slide names. Averaging (19.2) over the ensemble and exchanging the expectation with the integral gives \(\langle\overline{\mathbf U}\rangle=\overline{\langle\mathbf U\rangle}\): the mean of the filtered field is the filtered mean field. The filter removes small-scale fluctuations from each realization; it does not remove the randomness. The speaker's closing remark states this: \(\mathbf U\), \(\overline{\mathbf U}\), \(\mathbf u'\) and \(\overline{\mathbf u'}\) are all random, and no number of filterings eliminates the unsteady components.

One dimension. The lecture develops the remaining properties in one dimension. Let \(U(x)\) be a random scalar function defined for all \(x\) (\(-\infty\lt x\lt\infty\)), and \(G(r)\) a homogeneous filter. The filtered field is the convolution

\[ \overline U(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}G(r)\,U(x-r)\,dr. \tag{19.5} \]

The slides write \(U(x)\) here and lower-case \(u(x)\) for the same kind of function two slides later; this chapter writes \(U\) for a field that may have a non-zero mean and \(u\) for a zero-mean field, and says so where it switches.

The box filter. With the box filter, the slide states, \(\overline U(x)\) is simply the average of \(U(x')\) over the interval \(x-\tfrac12\Delta\lt x'\lt x+\tfrac12\Delta\). The kernel is \(1/\Delta\) on an interval of length \(\Delta\), so its integral is 1, and its second moment is \(\int_{-\Delta/2}^{\Delta/2}r^2\,dr/\Delta=\Delta^2/12\). The speaker first marks the interval on the figure as \(-1\le r/\Delta\le1\) and corrects it to \(-\tfrac12\le r/\Delta\le\tfrac12\) (L51 20:06); the corrected interval agrees with the table.

The Gaussian filter. The slide states that the Gaussian filter function is the Gaussian distribution with mean zero and variance \(\sigma^2=\Delta^2/12\), attributed to Leonard (1974). The normal density with variance \(\sigma^2\) is \((2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2}\exp(-r^2/2\sigma^2)\); with \(\sigma^2=\Delta^2/12\) this is the table entry, since \(2\pi\sigma^2=\pi\Delta^2/6\) and \(2\sigma^2=\Delta^2/6\). The variance is chosen so that the Gaussian and the box filter have the same second moment, as the table caption states. The transfer function of a normal density is \(\exp(-\kappa^2\sigma^2/2)=\exp(-\kappa^2\Delta^2/24)\).

The sharp spectral filter. This filter is defined by its transfer function: it keeps every Fourier mode with \(|\kappa|\lt\kappa_c\) unchanged and removes every mode with \(|\kappa|\gt\kappa_c\). Its kernel follows by the inverse of the transform (19.8) below:

\[ G(r)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\kappa_c}^{\kappa_c}e^{i\kappa r}\,d\kappa=\frac{\sin(\kappa_cr)}{\pi r}=\frac{\sin(\pi r/\Delta)}{\pi r}. \tag{19.6} \]

The slide prints \(\sin(\pi r\Delta)/(\pi r)\). That expression is not dimensionally consistent unless \(\Delta\) is measured in units in which it equals 1: the argument of a sine must be dimensionless, and \(r\Delta\) has dimension length squared. With \(\Delta=2\) the program evaluates the inverse transform of \(H(\kappa_c-|\kappa|)\) numerically at \(r=0.3\) and obtains 0.4817, equal to \(\sin(\pi r/\Delta)/(\pi r)\) to four digits, while \(\sin(\pi r\Delta)/(\pi r)=1.0091\). The slide's figure shows these oscillations as the dot-dashed curve. Its integral is 1 only as an improper integral, \(\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{-R}^{R}G\,dr\), because \(|G|\) is not integrable, and its second moment does not exist.

The Cauchy and Pao filters. The lecture names them as less often used. The Cauchy kernel is the Cauchy distribution with scale \(a\Delta\): its integral is 1, it is positive, and its second moment is infinite, because \(r^2G\) tends to the constant \(a\Delta/\pi\) as \(|r|\to\infty\). The Pao filter is specified only in spectral space; the slide leaves its kernel blank, and the speaker says that it has no equivalent filter function. A kernel exists, the inverse transform of the transfer function, but it has no elementary closed form.

The transfer function multiplies each Fourier mode L52 3:55

The Fourier convention. The slide writes \(\hat U(\kappa)\equiv\mathscr F\{U(x)\}\) for the Fourier transform of the velocity and defines the transfer function as \(2\pi\) times the Fourier transform of the filter. Both statements fix the convention. In one dimension the transform used by chapter 8 for the spectrum tensor (8.29) is

\[ \mathscr F\{f(x)\}(\kappa)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,e^{-i\kappa x}\,dx,\qquad f(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathscr F\{f\}(\kappa)\,e^{i\kappa x}\,d\kappa, \tag{19.7} \]

and the transfer function is

\[ \hat G(\kappa)\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}G(r)\,e^{-i\kappa r}\,dr=2\pi\,\mathscr F\{G(r)\}. \tag{19.8} \]

The factor \(2\pi\) is placed so that the transfer function is dimensionless and equal to 1 at \(\kappa=0\); the inverse of (19.8) is \(G(r)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int\hat G(\kappa)e^{i\kappa r}\,d\kappa\), which was used in (19.6).

The convolution theorem. Transforming (19.5) with (19.7), and substituting \(y=x-r\) at fixed \(r\),

\[ \mathscr F\{\overline U\}(\kappa)=\frac1{2\pi}\int\!\!\int G(r)\,U(y)\,e^{-i\kappa(y+r)}\,dy\,dr=\int G(r)e^{-i\kappa r}dr\cdot\frac1{2\pi}\int U(y)e^{-i\kappa y}dy=\hat G(\kappa)\,\hat U(\kappa). \tag{19.9} \]

A convolution in physical space becomes a product in wavenumber space, which is the property the speaker emphasizes. The constant comes out as 1 precisely because of the \(2\pi\) in (19.8). The derivation exchanges the order of integration, which requires \(G\) and \(U\) to be integrable; a statistically homogeneous random field is not integrable over the whole line, and its Fourier transform exists only as a generalized function. The statement (19.9) is used in this chapter in two forms that avoid that difficulty: mode by mode for a periodic field, and for the spectrum, in (19.12) below.

Three properties. The slide lists them. Because the filter functions \(G(r)\) of Table 19.1 are real and even, so are the transfer functions: \(\hat G(\kappa)=\int G(r)\cos(\kappa r)\,dr\), the sine part vanishing by symmetry. At the origin the transfer functions are unity, because \(\hat G(0)=\int G\,dr=1\) by (19.3). And the sharp spectral filter annihilates all Fourier modes with \(|\kappa|\gt\kappa_c\), whereas it has no effect on the lower-wavenumber modes. A fourth property follows from the first two. For an even, real kernel with a finite second moment, expanding the cosine gives \(\hat G(\kappa)=1-\tfrac12\kappa^2\int r^2G\,dr+\dots\) at small \(\kappa\): two filters with the same second moment act alike on the largest scales. This is the reason for matching the box and the Gaussian filters by their second moment (Exercise 1).

A single mode. The simplest check of (19.9) needs no generalized functions. Let \(U(x)=\cos(\kappa x)\). Then

\[ \overline U(x)=\int G(r)\cos(\kappa x-\kappa r)\,dr=\cos(\kappa x)\int G(r)\cos(\kappa r)\,dr+\sin(\kappa x)\int G(r)\sin(\kappa r)\,dr=\hat G(\kappa)\cos(\kappa x), \tag{19.10} \]

because the second integral vanishes for an even kernel. The filter leaves a Fourier mode a Fourier mode of the same wavenumber and multiplies its amplitude by \(\hat G(\kappa)\). The filtered one-dimensional spectrum L52 12:42

The slide considers \(U(x)\), a velocity component along a line, to be a statistically homogeneous field, with the fluctuating field \(u(x)\equiv U(x)-\langle U\rangle\). Its autocovariance is \(R(r)=\langle u(x+r)u(x)\rangle\), and the spectrum is defined as twice its Fourier transform:

\[ E_{11}(\kappa)=\frac1\pi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}R(r)\,e^{-i\kappa r}\,dr,\qquad L_{11}=\frac1{\langle u^2\rangle}\int_0^\infty R(r)\,dr. \tag{19.11} \]

With \(u=u_1\) and \(r\) along \(x_1\) this is the one-dimensional spectrum (13.13) of chapter 13, one-sided, with \(\int_0^\infty E_{11}\,d\kappa=\langle u_1^2\rangle\); "twice" refers to the convention (19.7), since \(1/\pi=2\times1/(2\pi)\). \(L_{11}\) is the longitudinal integral length scale.

What filtering does to the spectrum. The slide shows the filtered spectrum without stating its relation to \(E_{11}\); it follows from (19.5) and homogeneity. The filtered fluctuation is \(\bar u(x)=\int G(r)u(x-r)\,dr\), so its autocovariance is

\[ \bar R(s)=\langle\bar u(x+s)\bar u(x)\rangle=\int\!\!\int G(r)G(r')\,R(s-r+r')\,dr\,dr', \]

because \(\langle u(x+s-r)u(x-r')\rangle=R(s-r+r')\) by homogeneity. This is \(R\) convolved with \(G\) and with \(G\) reflected. Transforming as in (19.9), each convolution contributes a factor \(\hat G(\kappa)\), the reflected one its complex conjugate, and since \(\hat G\) is real,

\[ \overline E_{11}(\kappa)=\hat G(\kappa)^2\,E_{11}(\kappa). \tag{19.12} \]

The square appears because energy is quadratic in velocity, which is the speaker's explanation of the analogous three-dimensional formula in lecture 53. The derivation needs homogeneity and a homogeneous filter; it needs no assumption about the shape of the spectrum.

How many Fourier modes a filtered field needs L52 18:34

The slide asks: what grid spacing \(h\) is required to resolve the filtered field \(\bar u(x)\) adequately? Consider \(u(x)\), a zero-mean statistically homogeneous field on the interval \(0\le x\lt\mathcal L\), and let the filtered field be represented by its values at the \(N\) nodes of a uniform grid of spacing \(h=\mathcal L/N\). Suppose \(u(x)\) is periodic with period \(\mathcal L\) (chapter 12, (12.1)) and consider its Fourier series in the form of an inverse discrete Fourier transform,

\[ u(x)=\sum_{n=1-\frac12N_{\max}}^{\frac12N_{\max}}c_n\,e^{i\kappa_nx},\qquad\kappa_n=\frac{2\pi n}{\mathcal L}, \tag{19.14} \]

where \(c_n\) is the Fourier coefficient of the \(n\)th wavenumber. The series is the one-dimensional form of (12.2)–(12.3), with the lowest wavenumber \(\kappa_0=2\pi/\mathcal L\), truncated at the even integer \(N_{\max}\). The slide states that \(N_{\max}\) determines the maximum resolved wavenumber, \(\kappa_{\max}=\kappa_{N_{\max}/2}=\pi N_{\max}/\mathcal L\), and is chosen sufficiently large to resolve \(u(x)\): it is the DNS resolution. The discrete Fourier transform gives a one-to-one mapping between the \(N_{\max}\) coefficients and the values \(u(nh_{\max})\), \(n=0,1,\dots,N_{\max}-1\), on the grid of spacing

\[ h_{\max}\equiv\frac{\mathcal L}{N_{\max}}=\frac{\pi}{\kappa_{\max}}, \tag{19.15} \]

which is therefore the largest grid spacing that can resolve \(u(x)\). The relation \(h=\pi/\kappa_{\max}\) states that the shortest wave on a grid has wavelength \(2h\), two points per wavelength. The speaker connects \(\kappa_{\max}\) to the Kolmogorov scale through a resolution rule "of about 2.1" in units of \(\eta\); the rule of chapter 12, \(\kappa_{\max}\eta\approx1.5\), from Yeung, Sreenivasan and Pope (2018), gives \(h_{\max}=\pi\eta/1.5=2.09\eta\). A real DNS code also removes aliasing errors, which reduces \(\kappa_{\max}\) for a given \(N\); chapter 12 used that dealiased relation and found \(N=6.4\times10^{3}\) for the jet in a 1 m box, against \(6.0\times10^{3}\) from (19.15).

The filtered coefficients. The Fourier series of the filtered field is

\[ \bar u(x)=\sum_{n=1-\frac12N_{\max}}^{\frac12N_{\max}}\bar c_n\,e^{i\kappa_nx},\qquad\bar c_n=\hat G(\kappa_n)\,c_n, \tag{19.16} \]

where the \(\bar c_n\) are the coefficients of \(u(x)\) attenuated by the transfer function of the filter. This is (19.10) applied to each mode, and it is exact for a periodic field: a homogeneous filter acting on \(e^{i\kappa_nx}\) returns \(\hat G(\kappa_n)e^{i\kappa_nx}\). The coefficients of the filtered field are obtained from those of the fully resolved field \(u\), not from \(\bar u\).

The sharp spectral filter. In lecture 53 (L53 1:20) the slide takes the sharp spectral filter with cutoff \(\kappa_c\lt\kappa_{\max}\), and chooses \(\kappa_c\) so that \(N=\kappa_c\mathcal L/\pi\) is an even integer. Then, with \(\hat G=H(\kappa_c-|\kappa|)\),

\[ \bar c_n=c_n\ \ \text{for}\ |n|\lt\tfrac12N,\qquad\bar c_n=0\ \ \text{for}\ |n|\ge\tfrac12N,\qquad\bar u(x)=\sum_{n=1-\frac12N}^{\frac12N-1}c_n\,e^{i\kappa_nx}. \tag{19.17} \]

(The slide writes the upper limit of the sum as \(\tfrac12N\), as in (19.14). That mode has \(|\kappa_n|=\kappa_c\), so the filter removes it, \(\bar c_{N/2}=0\), and its term is absent from the filtered field; the sum therefore ends at \(\tfrac12N-1\). The \(N\) filtered coefficients \(\bar c_n\), \(1-\tfrac12N\le n\le\tfrac12N\), one of them zero, are what the \(N\)-point grid below carries.) Correspondingly, without loss of information, the discrete Fourier transform allows \(\bar u(x)\) to be represented in physical space on a grid of spacing

\[ h\equiv\frac{\mathcal L}{N}=\frac{\pi}{\kappa_c}. \tag{19.18} \]

"It is for this reason", the slide states, "that the characteristic filter width is defined as \(\Delta\equiv\pi/\kappa_c\)." The filtered field given by the sharp spectral filter is represented exactly by its \(N=\kappa_c\mathcal L/\pi\) Fourier coefficients (19.17), of which the one at \(n=\tfrac12N\) is zero, or by its \(N\) values on a grid of spacing \(h=\Delta=\pi/\kappa_c\). More Fourier modes, or more grid points, provide no further information: the coefficients above \(\kappa_c\) are zero. The speaker's version of the rule: \(h\) should not exceed \(\Delta\), because then the grid cannot carry the filtered field, and a grid finer than \(\Delta\) "wastes computing resources".

The statement "\(h=\Delta\) represents the filtered field exactly" holds for the sharp spectral filter only, for the reason found in the section "The transfer function multiplies each Fourier mode". The Gaussian and box transfer functions are non-zero above \(\kappa_c\), so the filtered field has non-zero coefficients up to \(\kappa_{\max}\) and is not band-limited; on a grid with \(h=\Delta\) its modes above \(\kappa_c\) are not represented. Pope (2004) reports the practice for such filters (p. 11): in LES with an explicit filter width, the numerical solution becomes independent of \(h\) only when \(h/\Delta\) is small enough, and test calculations reported there require \(h/\Delta\le\tfrac14\) for a scheme with second-order spatial accuracy and \(h/\Delta\le\tfrac12\) for sixth-order accuracy; physical LES involving some numerical error "is generally performed with \(h/\Delta=1\) or \(h/\Delta=\tfrac12\)"; and when the filter width does not appear in the equations, so that the grid alone sets the resolution, one simply defines \(\Delta=h\). So the lecture's \(h=\Delta\) is exact for the sharp spectral filter in a spectral method, and a choice with numerical consequences otherwise.

The running case. For the jet, the program finds (next section) that a sharp spectral filter resolves 80 % of the energy of the model spectrum at \(\Delta=17.95\ \mathrm{mm}\). With \(h=\Delta\), a 1 m periodic box needs \(N=56\) points per direction, \(1.8\times10^{5}\) in total; DNS with \(h=2.09\eta\) needs 6044 per direction, \(2.2\times10^{11}\) in total. The ratio of point counts is \(1.3\times10^{6}\). The work ratio is larger, because the stable time step is proportional to the grid spacing (chapter 5), and smaller by whatever the subgrid model costs per point.

Filtering in three dimensions and the resolved fraction of energy L53 8:31

The slide generalizes. The filtered velocity field is defined in terms of the function \(G(\mathbf r,\mathbf x)\) of (19.2); the filter may be uniform (independent of \(\mathbf x\)) or non-uniform, isotropic or anisotropic. All of the one-dimensional filters can generate uniform isotropic three-dimensional filters: the box filter becomes a volume average over a sphere of radius \(\Delta/2\), and the sharp spectral filter annihilates all Fourier modes with \(|\boldsymbol\kappa|\ge\kappa_c\). Most of the results obtained in one dimension carry through straightforwardly.

The filtered energy spectrum. For an isotropic filter the transfer function depends only on \(\kappa=|\boldsymbol\kappa|\). The argument that gave (19.12), with three-dimensional integrals, gives \(\overline\Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)=\hat G(\kappa)^2\Phi_{ij}(\boldsymbol\kappa)\) for the spectrum tensor of the filtered fluctuation. The shell integral (8.32) contains \(\hat G(\kappa)^2\) as a constant on each shell, so for homogeneous turbulence the filtered energy-spectrum function is

\[ \overline E(\kappa)=\hat G(\kappa)^2\,E(\kappa), \tag{19.19} \]

the formula on the slide. Isotropy of the turbulence is not needed, only homogeneity and an isotropic filter. For a filter that is not isotropic, \(\hat G\) varies over the shell and (19.19) does not hold.

Two kinds of isotropic filter. For the Gaussian, the isotropic three-dimensional kernel is the product of three one-dimensional kernels, \(G(\mathbf r)=G(r_1)G(r_2)G(r_3)\), because \(\exp(-6|\mathbf r|^2/\Delta^2)\) factorizes, and its transfer function is \(\exp(-\kappa^2\Delta^2/24)\) with \(\kappa=|\boldsymbol\kappa|\). For the box and the sharp spectral filters the isotropic versions are different filters from the products of one-dimensional ones. The sharp spectral filter of the slide keeps a sphere of wavevectors, not a cube. The spherical box filter, the average over a ball of radius \(\Delta/2\), has the transfer function

\[ \hat G(\kappa)=\frac{24}{\Delta^3}\int_0^{\Delta/2}\frac{\sin\kappa s}{\kappa s}\,s^2\,ds=\frac{3(\sin x-x\cos x)}{x^3},\qquad x=\tfrac12\kappa\Delta, \tag{19.20} \]

obtained by integrating \(e^{-i\boldsymbol\kappa\cdot\mathbf r}\) over the ball in spherical coordinates. The program checks (19.20) by quadrature at \(x=1\) (0.903506) and \(x=4\) (0.087083). The second moment of the ball in each direction is \((\Delta/2)^2/5=\Delta^2/20\), not the \(\Delta^2/12\) of the one-dimensional box, so a spherical box of "width" \(\Delta\) smooths less than a one-dimensional box of the same width. The name \(\Delta\) does not by itself fix the amount of smoothing; the kernel does.

The sharp spectral filter on a cubic grid. The one-dimensional statement that \(N=\kappa_c\mathcal L/\pi\) points represent the sharply filtered field exactly has a three-dimensional counterpart with a qualification. A grid of \(N^3\) points carries the Fourier coefficients with \(|\kappa_i|\lt\kappa_c\) in each direction separately, a cube of side \(2\kappa_c\) in wavenumber space. The isotropic sharp spectral filter keeps the sphere \(|\boldsymbol\kappa|\lt\kappa_c\) inscribed in that cube. The ratio of their volumes is \(\tfrac43\pi\kappa_c^3/(2\kappa_c)^3=\pi/6=0.524\): about 48 % of the coefficients that the grid can hold are zero in the filtered field. The grid is still the coarsest uniform grid that represents the filtered field exactly, because the sphere touches each face of the cube on a coordinate axis, and the statement "more grid points provide no further information" carries over; but about half of the coefficients of that grid carry no information. A grid with spacing \(h=\Delta\) in each direction therefore carries a range of wavenumbers between \(\kappa_c\) and \(\sqrt3\kappa_c\) along the diagonals of the cube that the isotropic filter removes. Whether a simulation keeps those coefficients zero, or lets the equations populate them, is a property of the numerical method, not of the filter.

The filtered rate of strain and Lilly's estimate L53 12:11

From the filtered velocity field one can form the filtered velocity gradients \(\partial\overline U_i/\partial x_j\), the filtered rate-of-strain tensor and the characteristic filtered rate of strain:

\[ \overline S_{ij}\equiv\frac12\Bigl(\frac{\partial\overline U_i}{\partial x_j}+\frac{\partial\overline U_j}{\partial x_i}\Bigr),\qquad\bar{\mathcal S}\equiv\bigl(2\overline S_{ij}\overline S_{ij}\bigr)^{1/2}. \tag{19.23} \]

\(\bar{\mathcal S}\) has units of \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\); the factor 2 makes \(\bar{\mathcal S}=|\partial\overline U_1/\partial x_2|\) for a simple shear flow with only that gradient non-zero. (It is not the sphere \(\mathcal S(\kappa)\) of chapter 13, which has an argument and no bar.) The speaker motivates (19.23) by the next chapter: the residual stress will be modelled, as the Reynolds stress was by the turbulent-viscosity hypothesis of chapter 9, in proportion to the filtered rate of strain, and \(\bar{\mathcal S}\) sets the size of the model's viscosity. Both quantities are prominent, the slide notes, in the subgrid-scale models described later.

The mean-square filtered strain rate. The slide considers high-Reynolds-number isotropic turbulence with zero mean velocity, so that \(\overline{\mathbf U}=\bar{\mathbf u}\), and states

\[ \langle\bar{\mathcal S}^2\rangle=\langle2\overline S_{ij}\overline S_{ij}\rangle=2\int_0^\infty\kappa^2\,\overline E(\kappa)\,d\kappa=2\int_0^\infty\kappa^2\hat G(\kappa)^2E(\kappa)\,d\kappa. \tag{19.24} \]

The slide calls the second equality "the standard relationship between the mean-square rate of strain of an isotropic velocity field and its spectrum"; it needs less than isotropy. Expanding the square, \(2\overline S_{ij}\overline S_{ij}=\partial_j\bar u_i\,\partial_j\bar u_i+\partial_j\bar u_i\,\partial_i\bar u_j\). The mean of the second term is \(\partial_j\langle\bar u_i\partial_i\bar u_j\rangle-\langle\bar u_i\,\partial_i\partial_j\bar u_j\rangle\); the first part vanishes because the mean of a homogeneous field does not depend on position, and the second because the filtered field is divergence-free (section "Filtering is a convolution with a normalized kernel"). The mean of the first term is \(\int\kappa^2\overline\Phi_{ii}\,d\boldsymbol\kappa=2\int_0^\infty\kappa^2\overline E\,d\kappa\), which is chapter 11's (11.4) with (11.3) applied to the filtered field. So (19.24) needs homogeneity, incompressibility and a homogeneous filter; isotropy enters only through (19.19), which needs an isotropic filter. The last equality is (19.19).

Without a filter (\(\hat G=1\)), (19.24) gives \(\langle\mathcal S^2\rangle=2\int\kappa^2E\,d\kappa=\varepsilon/\nu\), since \(\varepsilon=2\nu\int\kappa^2E\,d\kappa\) (11.3). The speaker first writes the unfiltered value as "dissipation divided by viscosity" and then as \(\varepsilon/2\nu\); the second form is \(\int\kappa^2E\,d\kappa\), without the factor 2 that the definition of \(\bar{\mathcal S}\) contains. For the jet, \(\varepsilon/\nu=5.48\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}\), a root-mean-square strain rate of \(2340\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}=1/\tau_\eta\), with \(\tau_\eta=(\nu/\varepsilon)^{1/2}\) the Kolmogorov time.

Where the filtered strain rate comes from. The factor \(\kappa^2\) weights the integral (19.24) toward high wavenumbers, whereas the factor \(\hat G^2\) attenuates it beyond the cutoff wavenumber \(\kappa_c\). Consequently, the slide states, the contributions to \(\langle\bar{\mathcal S}^2\rangle\) are predominantly from wavenumbers around \(\kappa_c\). If \(\kappa_c\) is in the inertial subrange, the integral can be well approximated by substituting the Kolmogorov spectrum for \(E(\kappa)\). With \(x=\kappa\Delta\), \(\kappa^2\cdot\kappa^{-5/3}=\kappa^{1/3}=x^{1/3}\Delta^{-1/3}\) and \(d\kappa=dx/\Delta\), which gives the estimate

\[ \langle\bar{\mathcal S}^2\rangle\approx2\int_0^\infty\kappa^2\hat G(\kappa)^2C\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3}\,d\kappa=a_fC\varepsilon^{2/3}\Delta^{-4/3},\qquad a_f\equiv2\int_0^\infty(\kappa\Delta)^{1/3}\hat G(\kappa)^2\,\Delta\,d\kappa. \tag{19.25} \]

The constant \(a_f\) depends on the filter type but not on \(\Delta\), because the integral is written in the variable \(\kappa\Delta\). The slide attributes this type of analysis to Lilly (1967), whose paper was not available; the speaker calls it the beginning of the development of subgrid-scale models.

The slide does not give values of \(a_f\). For the three common filters they are

\[ a_f=2\int_0^\pi x^{1/3}dx=\tfrac32\pi^{4/3}=6.902\ \text{(sharp)},\qquad a_f=12^{2/3}\,\Gamma(\tfrac23)=7.098\ \text{(Gaussian)},\qquad a_f=8.037\ \text{(box)}. \tag{19.26} \]

The Gaussian value follows by the substitution \(y=x^2/12\); the program confirms it by quadrature (7.098). The box value is \(8\int_0^\infty x^{-5/3}\sin^2(x/2)\,dx=4|\Gamma(-\tfrac23)|\cos(\pi/3)\), from \(\sin^2(x/2)=\tfrac12(1-\cos x)\) and the standard integral \(\int_0^\infty(1-\cos x)x^{-1-\mu}dx=|\Gamma(-\mu)|\cos(\pi\mu/2)\) for \(0\lt\mu\lt2\). The three values differ by 16 % from the smallest to the largest, so the filter type matters to the coefficient of any model built on (19.25). With \(\hat G=1\) the integral in \(a_f\) would diverge at large \(x\): without a filter the inertial-range approximation fails, and the dissipation range sets the strain rate, as the previous paragraph found.

How good the approximation is. For the jet with the model spectrum (19.13) and the sharp filter at the 80 % width \(\Delta=0.0977L\), the program gives \(\langle\bar{\mathcal S}^2\rangle(k/\varepsilon)^2=205.9\) from the full spectrum and 230.0 from (19.25) (the last digit depends on the quadrature grid; the ratio, 1.117, does not): the estimate is 12 % high, because the model spectrum lies below the Kolmogorov form near the energy-containing peak and the estimate extends the \(\kappa^{-5/3}\) form to \(\kappa\to0\). In SI units the estimate gives \(\langle\bar{\mathcal S}^2\rangle=6.902\times1.5\times80^{2/3}\times(0.01795)^{-4/3}=4.09\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}\), a root-mean-square filtered strain rate of \(202\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), 0.75 % of \(\varepsilon/\nu\) in the mean square. The filter removes 99 % of the mean-square strain rate while keeping 80 % of the energy, the quantitative form of the statement that energy lives at large scales and strain at small scales (chapter 5). How the next chapter uses (19.25) is its own subject.

Executable check: filters, residuals, the filtered spectrum and the strain-rate coefficient

Part 2 builds a random periodic signal of 4096 points with a \(\kappa^{-5/3}\) spectrum up to wavenumber 600, filters it by fast Fourier transform with \(\kappa_c=32\), and prints the residual, the filtered residual and the difference between the doubly and singly filtered fields. Part 3 solves the model spectrum (19.13) at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=453\) in units \(k=\varepsilon=1\), computes \(L_{11}\) and \(E_{11}\) from (13.14) and (13.17), the resolved fractions, the 80 % widths by bisection, the limit of \(L_{11}/L\) at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=10^{4}\) and \(10^{5}\), and the inertial-range estimates of (19.21). Inputs are set in the code; there are no files. What the filtering analysis establishes and what it does not

The exact results of the chapter are mathematical properties of a homogeneous filter: the convolution theorem (19.9), the action on a mode (19.10), the filtered coefficients (19.16), the exact representation of a sharply filtered field by \(N=\kappa_c\mathcal L/\pi\) modes (19.17)–(19.18), the filtered spectra (19.12) and (19.19) for a homogeneous field, the strain-rate relation (19.24), and the statement that only a filter with \(\hat G\in\{0,1\}\) has a zero filtered residual. Each holds under the stated assumptions: a filter independent of position, and statistical homogeneity for the spectral statements. A filter whose width varies in space, as on a stretched grid near a wall, does not commute with differentiation, and the filtered equations then contain additional commutation terms; the course's statements assume they are absent.

The numerical rules are estimates. The widths \(1.2\ell_{EI}\) and \(0.8\ell_{EI}\) assume a Kolmogorov spectrum from the cutoff to infinite wavenumber, \(C=1.5\) and \(L_{11}=0.43L\); the model spectrum at \(\mathrm{Re}_\lambda=453\) gives 1.29 and 0.93. The coefficient \(a_f\) and the estimate (19.25) assume an inertial subrange around \(\kappa_c\); for the jet they overestimate the model's filtered strain rate by 12 %. The 80 % target itself is a convention: Pope (2004) defines the fraction of unresolved energy \(M\) and gives \(M=0.2\), "the resolution of 80 % of the kinetic energy", as an example of a tolerance (p. 8), and he presents the existence of an inertial-range plateau in LES predictions, for filter widths between \(\ell_{EI}\) and \(\ell_{DI}\), as "a hypothesis, in need of testing" (p. 12).

Three limits of scope. First, the analysis is for homogeneous turbulence. In a wall-bounded flow the energy-containing motions near the wall scale with the viscous length, and a filter width fixed by the outer flow does not resolve them; the module returns to near-wall resolution in a later lecture. Second, the filtered field is defined from the true velocity, but an LES computes a model of it whose relationship to the turbulent flow is statistical (Pope 2004, pp. 9–10). Third, the chapter defines the filter and derives its consequences; it does not derive the filtered equations or any model for the residual stress. Those are steps 2 and 3, the subject of the chapter "Filtered equations, the Smagorinsky model, and LES in wavenumber space".

Exercises

Exercise 1 (derive). (a) For a real, even kernel with \(\int G\,dr=1\) and finite second moment \(\sigma^2=\int r^2G\,dr\), show that \(\hat G(\kappa)=1-\tfrac12\sigma^2\kappa^2+O(\kappa^4)\) when the fourth moment is finite. (b) Evaluate the box and Gaussian transfer functions at \(\kappa\Delta=1\) and at \(\kappa\Delta=\pi\), and explain why they agree closely at the first and less at the second.

Solution

(a) By (19.8) and evenness, \(\hat G(\kappa)=\int G(r)\cos(\kappa r)\,dr\). With \(\cos s=1-\tfrac12s^2+\tfrac1{24}s^4\theta\), \(|\theta|\le1\), \(\hat G=\int G\,dr-\tfrac12\kappa^2\int r^2G\,dr+O(\kappa^4\int r^4|G|\,dr)=1-\tfrac12\sigma^2\kappa^2+O(\kappa^4)\).

(b) Both have \(\sigma^2=\Delta^2/12\), so both are \(1-\kappa^2\Delta^2/24+\dots\). At \(\kappa\Delta=1\): box \(\sin(0.5)/0.5=0.95885\), Gaussian \(e^{-1/24}=0.95919\), a difference of \(3.4\times10^{-4}\). At \(\kappa\Delta=\pi\): box \(2/\pi=0.6366\), Gaussian 0.6628, a difference of 0.026. The common second moment fixes the \(\kappa^2\) term; the fourth moments differ (box \(\Delta^4/80\), Gaussian \(3\sigma^4=\Delta^4/48\)), and the \(\kappa^4\) term matters as \(\kappa\Delta\) approaches \(\pi\). Matching by second moment makes two filters equivalent for the largest scales, not near the cutoff.

Exercise 2 (counterexample). A student states: "a low-pass filter attenuates every Fourier mode, and more strongly the shorter the wave". Use the box filter to show that both parts are false, and say which filter of Table 19.1 satisfies both.

Solution

By (19.10) a mode \(\cos(\kappa x)\) is multiplied by \(\hat G=\sin(\tfrac12\kappa\Delta)/(\tfrac12\kappa\Delta)\). At \(\kappa\Delta=2\pi\) (wavelength \(\Delta\)) \(\hat G=0\): the mode is removed entirely. At \(\kappa\Delta=3\pi\) (wavelength \(\tfrac23\Delta\)) \(\hat G=\sin(1.5\pi)/(1.5\pi)=-0.212\): the shorter wave passes with 21 % of its amplitude and reversed sign, so the attenuation is not monotonic in the wavelength, and "attenuates" must include a sign change. Physically, a box of width \(\Delta\) holding \(1\tfrac12\) wavelengths averages one uncompensated half wave, whose sign is opposite to the value at the centre. The Gaussian satisfies both parts: \(\exp(-\kappa^2\Delta^2/24)\) is positive and decreases monotonically in \(|\kappa|\). The Cauchy and Pao filters also do. The sharp spectral filter does not attenuate modes below \(\kappa_c\) at all.

Exercise 3 (check an assumption). (a) Show that filtering twice with a Gaussian filter of width \(\Delta\) is the same as filtering once with a Gaussian filter of width \(\sqrt2\Delta\). (b) For which filters is \(\overline{\overline U}=\overline U\) for every field? (c) A colleague argues that the filtered residual must vanish "because the residual contains only small scales and the filter removes small scales". Identify the assumption and test it for the Gaussian filter at \(\kappa\Delta=\pi\).

Solution

(a) By (19.9) applied twice, the doubly filtered field has transfer function \(\hat G^2=\exp(-2\kappa^2\Delta^2/24)=\exp(-\kappa^2(\sqrt2\Delta)^2/24)\), the Gaussian transfer function of width \(\sqrt2\Delta\). Equivalently, variances add under convolution: \(2\times\Delta^2/12=(\sqrt2\Delta)^2/12\).

(b) \(\overline{\overline U}=\overline U\) for every field requires \(\hat G^2=\hat G\), so \(\hat G(\kappa)\in\{0,1\}\) at every \(\kappa\): a filter that keeps some modes unchanged and removes the others. Of Table 19.1 only the sharp spectral filter qualifies.

(c) The assumption is that the residual contains only scales the filter removes completely. For the Gaussian the residual's transform is \((1-\hat G)\hat U\), which is non-zero at every \(\kappa\ne0\), including large scales; and the filter passes part of every scale. At \(\kappa\Delta=\pi\), \(\hat G=0.663\), so a mode of the residual there has amplitude \(0.337\) of the original, and its filtered value \(\hat G(1-\hat G)=0.223\) of the original: not zero. The argument holds only for the sharp spectral filter, for which the residual is exactly the modes above \(\kappa_c\) and the filter removes them exactly.

Exercise 4 (compute). For the jet station with the model spectrum of the chapter, design two LES grids for a 1 m periodic box that resolve 80 % of the kinetic energy: (a) the sharp spectral filter in a spectral code with \(h=\Delta\); (b) a Gaussian filter in a sixth-order finite-difference code with \(h/\Delta=\tfrac12\) (Pope 2004, p. 11). Compare the point counts with each other and with DNS.

Solution

(a) The program gives \(\Delta=1.291\ell_{EI}=17.95\ \mathrm{mm}\), with \(\ell_{EI}=13.90\ \mathrm{mm}\). With \(h=\Delta\): \(N=1/0.01795=55.7\), rounded up to the even integer 56, so \(56^3=1.76\times10^{5}\) points.

(b) The Gaussian needs \(\Delta=0.932\ell_{EI}=12.95\ \mathrm{mm}\), and \(h=\Delta/2=6.48\ \mathrm{mm}\): \(N=154.4\), rounded up to 156 for an even count, so \(156^3=3.80\times10^{6}\) points, 22 times (a). The Gaussian costs more for two reasons: its smaller width for the same resolved energy (factor \((1.291/0.932)^3=2.7\)) and the finer grid relative to the width (factor 8).

DNS with \(h=2.09\eta\) needs \(6044^3=2.2\times10^{11}\) points, \(1.3\times10^{6}\) times (a) and \(5.8\times10^{4}\) times (b). The comparison is of grid points only; the time step and the cost per point of the subgrid model change the work ratio.

Exercise 5 (derive and compute). (a) Show that for a homogeneous incompressible velocity field with zero mean, \(\langle2s_{ij}s_{ij}\rangle=\varepsilon/\nu\), where \(s_{ij}\) is the fluctuating rate of strain, and identify where incompressibility is used. (b) For the jet, compute the root-mean-square value of \(\mathcal S\) and, from (19.25), of \(\bar{\mathcal S}\) for the sharp filter with \(\Delta=17.95\ \mathrm{mm}\). (c) The speaker's first statement was that \(\langle\bar{\mathcal S}^2\rangle\) "is related to the dissipation rate divided by kinematic viscosity". In what sense is this true for the filtered field?

Solution

(a) Chapter 5 defines \(\varepsilon=2\nu\langle s_{ij}s_{ij}\rangle\) (5.13), so \(\langle2s_{ij}s_{ij}\rangle=\varepsilon/\nu\) by definition. The spectral form used in (19.24) needs more (chapter 6 takes the same step after (6.27)): \(2s_{ij}s_{ij}=\partial_ju_i\partial_ju_i+\partial_ju_i\partial_iu_j\), and the mean of the second term is \(\partial_j\langle u_i\partial_iu_j\rangle-\langle u_i\partial_i(\partial_ju_j)\rangle\), zero by homogeneity (first part) and incompressibility (second part). Then \(\varepsilon=\nu\langle\partial_ju_i\partial_ju_i\rangle\) (11.4) \(=2\nu\int\kappa^2E\,d\kappa\) (11.3).

(b) \(\varepsilon/\nu=80/1.4607\times10^{-5}=5.48\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}\), root-mean-square \(2340\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\). From (19.25) with \(a_f=6.902\), \(C=1.5\), \(\varepsilon^{2/3}=18.57\ \mathrm{m^{4/3}s^{-2}}\), \(\Delta^{-4/3}=(0.01795)^{-4/3}=212.8\ \mathrm{m^{-4/3}}\): \(\langle\bar{\mathcal S}^2\rangle=4.09\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}\), root-mean-square \(202\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), 8.6 % of the unfiltered root-mean-square value.

(c) It is true without a filter. With a filter in the inertial subrange, \(\langle\bar{\mathcal S}^2\rangle\) depends on \(\varepsilon\) and \(\Delta\), through \(\varepsilon^{2/3}\Delta^{-4/3}\), and not on \(\nu\): the viscosity does not appear in (19.25). This independence of \(\nu\) is what lets a model built on \(\bar{\mathcal S}\) and \(\Delta\) represent a dissipation rate that the resolved field cannot produce by molecular viscosity.

Exercise 6 (judge a design). A large-eddy simulation of the jet's shear layer uses a uniform anisotropic grid with \(h_1=20\ \mathrm{mm}\) along the flow, \(h_2=1\ \mathrm{mm}\) across the layer and \(h_3=10\ \mathrm{mm}\) spanwise, with \(\Delta_i=h_i\). The designer argues: "by (19.22) \(\Delta\) is below \(\ell_{EI}=13.9\ \mathrm{mm}\), so the energy-containing motions are resolved." Evaluate the argument.

Solution

\(\Delta=(20\times1\times10)^{1/3}=5.85\ \mathrm{mm}\), below \(\ell_{EI}\). But (19.22) is a convention for assigning one length to a model, not a resolution criterion. Along \(x_1\) the spacing is 20 mm, larger than \(\ell_{EI}\) and larger than the 80 % width of the sharp filter (17.95 mm): a mode along \(x_1\) with wavelength between \(2\Delta=36\ \mathrm{mm}\), the shortest wave the 80 % sharp filter keeps, and \(2h_1=40\ \mathrm{mm}\), the shortest wave the grid can carry at two points per wavelength, belongs to the resolved field and yet has no representation on the grid. The fine spacing across the layer does not compensate: points added in \(x_2\) do not resolve modes along \(x_1\). The judgement: the grid resolves fewer of the energy-containing motions along the flow than the single width suggests; the resolution criterion must be checked direction by direction, with the largest spacing.

Exercise 7 (course practice problem, adapted; Problem 10 of L60 0:25). Let \(U(x)\) have the Fourier transform \(\hat U(\kappa)\), so that the filtered velocity \(\overline U(x)\) has the Fourier transform \(\hat{\overline U}(\kappa)=\hat G(\kappa)\hat U(\kappa)\). Show that the Fourier transform of (1) the residual \(u'(x)\) is \([1-\hat G(\kappa)]\hat U(\kappa)\); (2) the filtered residual \(\overline{u'}(x)\) is \(\hat G(\kappa)[1-\hat G(\kappa)]\hat U(\kappa)\); (3) the doubly filtered field \(\overline{\overline U}(x)\) is \(\hat G(\kappa)^2\hat U(\kappa)\). Changes to the statement: the definition \(u'=U-\overline U\) of (19.1), which the slide does not print, is written in; the problem's capital \(U\) with lower-case residual \(u'\) is kept. Add part (4): use (2) to state for which filters the filtered residual vanishes.

Solution

(1) The transform (19.7) is linear, so \(\mathscr F\{u'\}=\mathscr F\{U\}-\mathscr F\{\overline U\}=\hat U-\hat G\hat U=[1-\hat G]\hat U\).

(2) \(\overline{u'}\) is the filter applied to \(u'\), so by the convolution theorem (19.9) its transform is \(\hat G\) times the transform of \(u'\): \(\hat G[1-\hat G]\hat U\).

(3) \(\overline{\overline U}\) is the filter applied to \(\overline U\), whose transform is \(\hat G\hat U\); applying (19.9) again gives \(\hat G\cdot\hat G\hat U=\hat G^2\hat U\). Consistency check: (2) equals \(\hat G\hat U-\hat G^2\hat U\), the transform of \(\overline U-\overline{\overline U}\), which is (19.4).

(4) \(\overline{u'}=0\) for every \(U\) if and only if \(\hat G(1-\hat G)=0\) at every \(\kappa\), that is \(\hat G\in\{0,1\}\): the sharp spectral filter. For the Gaussian, \(\hat G(1-\hat G)\) has its maximum \(\tfrac14\) where \(\hat G=\tfrac12\), at \(\kappa\Delta=(24\ln2)^{1/2}=4.08\), just above \(\kappa_c\Delta=\pi\).

Exercise 8 (course practice problem, adapted; Problem 11 of L60 7:55). Consider high-Reynolds-number homogeneous isotropic turbulence and an isotropic filter of width \(\Delta\) and characteristic wavenumber \(\kappa_c=\pi/\Delta\). The problem defines the kinetic energy of the residual motions as \(\langle k_r\rangle=\int_0^\infty[1-\hat G(\kappa)^2]E(\kappa)\,d\kappa\) (its eq. 16; the general definition of \(k_r\) belongs to the chapter "Filtered equations, the Smagorinsky model, and LES in wavenumber space"). (1) For the sharp spectral filter with cutoff \(\kappa_c\) in the inertial subrange, use the Kolmogorov spectrum to show that \(\langle k_r\rangle/k=\tfrac32C(\kappa_cL)^{-2/3}\), with \(L\equiv k^{3/2}/\varepsilon\). (2) Show that 80 % of the energy is resolved (\(\langle k_r\rangle/k=0.2\)) if \(\kappa_cL=(\tfrac{15}2C)^{3/2}=38\). (3) Using \(\ell_{EI}=\tfrac16L_{11}\) and \(L_{11}=0.43L\), show that \(\Delta/\ell_{EI}=6\pi/(0.43\kappa_cL)\approx1.16\). Changes to the statement: the data \(C=1.5\) and \(\hat G=H(\kappa_c-|\kappa|)\), which the lecturer supplies in speech, are written into it; part (4) is added: compare \(\langle k_r\rangle\) with the energy of the residual field, \(\tfrac12\langle u'_iu'_i\rangle\), for the sharp and the Gaussian filters.

Solution

(1) For the sharp filter \(1-\hat G^2=0\) for \(\kappa\lt\kappa_c\) and 1 for \(\kappa\gt\kappa_c\). With \(E=C\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa^{-5/3}\) for all \(\kappa\gt\kappa_c\), \(\langle k_r\rangle=C\varepsilon^{2/3}\int_{\kappa_c}^\infty\kappa^{-5/3}d\kappa=\tfrac32C\varepsilon^{2/3}\kappa_c^{-2/3}\). Since \(L^{2/3}=k/\varepsilon^{2/3}\), dividing by \(k\) gives \(\tfrac32C(\kappa_cL)^{-2/3}\). The step extends the Kolmogorov form to infinite \(\kappa\), ignoring the dissipation range; the model spectrum of the chapter has 17.8 % of its energy above \(\kappa_cL=37.7\), not 20 %, so the extension overestimates the residual energy by about 12 % of itself at the jet's Reynolds number.

(2) \(0.2=\tfrac32C(\kappa_cL)^{-2/3}\) gives \((\kappa_cL)^{2/3}=7.5C\), \(\kappa_cL=(7.5C)^{3/2}=(11.25)^{3/2}=37.73\), which rounds to 38.

(3) \(\Delta/\ell_{EI}=(\pi/\kappa_c)/(L_{11}/6)=6\pi/(0.43\,\kappa_cL)\). With the unrounded 37.73 this is 1.162; with the rounded 38 it is 1.154. The printed 1.16 follows from the unrounded value. For the jet with \(L=183.7\ \mathrm{mm}\): \(\kappa_c=205\ \mathrm{rad/m}\) and \(\Delta=15.3\ \mathrm{mm}\).

(4) The residual field \(u'=U-\overline U\) has transform \((1-\hat G)\hat U\) (Exercise 7), so its energy is \(\int(1-\hat G)^2E\,d\kappa\), while \(\langle k_r\rangle=\int(1-\hat G^2)E\,d\kappa\). The difference is \(\int[(1-\hat G^2)-(1-\hat G)^2]E\,d\kappa=2\int\hat G(1-\hat G)E\,d\kappa\), zero if and only if \(\hat G\in\{0,1\}\). For the sharp filter the two agree. For the Gaussian with \(\Delta=\ell_{EI}\) on the model spectrum, the program gives \(\langle k_r\rangle/k=0.210\) and \(\tfrac12\langle u'_iu'_i\rangle/k=0.118\): the energy missing from the filtered field is nearly twice the energy of the residual field. The difference, \(2\int\hat G(1-\hat G)E\,d\kappa=0.092k\), is the correlation \(\langle\bar u_iu'_i\rangle\), because \(k=\tfrac12\langle\bar u_i\bar u_i\rangle+\langle\bar u_iu'_i\rangle+\tfrac12\langle u'_iu'_i\rangle\): for a filter that is not a projection, the filtered and residual fields are correlated, and the kinetic energy does not split into a resolved and a residual part. "Residual kinetic energy" must therefore always be stated with its definition.
